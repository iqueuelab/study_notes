Economic Growth versus Economic Development

1. Growth and development

  • Economic growth: increase in real output/income over time; mainly quantitative. Growth rate = [(real GDP this year − real GDP last year) / real GDP last year] × 100. Use comparable constant-price data.
  • Economic development: broader improvement in living standards, capabilities and opportunities, alongside economic/structural change; includes poverty, jobs, distribution, health, education and gender equity.
  • Growth indicators: real GDP/GNP growth, real per-capita income. Development indicators: HDI/IHDI, gender indices, poverty and wellbeing measures.
  • Growth increases resources; jobs, fair opportunities and effective public services help translate them into development. Higher income alone does not ensure shared benefits.
  • Corrections: growth can be long-term; development can be quantified through indicators, though no single number captures it fully. Both concepts matter in developed and developing countries.
  • Human development enlarges choices, capabilities and freedoms. Four pillars associated with Mahbub ul Haq: equity, sustainability, productivity and empowerment. Amartya Sen's capability approach emphasises people's real freedom to live lives they value.

2. Drivers, obstacles and remedies

  • Resources/production: natural resources, capital formation, agricultural surplus/productivity, structural transformation and an efficient financial system.
  • People/institutions: human capital; education, skills and technology; health; effective law/order; ethical/social values and good governance. These overlap with economic factors—the book's two-way grouping is not a rigid separation.
  • Population pressure relative to resources → education, health, productive employment and infrastructure; population can become a demographic dividend when skills/jobs exist.
  • Agriculture dependence/disguised unemployment → modern technology, higher productivity, diversification and non-farm jobs.
  • Low income/inequality/unemployment/skill mismatch → labour-intensive employment, inclusion, quality education and skills; implement NEP effectively.
  • Infrastructure/finance gaps → investment, wider banking access and resolution of non-performing assets (NPAs).
  • Corruption/misdirected priorities → transparency, accountability and accessible digital processes.
  • Poor health/resource depletion → better health spending and welfare delivery; sustainable resource use.
  • Underdevelopment: low per-capita income, capital formation and labour productivity; widespread poverty/inequality; unemployment/underemployment; weak human capital and high dependence on primary-sector employment. It is multidimensional, not an income label alone.

3. Inclusive growth

  • Meaning: broad participation in growth and fair access to its benefits, across income, gender, caste, religion and region. Inclusion requires opportunities and capabilities, not only redistribution after growth.
  • Vertical inequality: between people at different income levels. Horizontal inequality: between groups, e.g. gender/social groups. Also address regional disparities.
  • Goals: poverty reduction, productive jobs, opportunities across all sectors, smaller gaps between advantaged/disadvantaged groups. “Balanced sectors” does not mean identical sectoral growth rates.
  • India's obstacles: poverty, weak job creation, skills mismatch, agricultural productivity gaps, social barriers, regional disparities, inequality and governance weaknesses.
  • Global MPI 2025: India's latest survey used is 2019–21; incidence 16.4%, approximately 235.74 million poor using 2023 population. A 2025 report date is not a 2025 survey. UNDP country profile
  • 415 million exited multidimensional poverty during 2005–06 to 2019–21 in the global MPI comparison. Do not mix this with national-MPI estimates or shorter periods. UNDP
  • Inclusive Development Index, WEF (2018): growth/development; inclusion; intergenerational equity/sustainability. Covered 103 economies in two groups; India 62nd of 74 emerging economies. Historical rank, not a current league table. WEF

4. Composite Development Index of States

  • Raghuram Rajan Committee, 2013: proposed a composite (under)development index for States and a funds-allocation framework considering need and performance. Committee release
  • Ten equally weighted components: monthly per-capita consumption expenditure; education; health; household amenities; poverty; female literacy; SC/ST population share; urbanisation; financial inclusion; connectivity.
  • This is distinct from UNDP's HDI and WEF's Inclusive Development Index. A deprivation/underdevelopment score should not be interpreted like an achievement score.

5. HDI: health + education + income

  • Publisher: UNDP, within the Human Development Report, first issued 1990. HDI summarises average achievement; the report also includes IHDI, GDI, GII and MPI.
  • Health: life expectancy at birth. Education: expected years of schooling for a school-entry-age child and mean years of schooling for adults 25+. Living standard: GNI per capita in PPP dollars, not GDP per capita.
  • HDI = (health index × education index × income index)^(1/3): geometric mean of three normalised dimension indices. Values range from 0 to 1; higher is better.
  • Categories: very high ≥0.800; high 0.700–0.799; medium 0.550–0.699; low <0.550.
  • Equal GNI per capita can yield different human-development outcomes due to policy, distribution and public services. HDI is broader than income, but still hides inequalities and does not capture every freedom or environmental cost.

6. HDI, IHDI and planetary pressures

  • HDI: average achievements. IHDI: discounts the same health/education/income dimensions for inequality in their distribution; introduced 2010. Inequality is not a new fourth HDI dimension.
  • IHDI ≤ HDI; equality gives IHDI = HDI. Loss due to inequality = (1 − IHDI/HDI) × 100.
  • HDR 2025, mainly 2023 data: India HDI 0.685, rank 130/193, medium category; IHDI 0.475, loss 30.7%. The book incorrectly calls 0.475 “HDI”.
  • India indicators: life expectancy 72.0 years; mean schooling 6.88 years; expected schooling 12.95 years; GNI/person $9,046.76, 2021 PPP. UNDP India
  • Consistent 2025-report series: 2022 HDI 0.676 → 0.685 in 2023. The older HDR2023/24 publication reported 0.644 for 2022; revisions mean these editions should not be treated as a directly comparable unrevised series. Expected schooling was 12.96 → 12.95, so not every indicator increased.
  • Official HDR2025 dimension-inequality figures: health 15.5%, education 36.9%, income 37.4%; these replace the book's mixed-vintage values. HDR tables 1–3
  • HDR2025 ranks: Iceland 1 (0.972); Norway/Switzerland 2 (0.970); Denmark 4 (0.962); South Sudan 193 (0.388). Neighbours: China 78, Sri Lanka 89, Bhutan 125, India/Bangladesh 130, Nepal 145, Pakistan 168, Afghanistan 181. Corrected Bhutan/China ranks replace the book's 127/75. HDR
  • Report title, 2025: “A Matter of Choice: People and Possibilities in the Age of AI”.
  • PHDI, introduced 2020: adjusts HDI for per-capita CO₂ emissions and material footprint; achievements with less planetary pressure are favoured. It complements rather than replaces HDI.

7. GII and GDI: do not interchange

  • GII—Gender Inequality Index (UNDP; 2010): disadvantage in reproductive health, empowerment and labour-market participation. 0 = no measured inequality; 1 = maximum; lower is better.
  • Health indicators: maternal mortality and adolescent births. Empowerment: parliamentary representation and secondary education. Labour: female/male labour-force participation.
  • HDR2025: India GII 0.403, rank 102/172; world 0.455, South Asia 0.458. Adolescent births 14.1 per 1,000 women aged 15–19; LFPR, ages 15+: women 35.1%, men 76.4%, gap 41.3 percentage points. These replace the older 0.419/15.7/28.9% figures mixed into the book. Reference years vary by component. HDR table 5
  • GDI—Gender Development Index (UNDP): GDI = female HDI / male HDI. Same three HDI dimensions; education includes both mean and expected schooling. A value closer to 1 means greater parity; GDI can exceed 1.
  • HDR2025, 2023 data: GDI 0.874; female HDI 0.631, male 0.722. Female/male life expectancy 73.6/70.5 years; mean schooling 5.8/8.0; expected schooling 13.0/12.9; GNI/person $4,543/$13,273 (2021 PPP). The book's 0.865 and 0.616/0.712 use older figures; they are not current HDR2025 values. HDR table 4

8. Global Gender Gap Index

  • Publisher: World Economic Forum; first introduced 2006. Measures gender parity, not simply absolute development levels.
  • Four subindices: economic participation/opportunity; educational attainment; health/survival; political empowerment.
  • 0–1: higher score means more of the gap closed; 1 is the parity benchmark. This direction is opposite to GII.
  • 2025: India 131/148, score 0.644; Iceland 0.926, followed by Finland, Norway and the UK. World score 68.8%; the matched-country comparison improved by about 0.3 percentage points. WEF2025
  • 2026 update: India retains rank 131, score 0.645 (64.5% parity). Keep this separate from the 2025 edition. WEF2026

9. World Happiness Report

  • First published 2012. Published by Oxford's Wellbeing Research Centre, in partnership with Gallup, the UN Sustainable Development Solutions Network and an independent editorial board; attributing publication solely to SDSN is incomplete. About the report
  • Ranking: average self-reported life evaluations on the Cantril ladder, 0–10, using Gallup World Poll data, normally averaged over three years. It is not a weighted index of six inputs.
  • Six factors used to explain differences: GDP/person (PPP); social support; healthy life expectancy; freedom to choose; generosity; perceived corruption. Methodology FAQ
  • 2026: India 116/147, score 4.536; book's previous score 4.389 refers to the older edition. Finland first for the ninth consecutive year, then Iceland and Denmark (the book reverses these two). Afghanistan last; Sierra Leone second-last; Lebanon also near the bottom. 2026 rankings
  • Report focus: Happiness and Social Media (2026); Caring and Sharing (2025). India's social support and freedom are useful policy lenses, but avoid assigning a cause to a rank without evidence. 2026 report
  • International Day of Happiness: 20 March. A report's theme should not automatically be labelled the UN observance's official theme.

10. Gross National Happiness

  • Bhutan: associated with fourth King Jigme Singye Wangchuck, 1970s; holistic progress gives importance to non-economic wellbeing.
  • Four pillars: sustainable/equitable socio-economic development; good governance; cultural preservation; environmental conservation.
  • GNH Index: 33 indicators, nine domains—psychological wellbeing, health, education, time use, cultural diversity/resilience, good governance, community vitality, ecological diversity/resilience and living standards. Bhutan consulate
  • GNH ≠ World Happiness Report: a multidimensional Bhutanese framework versus international self-reported life-evaluation rankings.
  • Delhi Happiness Curriculum, 2018: mindfulness/attention, critical thinking/reflection, social-emotional skills and confident, responsible personalities. SCERT
  • Chapter's Indian example: Madhya Pradesh's Happiness Department/Anand Sansthan, 2016. The book also names Andhra Pradesh as a subsequent adopter; treat the “second State” chronology as its claim, not an independently established ranking.

11. Sustainability and country classification

  • Sustainable development: improve present wellbeing without undermining future generations' ability to meet needs. Inclusion concerns who benefits now; sustainability also concerns future people and environmental limits.
  • Sustainable Development Report: SDSN's SDG Index/Dashboards assess progress towards the 17 SDGs; separate from HDI and from the UN's own SDG progress reporting. 2026 India: rank 94, score 68.33/100. SDSN report, rankings
  • World Bank: classifies economies by GNI/person, Atlas method; this is not PPP and not a sector-based classification. Thresholds are updated annually on 1 July.
  • FY2027 (July 2026–June 2027), 2025 GNI: low ≤$1,175; lower-middle $1,176–4,635; upper-middle $4,636–14,375; high >$14,375. India lower-middle; China upper-middle; USA high. The book's $1,005/$3,995/$12,235 thresholds are outdated and its ranges contain inconsistencies. World Bank
  • Developed/developing: broad structural labels involving industrialisation, technology, institutions and living standards; not exact synonyms for World Bank income classes. A developing economy need not be dominated by agriculture.
  • Least Developed Countries (UN category): severe structural constraints, low income, weak human assets and vulnerability to economic/environmental shocks. Income, human assets and vulnerability all matter; Somalia and Sudan are examples cited in the chapter.

12. Prelims PYQs: recall

  • 2018 human-capital formation → (c), 2 and 4 only: knowledge, skills and capacities; intangible wealth, not merely tangible assets or financial capital.
  • 2013 growth in country X → (c), capital formation: the supplied key identifies additions to productive capital. In real economies, efficient use and demand also matter; do not turn the question into an unconditional guarantee.
  • 2012 inclusive governance → (c), 2, 3 and 4 only: effective District Planning Committees, public-health spending and Mid-day Meals. Simply permitting NBFCs to do banking does not by itself establish inclusive governance.

13. Mains PYQs: answer cues

  • 2025 HDI versus IHDI, India: average versus distribution-adjusted achievement → India 0.685/0.475 and 30.7% loss → explain why shared health, education and income achievements better reflect inclusion; IHDI still does not capture every dimension.
  • 2022 inclusive growth in a market economy/financial inclusion: markets create opportunities, but exclusion/market failures need public action. Savings, payments, responsible credit and insurance support enterprise/resilience; access alone is insufficient without affordability, capability and consumer protection.
  • 2019 inclusion and sustainability together: jobs/services and equity today + conservation, resilient infrastructure and intergenerational equity; manage trade-offs through a just transition.
  • 2017 salient features/India/measures: participation, opportunity, jobs, reduced poverty/group/regional gaps → balanced evidence → human capital, agriculture/MSMEs, finance, social protection and governance. This PYQ also relates to National Income.
  • 2016 manpower-related challenges: interpret through skill mismatch, low productivity, unemployment, job quality and demographic pressures; suggest training, health/education, labour-intensive opportunities and better matching, rather than labelling people as inherently unproductive.

14. One-minute recall

  • Growth: more real output. Development: better lives/capabilities. Inclusion: who participates/benefits. Sustainability: whether benefits endure.
  • HDI: health + education + GNI. IHDI: same dimensions, inequality discount. PHDI: planetary-pressure adjustment.
  • GDI: female/male HDI, parity near 1. GII: inequality, lower better. WEF gender gap: parity, higher better.
  • WHR: reported life evaluation; six variables explain it. GNH: Bhutan's 4 pillars/9 domains/33 indicators.
  • Income groups: World Bank, GNI/person, Atlas method; annual thresholds. LDC: separate UN structural category.
  • Rank ≠ score; report year ≠ data year. Check edition, survey period, units and publisher before comparing.
