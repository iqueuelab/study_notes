1. Labour force, workforce and core indicators

  • Employed: doing economic work under the survey's reference-period criteria. Unemployed: without employment and seeking or available for work under the applicable survey definition. Labour force = employed + unemployed; workforce = employed only.
  • People outside the labour force include those neither working nor seeking/available for work, such as some students and persons occupied exclusively in domestic duties. Unpaid work in a family enterprise can count as employment; unpaid domestic services are a different category. Statistical employment is about economic activity, not the occupation's legality or whether cash wages are received.
  • The book's 15-60 working-age boundary is not universal: the main PLFS figures below cover age 15 and above, without an upper limit. Unemployment may be temporary or persistent.
  • Labour supply: hours/person-days offered at a wage; can change without changing the number of workers. Labour force: people participating; workforce: people employed. The assertion that labour force is unrelated to wages is too strong: wages can affect participation.
IndicatorFormulaDenominator trap
Unemployment rate (UR)Unemployed / labour force × 100Not total population

  • Identity: WPR = LFPR × (1 - UR/100). If population = 1,000, labour force = 600 and employed = 570: LFPR 60%; WPR 57%; UR 5%; unemployed/population = 3%.
  • A low UR can coexist with low LFPR, disguised unemployment, low earnings or poor-quality work. Discouraged workers leaving the labour force can lower measured unemployment without improving job access.
  • Causes in India: insufficient productive jobs; skill/job mismatch; regional and social barriers; demographic pressure; weak education/training links; slow structural transformation and uneven policy implementation.

2. Measuring unemployment: reference periods

ApproachReference / ruleBest use and limitation
Usual principal status (UPS / ps)365 days; major-time activity determines principal statusLonger-term attachment; can miss intermittent unemployment
Usual status (ps+ss), also called UPSSPrincipal status plus subsidiary economic work of 30 days or more during preceding 365 daysBroader employment identification than UPS
Current weekly status (CWS)Last 7 days; at least 1 hour of work on any day identifies employmentCaptures shorter-term changes; intermittent workers can still count as employed
Current daily status (CDS), historical EUS methodDaily activity over 7 days, expressed in person-daysCaptures unemployment within a week and underemployment
  • CDS historical rule: 4+ hours of work = full employed day; 1 to under 4 hours = half employed day. The remaining half is unemployed only if seeking/available for work; otherwise outside labour force. No work does not automatically mean unemployment.
  • The Dantwala Committee (1970) recommended the activity-status framework; NSS employment surveys used it from 1972-73. UPS tends to give a lower and CDS a higher estimate because of reference periods and units; this is not an unconditional mathematical ranking for every sample.
  • PLFS began in 2017, replacing earlier large NSS employment-unemployment rounds; Labour Bureau also ran a separate EUS series. Do not treat all earlier EUS reports as one identical programme.
  • First PLFS annual period: July 2017-June 2018; education-based stratification and computer-assisted personal interviewing (CAPI) improved data collection.
  • From January 2025: redesigned PLFS provides monthly all-India rural/urban CWS indicators, quarterly rural and urban CWS estimates, and annual usual-status/CWS estimates. Annual period changed to January-December. Design changes limit strict comparability with earlier estimates. MoSPI PLFS redesign

3. Labour underutilisation and the jobs gap

  • Time-related underemployment: employed people working insufficient hours who want and are available for more work. They remain employed in the headline UR.
  • Potential labour force: non-employed persons who either seek work but are not currently available, or are available but have not recently sought work. Willing non-job seekers: want work but neither actively seek nor are immediately available.
  • ILO jobs gap includes unemployed people and other non-employed people who want employment. It captures unmet need beyond the UR.
  • Wanting a job is broader than fulfilling availability/search conditions for unemployment. National PLFS formulations and strict international classifications should not be assumed identical in every detail.
  • The unemployed component is generally more sensitive to the business cycle; the other components can reflect persistent constraints such as care duties and barriers to participation. The unemployment-inflation relationship is often discussed through the Phillips curve, but is not a fixed causal law for the entire jobs gap.

  • Vulnerable employment, not “vulnerable unemployment”: own-account workers + contributing family workers. They are employed but often face weak security, low earnings and limited formal protection. Not every self-employed person is vulnerable: employers are a separate category.
  • Disguised unemployment reflects low or zero marginal, not necessarily zero average, productivity. Seasonal unemployment concerns missing work over time, rather than surplus workers at the same task.

8. Jobless growth, employment elasticity and Beveridge curve

  • Jobless growth: output expands with little or inadequate employment expansion. Merely having output growth faster than employment growth does not, by itself, prove no jobs were created; productivity gains normally create such a difference.
  • Drivers: labour-saving technology; skill-intensive growth; insufficient labour-intensive manufacturing; weak MSME credit/infrastructure; skill mismatch; disguised employment; poor implementation of labour reforms.
  • Services are heterogeneous: IT can be skill-intensive, while tourism, retail and care can absorb much labour. FDI is not inherently job-destroying; its net effect depends on sector, technology, linkages and investment. The book's assertion that capital shortage forces labour-saving technology is not a general economic rule.
  • Historical elasticity estimates quoted: 0.52 (1972-73 to 1983) → 0.41 (next decade) → 0.29 (1993-94 to 2004-05) → 0.04 (2004-05 to 2011-12); agriculture 0.46 → -0.42 across cited first/last periods. These are dimensionless ratios, not 0.52% etc.; negative elasticity can mean labour exits a growing sector. The book's 1951-2000 GDP/employment-growth comparisons are broad historical claims, not a current forecast.
  • Beveridge / UV curve: X = unemployment rate; Y = vacancy rate; usually slopes downward. Recession: move toward high unemployment, low vacancies; expansion: reverse. Outward shift suggests weaker matching, mismatch or other structural frictions; not merely a cyclical movement.

9. Casualisation and informal employment

  • NSO employment-status groups: self-employed, regular wage/salaried, casual labour. Self-employed includes employers, own-account workers and unpaid helpers in household enterprises.
  • Casualisation: greater reliance on day-to-day or short-duration hiring. Drivers include rural-to-urban migration without suitable skills, construction expansion and employers' flexible hiring practices.
  • Informal sector ≠ informal employment. Enterprise classification depends on ownership/size/registration; employment classification concerns social protection, contract and benefits. Informal jobs exist inside organised/formal enterprises; regular salary does not guarantee formal protection. The book's simple 10-worker cutoff cannot establish whether every worker is formal.
  • Casual workers can be legally eligible for EPF and wage/hour protections when coverage conditions apply. Permanent status does not automatically guarantee actual compliance; not every casual worker receives every benefit.
  • Book's “90% informal / 10% formal” is a broad, undated approximation, not a current measured split. Migration, skills gaps, firm size, compliance costs, contracting and weak enforcement can increase vulnerability; market reforms alone do not demonstrate a single causal explanation.
  • Responses: quality vocational education; apprenticeships; credit and infrastructure for productive self-employment; MSME growth; simpler compliance with effective enforcement; portable social security and migrant-worker support. Self-employment without income/security improvement is not an automatic cure.

11. Labour reform and the four codes

  • Labour is mainly in the Concurrent List: Union and States legislate. Reform seeks productivity, employment and simpler compliance while protecting wages, safety, collective bargaining and social security; the Second National Commission on Labour recommended consolidation.
  • Four codes rationalise 29 central labour laws. Government made them effective from 21 November 2025. Distinguish enactment, commencement and specific operational rules/coverage. Official commencement announcement
CodeLaws consolidatedCore provisions
Code on Wages, 20194Minimum wages, timely payment, bonus and gender non-discrimination; Centre fixes floor wage; minimum wages cannot be below the applicable floor
Industrial Relations Code, 20203Trade unions/negotiating arrangements, standing orders, disputes, strike/lockout notice, retrenchment/closure permission where applicable, reskilling fund
Code on Social Security, 20209EPF/ESI and other social security; enabling schemes for unorganised, gig/platform workers and funding contributions
Occupational Safety, Health and Working Conditions Code, 202013Safety, health, working conditions; 8-hour daily work framework; contract labour; interstate migrants; women's employment with consent and safeguards
  • Correction: book prints Social Security 8 laws and OSH Code 2019. Correct recall: 4 + 3 + 9 + 13 = 29; years 2019 + three 2020 codes. Social Security Code text
  • Gig/platform recognition and funds do not mean every worker immediately receives every contributory benefit. Coverage depends on applicable provisions, notified schemes and implementation.
  • Fixed-term employment, 2018 standing-order rules: extended provision across sectors; written contract for fixed duration; comparable hours, wages, allowances and proportionate statutory benefits; rule barred conversion of existing permanent posts to fixed-term posts. Expiry of agreed term differs from retrenchment before expiry; the book's “no notice” statement is not a general permission to dismiss anyone without process.
  • Trade-off: flexibility and simpler hiring versus repeated short contracts, insecurity, bargaining weakness and evasion. Combine enforceable parity/benefits and skills with predictable hiring rules.

  • EPF: EPFO under Labour Ministry; covered establishments generally 20+ employees. Standard employee contribution 12% of applicable wages; employer contribution generally 12%, split between EPF and EPS for eligible pension members. Book's 3.67% EPF + 8.33% EPS is the standard illustrative employer split, subject to EPS eligibility and ceiling; not 12% of every person's entire gross salary.
  • Book's EPF mandatory wage ceiling ₹15,000 is historical. Government raised it to ₹25,000/month effective 17 September 2026. Existing membership normally continues when wages rise above the ceiling; do not say existing members can simply opt out. Official EPF ceiling revision
  • ESI: ESIC's integrated medical/cash protection for covered workers and dependants against sickness, maternity, employment injury, disablement and death. Book's general insured-wage ceiling ₹21,000/month is distinct from EPF's ceiling; disability-specific exceptions and establishment coverage matter.
  • EDLI = insurance; EPF = savings; EPS/APY/PM-SYM = pensions; ESI = medical and cash protection. Eligibility and actual coverage, not job label alone, determine benefits.

13. AI and the labour force

  • AI may automate tasks, complement workers, raise productivity and create new occupations. Task exposure is not observed job loss; the book's “75 million jobs at complete risk” is a modelled older claim, not a verified tally of jobs eliminated.
  • Risks: displacement of routine IT/service tasks; skill polarisation; wage/income inequality; surveillance and bias; weak regulatory capacity; consumption slowdown if livelihoods suffer. Emerging economies can face adoption and adjustment constraints alongside opportunities.
  • ILO's 2025 analysis emphasises job transformation and augmentation, with outcomes differing by occupation, gender and digital infrastructure. ILO AI research
  • Economic Survey 2023-24 estimated a need for roughly 78.5 lakh additional non-farm jobs annually to 2030; this is a planning requirement, not measured annual creation.
  • Response: foundational education; continuous reskilling; affordable digital access; worker consultation; portable social security; safeguards for bias/privacy; support labour-intensive and care/green sectors; strengthen labour-market institutions. Assess net jobs, earnings and job quality, not productivity alone.

14. PYQs and answer cues

  • Prelims 2022: industrial disputes, closures, retrenchment and layoffs → Labour Bureau (c).
  • Prelims 2021: casual workers' PF coverage, working hours/overtime and notified bank-only wage payment → source key all three (d). Apply legal coverage conditions; “casual” does not itself exclude a worker from protections.
  • Prelims 2013: disguised unemployment → marginal productivity of labour is zero (c).
  • Mains 2023: structural unemployment → define mismatch; compare usual/CWS/CDS methods and denominators; explain missing underemployment, discouragement, care and job quality; propose higher-frequency surveys, skill/vacancy mapping, better administrative-data integration and comparable reporting.
  • Mains 2015: jobless-growth debate → compare employment elasticity, sector mix and job quality; distinguish productivity-led output growth from zero jobs; discuss informality/measurement; propose labour-intensive manufacturing, MSMEs, skills and service opportunities with evidence.
  • Recall: UR denominator = labour force; WPR = population; usual = 365 days, CWS = 7 days; employed ≠ adequately employed; informal job ≠ informal enterprise; enactment ≠ commencement; elasticity is a ratio.
