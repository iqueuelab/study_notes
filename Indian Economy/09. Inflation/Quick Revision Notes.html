Meaning, formula and base effect

Inflation = sustained rise in the general price level; purchasing power of money falls. A rise in one commodity alone is a relative-price change, not necessarily general inflation.

YoY inflation (%) = [(current index / index in the same month last year) - 1] × 100. Month-on-month inflation uses the preceding month. The base year anchors an index at 100; the comparison base is the earlier period used to calculate inflation.

Base effect: a high comparison-period price level can lower measured inflation even while current prices rise. Example: index 100 → 110 → 115.5 gives inflation 10% → 5%; prices have not fallen.

Choose a recent, representative base year and revise weights when consumption/production patterns change. Avoid treating an abnormal drought, crisis or price spike as representative. Index levels from differently based series cannot be compared directly.

These speed bands are descriptive conventions, not RBI statutory categories. Historical hyperinflation examples: Germany in the early 1920s, Hungary in 1946 and Zimbabwe in the 2000s. Reflation may use fiscal spending/tax relief and monetary easing; currency printing is not its sole instrument.

Why prices rise

TypeTriggerExamples
Demand-pullAggregate demand outruns available output/capacityFiscal stimulus, private spending, credit expansion, exports, higher purchasing power
Cost-pushCosts rise or supply contractsOil/import prices, currency depreciation, wages beyond productivity, indirect taxes, drought/floods, supply-chain disruption
Structural / bottleneckPersistent supply rigidities prevent responseLow agricultural productivity, poor storage/logistics, fragmented markets, shortages

Demand ≠ automatic inflation: greater demand can initially raise output when spare capacity exists. Wage indexation can sustain a wage-price spiral but is not itself the same as an initial demand shock. Higher interest rates normally restrain demand. Depreciation can support exports and also raise imported input costs.

India: volatile monsoons/El Niño, perishable-food shortages, oil-import dependence, geopolitical disruption (including Russia-Ukraine), hoarding, distribution costs and changing diets. MSP increases may affect some prices; the effect depends on procurement, supply response and market conditions. Monetised deficits can add inflationary pressure; productive public investment may instead expand capacity over time.

CPI and consumer food prices

  • Headline inflation: change in the full CPI basket. Core inflation: usually excludes food and fuel; the exact exclusion must be stated. Refined core may also exclude petrol, diesel and lubricants within transport.
  • CPI reflects consumer living costs and is India's monetary-policy anchor. It includes retail margins and relevant consumer taxes, unlike a basic-price producer index.
  • The 2024 series uses HCES 2023-24 consumption information and COICOP 2018 classification. Food-and-beverages weight is 36.75%; the old 2012 classification weight was 45.86%. A like-for-like COICOP reclassification of the old series gives 42.62%, so the raw change also reflects classification changes.
  • Consumer Food Price Index (CFPI): a food subset of CPI; not identical to the entire CPI food-and-beverages division. Old claims about 2012 weights and rural housing must be read with that series label; the 2024 series also measures rural housing.
  • NSO was formed by integrating CSO and NSSO in 2019. DA for industrial workers is linked to CPI-IW, not automatically to CPI Combined.

CPI methodology and January 2026 release; Labour Bureau index bases.

GDP deflator and PPI

GDP deflator = nominal GDP / real GDP × 100. If nominal GDP is 220 and real GDP is 200, deflator = 110. This means prices are 10% above the reference level; it does not prove current-year inflation is 10%. Deflator inflation requires comparison with the previous period's deflator.

  • Includes domestic final consumption, investment, government output and exports; imports are not directly part of domestic production.
  • Its composition can change with output; CPI instead represents household consumption. Neither measure is universally 'best'. GDP estimates/deflators are available quarterly and annually, not as a monthly consumer-price measure.
  • PPI: producer prices received, distinct from consumer prices paid. The B.N. Goldar working group (2014) recommended PPI development; the claim that India has no PPI is now historical.
  • New output PPI is monthly; manufacturing input PPI is experimental; seven service PPIs are quarterly. Output/service PPI uses basic prices; trial input PPI uses purchasers' prices. Service coverage is selective, not the whole service economy. DPIIT launch and release arrangements.

Other indices

FAO Food Price Index: international traded food prices; cereals, vegetable oils, dairy, meat and sugar. January 2026: 123.9 points, down 0.4% month-on-month; this is a dated global indicator, not India's retail inflation. FAO January release.

House Price Index: residential property-price movement. The supplied RBI 2010-11 base, ten-city description refers to the older series. Do not confuse it with NHB RESIDEX, a separate housing index. Property-price inflation need not move with headline CPI.

Who gains and who loses?

Group / channelEffect, subject to conditions
Fixed nominal debtorsUnexpected inflation reduces real repayment burden
Lenders / fixed-income recipientsLose purchasing power unless adequately compensated/indexed
Existing fixed-rate bondsHigher market interest rates generally lower market prices
BusinessesMay gain if selling prices rise faster than costs; squeezed if costs outrun prices
Poor householdsFood inflation hurts disproportionately; real wages may lag
SaversNegative real returns can encourage gold/property holdings
External sectorHigher domestic inflation may erode competitiveness; depreciation has mixed effects
GovernmentNominal revenues may rise, but subsidies, wages, interest and procurement costs can also rise

Inflation tax: erosion of purchasing power of money balances. Inflation premium: component of nominal interest compensating expected inflation. Real interest ≈ nominal interest - inflation; exact relation is (1 + nominal)/(1 + inflation) - 1. A bank's loan-deposit spread is not the definition of inflation premium.

Inflation does not inevitably increase employment, reduce saving, depreciate currency or produce stagflation. Outcomes depend on expectations, policy, supply and whether inflation was anticipated.

Phillips curve and inflation targeting

A.W. Phillips (1958) originally examined wage inflation and unemployment. A short-run inverse relationship can occur under given expectations; supply shocks and changing expectations shift the curve. No permanent long-run employment gain follows from ever-higher inflation. The 1970s oil shocks illustrated stagflation and challenged a stable trade-off.

Urjit Patel committee: constituted 2013, report 2014; recommended CPI as nominal anchor and a transparent inflation-targeting framework. Monetary Policy Framework Agreement: 2015; statutory framework in the RBI Act, 1934: 2016.

Flexible inflation targeting: price stability while keeping growth in mind; not a rule to ignore output or employment. Central Government fixes the target in consultation with RBI under section 45ZA. Target = 4% CPI inflation, tolerance 2-6%. Renewed for 1 April 2026-31 March 2031, after the 2016-21 and 2021-26 periods. Gazette notification S.O. 1580(E).

Failure criterion: average inflation above 6% or below 2% for three consecutive quarters triggers statutory accountability; a single month's breach is different. Benefits: anchor expectations, transparency, protect real returns and reduce inflation uncertainty. Limits: supply shocks, monetary-transmission lags and growth costs of excessive tightening.

Control: match the policy to the cause

PolicyInstrumentsLimitation / trade-off
MonetaryRaise repo/SDF rates, absorb liquidity via OMO sales, adjust CRR; selective credit controls/moral suasionRestrains demand and second-round effects; cannot directly produce onions/oil
FiscalRestrain excess demand, reprioritise expenditure, targeted relief; tax/duty adjustmentBroad contraction can hurt employment/capex; tax rises may raise costs
Supply / administrativeBuffers, imports, lower logistics losses, anti-hoarding action, temporary stock/export measuresExport bans hurt producers; blanket controls can distort incentives
StructuralIrrigation, storage/cold chains, productivity, diversified imports and competitive marketsTakes time; most important for persistent bottlenecks

SDF has been the liquidity-corridor floor since 2022; do not mechanically describe fixed reverse repo as the current floor. SLR/bank rate are textbook instruments but not interchangeable with the operating repo rate. Countercyclical government taxation/spending is fiscal, not qualitative monetary policy.

Food-inflation answer: identify climate/yield, seasonality, oil/feed/fertiliser, protein-demand changes and storage/marketing bottlenecks → RBI anchors expectations and limits spillovers → government tackles supply → protect vulnerable households without fuelling broad demand.

Indian measures and dated examples

  • Essential Commodities Act, 1955 and Prevention of Blackmarketing and Maintenance of Supplies of Essential Commodities Act, 1980: legal tools against shortages/hoarding, subject to lawful notification and safeguards.
  • Price Stabilisation Fund buffers; OMSS wheat/rice releases; Bharat atta/rice/dal; pulse/onion imports and temporary duty or export adjustments.
  • Operation Greens began with TOP (tomato, onion, potato) and was expanded to 41 perishables; Kisan Rail and cold-chain investment improve distribution. GST can reduce tax cascading, but not every GST change reduces prices.
  • Source's wheat stock limits (24 June 2024-31 March 2025), pulse stock/import measures (2024-25), onion buffer/export-duty measures (2024) and 2021-22 buffer quantities are historical examples, not continuing deadlines.
  • Economic Survey 2025-26: April-December 2025 average headline inflation 1.7%, a partial-year figure in the then prevailing CPI series. Economic Survey release.
  • January 2026 CPI: initially 2.75% provisional, revised to 2.74% final on the new 2024 base. Never compare index levels across rebased series directly. MoSPI February release.
