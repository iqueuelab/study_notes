Money and Money Supply

1. Money: meaning, functions and qualities

  • Money: anything generally accepted as payment for goods/services and settlement of debts. Legal recognition is important for currency, but all money is not legal tender: bank deposits also function as money.
  • Medium of exchange: removes barter's double coincidence of wants; sell wheat for money, then buy rice.
  • Unit of account: common measure for prices, accounting and comparison.
  • Store of value: transfers purchasing power across time; inflation erodes its real value. Keynes emphasised holding money as an asset; avoid the absolute claim that earlier economists never recognised this function.
  • Standard of deferred payment: denominates future obligations, enabling credit and contracts.
  • Six desirable qualities: acceptability, portability, durability, divisibility, uniformity and controlled scarcity. A payment card is a means of accessing money, not a separate currency.

2. Types of money: distinguish the basis

  • Commodity money: a commodity itself used as money, e.g. gold. Metallic money: coins made of metals. Paper money: notes; these describe material, not necessarily backing.
  • Full-bodied money: commodity/intrinsic value approximately equals face value. Token money: intrinsic value below face value.
  • Representative money: redeemable claim on a backing commodity; representative full-bodied money has equivalent bullion backing held by the issuer. Modern Indian notes are not promises to deliver gold.
  • Fiat money: value rests on legal authority, public confidence and acceptance, without commodity convertibility. “Non-commodity-backed” and “government-backed” describe its features, not two separate types. Market purchasing power is not fixed by government decree alone.
  • Fiduciary/optional money: accepted on trust/agreement, e.g. cheques or bills of exchange; a creditor need not accept these as legal tender. A cheque instructs a deposit transfer; it is not an additional stock of money.
  • Commercial-bank money: deposit balances usable for payment, created partly through lending. Actual money: general textbook label for money used in exchange. Standard money: recognised currency/unit of account; do not confuse this with the intrinsic-value test for full-bodied money.
  • Near money: liquid assets readily convertible into transaction money, e.g. treasury bills, certificates of deposit and short-term deposits. Classification depends on the aggregate: savings-bank deposits may already count as money; they are not universally “outside money”.

3. Legal tender and currency issuance in India

  • Legal tender: legally valid means of discharging a monetary debt, subject to applicable limits. Unlimited: no aggregate payment ceiling for valid notes; limited: statutory transaction ceiling for coins.
  • RBI issues banknotes; Government of India issues coins and ₹1 notes. RBI distributes coins. RBI notes are Central-Government-guaranteed; ₹1 notes/coins are government liabilities. Banknotes carry the RBI Governor's signature; ₹1 notes carry the Finance Secretary's.
  • Coin limits per transaction: denominations ₹1 and above: ₹1,000; 50 paise: ₹10. Voluntary acceptance above these limits is possible; coin deposits into bank accounts are a different matter.
  • ₹2,000 notes: withdrawal from circulation announced in 2023, but they continue as legal tender. Withdrawal from circulation and demonetisation are different. RBI currency FAQ
  • 2016 demonetisation: old-series ₹500/₹1,000 notes lost legal-tender status from midnight 8 November 2016. New-series ₹500 notes are valid.
  • RBI Act, 1934: Section 22—banknote issuance; 26—legal tender/guarantee; 33—Issue Department assets. The printed promise is a currency obligation, not gold convertibility.
  • Seigniorage: economic benefit from issuing money. Face value minus production cost is a simplified illustration; RBI does not book every newly printed note's face value as profit. Currency is a liability; earnings on backing assets, costs and provisioning affect surplus transferred to government.

4. Money demand: Keynes's liquidity preference

  • Demand for money: desired holdings of liquid purchasing power; distinct from demand for income or loans. Influenced by income, prices, interest/opportunity cost and expectations.
  • Transactions motive: routine household/business payments. Higher real activity/income or prices generally increases nominal cash requirements; payment frequency and digital arrangements also matter.
  • Precautionary motive: emergencies and unforeseen opportunities; normally rises with income and uncertainty. Transactions + precautionary holdings are active cash balances.
  • Speculative motive: holding money rather than bonds because of expected returns/capital losses; linked to the store-of-value function. Generally interest rate ↑ → money demand ↓.
  • Bond price and yield move inversely: low current rates may lead people to expect future rate increases and bond-price falls, encouraging cash holdings.
  • Liquidity trap: at very low rates, additional liquidity may be willingly held rather than used for spending; conventional rate cuts become less effective. It has no universal “3%” threshold. Expectations, weak demand and balance-sheet problems matter.

5. Money supply: stock, sources and determinants

  • Money supply: stock of monetary assets held by the public at a point in time. It is a stock, unlike annual income/GDP, which is a flow.
  • “Public” excludes the money-producing banking system and relevant government holdings. Deposits owed by banks to the public count; banks' own cash/interbank balances are not public money. Reserve money separately includes bank reserves.
  • RBI: supplies currency/reserves and influences liquidity through policy rates, reserve requirements, securities and foreign-exchange operations. Banks: create deposit money when lending or buying assets from non-banks.
  • Loan → deposit creation; repayment of loan principal → deposit destruction. Lending is constrained by capital, liquidity, regulation, borrower demand and credit risk—not an unlimited mechanical process.
  • Determinants: reserve requirements, central-bank operations, banks' lending decisions, public currency preference, business cycle, financial inclusion/payment innovation, government financing and external flows.
  • Booms often support credit/deposit growth; recessions can weaken it, but this is not an unavoidable rule. Financial innovation may change how aggregates are measured.
  • Velocity changes spending per unit of money; it does not itself mechanically create additional money stock. Government borrowing/spending does not automatically increase broad money: financing source and subsequent transactions matter.

6. Conventional monetary aggregates

  • C = currency with the public; DD = demand deposits with banks; OD = other deposits with RBI; TD = time deposits with banks. Currency with the public = currency in circulation minus banks' cash holdings.
  • M₀ (reserve/base/high-powered money) = currency in circulation + bankers' deposits with RBI + other deposits with RBI. Bank reserves make M₀ different from public money.
  • M₁ (narrow money) = C + DD + OD. The chapter's table omits OD; include it.
  • M₂ = M₁ + savings deposits with post-office savings banks.
  • M₃ (broad money) = M₁ + TD. Main broad-money measure used in India.
  • M₄ = M₃ + all post-office savings-bank deposits, excluding National Savings Certificates.
  • M₂ and M₃ are different additions to M₁: they are not simply successive nested steps. Moving to broader aggregates generally adds less-liquid assets; do not treat M₀ > M₁ > M₂ > M₃ > M₄ as a universal size or liquidity ranking. RBI definitions

7. New monetary and liquidity aggregates

  • 1998 Working Group: added aggregates using residency and maturity distinctions. Conventional M₁–M₄ did not simply disappear; distinguish new NM definitions from conventional measures.
  • NM₀ = M₀; NM₁ = C + DD + OD. Demand/current deposits should not be double-counted.
  • NM₂ = NM₁ + residents' short-term time deposits, contractual maturity up to and including 1 year.
  • NM₃ = NM₂ + residents' long-term time deposits + banks' call/term funding from financial institutions. Residency treatment excludes specified non-resident foreign-currency repatriable deposits from NM₂/NM₃; do not equate every non-resident deposit category. RBI guide
  • L₁ = NM₃ + all post-office savings-bank deposits, excluding NSCs.
  • L₂ = L₁ + term deposits with term-lending/refinancing institutions + term borrowings by financial institutions + CDs issued by financial institutions.
  • L₃ = L₂ + public deposits of NBFCs. These extend measurement beyond banks; post-office deposits are included in L₁, contrary to the chapter's introductory exclusion claim. RBI liquidity definitions
  • Publication rhythm described in the chapter: M₀ weekly; M₁/M₃ fortnightly; L₁/L₂ monthly; L₃ quarterly. These are series conventions, not a promise that every release arrives at that interval.

8. Money multiplier and velocity

  • Observed money multiplier = M₃ / M₀. If M₃ = ₹600 and M₀ = ₹100, multiplier = 6. A rise in M₃ raises the ratio only if M₀ does not rise proportionately or faster.
  • Simple textbook deposit multiplier = 1/r, where r is a reserve ratio; assumes full lending/redepositing, no currency leakage and no excess reserves. It is not identical to the observed M₃/M₀ ratio.
  • With public currency preference c = C/D and bank reserve ratio r = R/D, a simplified multiplier is (1+c)/(r+c). Higher currency leakage/excess reserves generally lowers it; stronger banking habits generally raise it.
  • Higher CRR reduces lendable resources; SLR can constrain lending but cannot simply be added into every multiplier formula. Financial inclusion encourages deposit use. Loan demand, capital constraints and risk affect realised credit expansion.
  • Historical chapter claim: multiplier rose broadly from the mid-1990s to 2016–17 and weakened thereafter, including 2019–20 to 2022–23. Treat this as period-specific, not a perpetual trend; neither every RBI deposit nor reverse-repo placement is mechanically identical to required reserves.
  • Velocity V = nominal GDP / chosen money stock M; MV = PY, where PY is nominal output. If GDP = ₹1,000 and M = ₹200, V = 5 per year. Use consistent period/stock convention and state the chosen aggregate.
  • Velocity reflects payment frequency, income/spending patterns, confidence, interest rates, credit/EMIs, financial inclusion, innovation and uncertainty. Development or EMI use does not guarantee a rise. Low-income households may spend receipts quickly, but economy-wide velocity is not inferred from that alone.
  • Multiplier: broad-money stock per unit of monetary base. Velocity: nominal spending/output per unit of money over time. GDP cannot be controlled through money supply alone when velocity/real output change.

9. How monetary expansion works

  • RBI buys government securities → bank reserves/liquidity rise. Sale normally absorbs liquidity. Broad-money effects depend partly on whether the counterparty is a bank or non-bank.
  • RBI buys foreign exchange → injects rupee liquidity; sale normally absorbs it. Sterilisation through offsetting operations can neutralise the liquidity effect.
  • Lower reserve requirements can support lending; actual credit expansion depends on demand and bank constraints. Government financing through bank/central-bank transactions can affect deposits/reserves; borrowing from existing private savings is not automatically money creation.
  • PYQ trap: with money supply fixed, greater money demand tends to raise interest rates in the standard liquidity-preference model.

10. Indian currency: history and banknote facts

  • INR; ₹; ₹1 = 100 paise. Symbol combines Devanagari “र” and Roman “R”, with two horizontal strokes.
  • 1861 Paper Currency Act: government monopoly of note issue replaced private/presidency-bank issuance; James Wilson was associated with early paper-currency reform. RBI began operations 1 April 1935. ₹1 notes were reintroduced in 1940; the chapter says August, while RBI records issuance since July 1940. RBI currency history
  • ₹10,000: highest denomination historically printed; introduced 1938, demonetised 1946, reintroduced 1954, demonetised again 1978.
  • 1949 ₹1 note: first note of independent India; Lion Capital at Sarnath replaced the British monarch's portrait. 1970: “Satyameva Jayate” introduced. October 1987: ₹500 with Gandhi portrait and Ashoka Pillar watermark.
  • Languages: 15 in the language panel plus Hindi and English = 17. Paper is cotton-based. Equal serial numbers can occur with differing inset letter, printing year or Governor's signature; some notes have no inset letter.
  • 2011: non-sequential numbering introduced for efficiency. Star series: an asterisk identifies replacement notes in packets; it does not invalidate a note.
  • Currency backing includes eligible assets under Section 33, such as gold, government securities and foreign securities; asset backing ≠ commodity convertibility.
  • MANI (Mobile Aided Note Identifier): launched 1 January 2020 for visually impaired users; identifies denominations through audio. Hindi/English plus 11 additional languages are described in the chapter: Assamese, Bengali, Gujarati, Kannada, Malayalam, Marathi, Odia, Punjabi, Tamil, Telugu and Urdu. Denomination recognition does not establish authenticity.

11. Banknote series and motifs

  • Ashoka Pillar series: Lion Capital imagery. Mahatma Gandhi series, 1996: Gandhi portrait on front; Lion Capital retained elsewhere, with Gandhi watermark.
  • MG 2005 security upgrade: ₹50/₹100—August 2005; ₹500/₹1,000—October 2005; ₹10—April 2006; ₹20—August 2006. Old ₹500/₹1,000 subsequently demonetised in 2016.
  • MG New Series, 2016 onward: smaller, distinctive colours; motifs reflect India's heritage/scientific achievements. ₹2,000 was announced in November 2016 with Mangalyaan; withdrawal from circulation began in 2023.
  • Reverse motifs: ₹10—Konark Sun Temple; ₹20—Ellora Caves; ₹50—Hampi with chariot; ₹100—Rani ki Vav; ₹200—Sanchi Stupa; ₹500—Red Fort; ₹2,000—Mangalyaan.

12. Cryptocurrency: opportunities and risks

  • Cryptoassets: cryptographically secured digital assets, often transferred on distributed ledgers. Examples: Bitcoin, Ether (Ethereum network), Litecoin and XRP (associated with Ripple). Decentralisation varies; not all digital money is cryptocurrency.
  • Potential uses: programmable transfers, some cross-border payments, tokenisation, technology/startup opportunities and financial access. Low cost, inclusion and greater safety are possible benefits, not guarantees; internet access, fees, skills and safeguards matter.
  • Risks: volatility/speculation, exchange/wallet hacks, fraud, consumer losses, illicit finance and monetary/financial-stability challenges. Public chains are often pseudonymous, not fully anonymous; transaction tracing is possible.
  • Most unbacked crypto has no issuer-guaranteed redemption; lack of gold backing alone is not the decisive difference from fiat money. Stablecoins may have reserves but involve reserve/issuer risk.
  • Environmental cost: proof-of-work mining can be energy-intensive; not all networks use mining or the same energy model. Crypto does not inherently improve monetary/fiscal-policy control; currency substitution may weaken sovereignty.
  • Indian society: youth participation and innovation coexist with scams, speculative losses, unequal digital access and regulatory/compliance challenges. Assess financial inclusion against actual usability and consumer protection, rather than assuming universal access.

13. India: correct the regulatory timeline

  • April 2018: RBI restricted regulated entities from providing services connected with virtual currencies; this was not a parliamentary blanket criminal ban on owning/trading crypto.
  • 4 March 2020: Supreme Court set aside that RBI circular in *Internet and Mobile Association of India v. RBI*. The chapter's “May 2020” date is incorrect. In May 2021, RBI clarified banks could not rely on the quashed circular; due diligence obligations continued. RBI clarification
  • 2021 proposed bill: historical proposal, not an enacted law merely because it was announced. Avoid memorising a proposed blanket ban as existing law.
  • March 2023 onward: specified VDA service activities fall under PMLA; service providers must meet FIU-IND registration, reporting and AML/CFT obligations. Thus “crypto has no regulation” is inaccurate. Regulation does not confer legal-tender status. FIU-IND framework
  • Digital rupee (e₹): RBI-issued CBDC, legal tender and RBI liability. A bank deposit is the bank's liability; UPI is a payment mechanism, not a separate currency. Private crypto is distinct from e₹. RBI digital-rupee FAQ
  • International examples are historical: El Salvador adopted Bitcoin in 2021; its 2025 reforms made private acceptance voluntary. CAR adopted crypto legal-tender provisions in 2022 and revised them in 2023. Do not use the chapter's two-country list as a current rule. IMF: El Salvador, IMF: CAR

14. Blockchain, NFTs and stablecoins

  • Blockchain: ledger whose records are grouped into cryptographically linked blocks; participants use consensus to agree on updates. It can be public/permissionless or private/permissioned. Blockchain and cryptocurrency are not synonyms.
  • Tamper resistance: changing old records is difficult under the system's assumptions, not impossible under every attack/governance arrangement. Security does not automatically protect wallets, exchanges or erroneous inputs.
  • Uses beyond crypto: supply-chain records, provenance, identity/credential verification and shared registries. Permissionless applications can be built without a central gatekeeper; this does not mean every blockchain has unrestricted access.
  • NFT: non-fungible token—unique identifier/record; not mutually interchangeable like equal-denomination rupees or units of the same fungible token. Used with artwork, music, collectibles and other assets; can be bought/sold.
  • NFT ownership ≠ automatic copyright or ownership of the underlying work. Rights depend on the contract/law; links/files can remain off-chain. Fractional arrangements exist, so “never divisible in any form” is too absolute.
  • Stablecoin: token designed to track a reference value, often a fiat currency. It may use reserves or other mechanisms; a peg can fail. It is not automatically government-guaranteed money or a CBDC.

15. PYQ recall and quick revision

  • 2021 money multiplier: banking habit increases → currency leakage tends to fall → multiplier rises. Answer (c).
  • 2020 blockchain: public-ledger/permissionless descriptions fit the question; crypto-only claim is false. Statements 1 and 3; answer (d). Real-world permissioned chains also exist.
  • 2013 money demand: supply unchanged + demand rises → interest rate rises. Answer (b).
  • Four functions: exchange, account, store, deferred payment. Three motives: transactions, precautionary, speculative.
  • M₀ uses currency in circulation; M₁ uses currency with public. M₁ includes OD; M₃ adds time deposits.
  • Multiplier = M₃/M₀; velocity = nominal GDP/M. Higher banking habit generally raises the former; faster spending raises the latter.
  • Fiat ≠ gold-redeemable; cheque ≠ legal tender; e₹ ≠ UPI; NFT ≠ copyright; stablecoin ≠ guaranteed stability.
  • Mains answer structure: define money/technology → explain mechanism → benefits → risks/distributional effects → regulation, literacy, cybersecurity and consumer safeguards. No mains PYQs appear in the supplied chapter; these are answer cues, not PYQs.
