The Maurya Empire

1. Chronology and extent

Magadha → Pataliputra → first large subcontinental empire. Dates vary between reconstructions: the chapter uses c.324–187 BCE; common modern chronologies use approximately 322/321–185 BCE. Do not mix accession and coronation dates as though all sources agree.

Ruler / stageEssential facts
Chandragupta, c.321–297 BCEOverthrew Nandas with Chanakya/Kautilya; consolidated Magadha and north-west. Greek Sandrokottos/Sandrocottus identified with him by William Jones
Seleucus settlement, c.305–303 BCEConflict followed by territorial/diplomatic agreement; areas associated with Arachosia, Gedrosia and Paropamisadae passed under Mauryan control; 500 elephants given to Seleucus. Exact boundaries and marriage-alliance details debated
Bindusara, c.297–273 BCEGreek Amitrochates, linked to Amitraghata (“slayer of foes”); extended/consolidated southern control, but precise conquests uncertain
Ashoka, c.268–232 BCEAccession sometimes placed c.273 BCE, with later consecration; Buddhist accounts describe a succession struggle, not independently proved slaughter of 99 brothers
Kalinga, c.261 BCEConquered in eighth regnal year after consecration, recorded in Major Rock Edict XIII; remorse became central to royal dhamma policy
After c.232 BCERegional fragmentation and later rulers including Dasharatha; Brihadratha, last Maurya, killed by commander Pushyamitra Shunga, conventionally c.185 BCE (chapter: 187)
  • Imperial centres connected the north-west/Afghanistan, Gangetic basin, western India and much of the Deccan. Far-southern Cholas, Pandyas, Satiyaputras and Keralaputras were neighbours, not all Mauryan provinces. Sri Lanka/Tamraparni was outside the empire.
  • A map of maximum extent is a reconstruction; inscription distribution does not establish identical administrative control everywhere. Core Magadha, provincial centres, routes, forests and frontier areas differed.
  • Jain tradition: Chandragupta renounced, accompanied Bhadrabahu south to Shravanabelagola, and practised sallekhana. Treat as later religious tradition, not a contemporary eyewitness record.
  • Bindusara's association with Ajivikas, and Taranatha's account of conquest “between the seas”, are literary traditions. Greek sources mention Deimachus; Pliny records Dionysius sent by Ptolemy II, but the exact recipient/timing is less secure.

SourceUse / caution

Arthashastra: artha = material/political well-being, not merely money. Discusses rulers' welfare duties alongside coercion, spies and practical power politics. R. Shamasastry brought it to modern scholarly attention in 1905; publication followed. “India's Machiavelli” is a comparison, not an ancient title.

Indica traps: its seven occupational classes—philosophers, cultivators, herders/hunters, artisans/traders, soldiers, overseers, councillors—are not seven varnas. Claims of no slavery, no famine or no writing conflict with other evidence. Descriptions of Krishna/Heracles and Shiva/Dionysus reflect Greek identifications.

3. Ashoka: personal faith and public dhamma

  • Earlier service at Ujjain and Taxila, mother Subhadrangi, wife Devi/Mahadevi, and association with Nigrodha or Samudra belong principally to Buddhist narrative traditions.
  • Other named queens: Asandhimitra, Padmavati, Tissarakkha; Karuvaki is named in the Queen's Edict as mother of Tivara. Mahinda and Sanghamitta are prominent in Sri Lankan mission traditions.
  • Inscriptional titles: Devanampiya (“beloved of the gods”), Piyadasi/Priyadarshi; personal name Ashoka appears at Maski, with other named versions also known.
  • Buddhism influenced Ashoka, but public dhamma was a broad ethical and social programme, not simply an order to convert everyone to Buddhism. Respect extended to Brahmanas, shramanas and other sects; Ajivika cave gifts demonstrate patronage beyond Buddhism.
  • Duties: respect parents/elders/teachers; kindness to servants and enslaved people; generosity; truth, purity, restraint and compassion; avoid needless killing and sectarian abuse; promote welfare and responsible government.
  • Dhamma-vijaya prioritised ethical persuasion over conquest. It did not mean dismantling the army, relinquishing Kalinga or abolishing punishment; Ashoka retained coercive authority towards forest peoples and the death penalty.

Interpretations: Buddhist-inspired kingship; a shared ethic among diverse subjects; paternal governance and imperial cohesion. These overlap. Avoid describing it as either entirely sectarian or modern secular democracy.

4. Inscriptions: language, script and geography

  • Prakrit in Brahmi predominates; Kharoshthi at Shahbazgarhi and Mansehra, read right to left. Brahmi generally reads left to right.
  • Greek and Aramaic occur in north-western/Afghan records, including Kandahar. Sanskrit and Pali are not the standard language of the major Ashokan corpus.
  • James Prinsep, 1837: key decipherment of Brahmi/Ashokan inscriptions; do not repeat the chapter's 1887 error.
  • Learn categories rather than a supposedly fixed total of monuments: 14 Major Rock Edicts, Minor Rock Edicts, 2 Separate Kalinga Edicts, 7 Major Pillar Edicts, minor pillar/Schism/Queen's records and cave dedications. Surviving versions vary by place.

5. Statecraft: seven limbs and governing machinery

Saptanga: Swamin—king; Amatya—officials; Janapada—territory/people; Durga—fort; Kosha—treasury; Danda/Bala—coercive/military power; Mitra—ally. These are interdependent elements, not seven separate ministries.

  • Textual salary scales show hierarchy: 48,000 panas for top officers; 12,000 for council members in cited lists; not a complete surviving Mauryan payroll.
  • Appointment tests in Arthashastra: dharma, money, desire and fear—assessment of loyalty/integrity. Security included stationary (samstha) and mobile (sanchara) spies, palace precautions and female guards.

Provinces and local administration

Five major centres: Pataliputra—imperial core; Taxila—north-west; Ujjain—west; Tosali—Kalinga/east; Suvarnagiri—south. Tosali and Kalinga are not two additional separate eastern provinces in this standard reconstruction.

  • Functions overlapped; inscriptional and Arthashastra schemes cannot be mechanically combined into a perfectly uniform nationwide hierarchy.
  • Arthashastra settlement units: sthaniya—800 villages; dronamukha—400; kharvatika—200; samgrahana—10. Dronamukha is one word, not separate “drona” and “mukha” units.
  • Nagaraka: city administration; gramika/gramani: village head; gramavriddhas: elders; gopas/sthanikas: records and revenue duties.
  • Megasthenes' Pataliputra municipal board: 30 members, six groups of five—industry; foreigners; births/deaths; trade/weights/measures; manufactured goods; sales-tax collection. Care of foreigners included medical assistance, burial and estate return. Reported sales levy one-tenth; do not confuse with agricultural one-sixth.

6. Army, law and diplomacy

Military system

  • Large standing forces and forts, backed by revenue, supplies, trained commanders and reserve/auxiliary forces. Greek army totals are literary estimates, not audited numbers.
  • Six military committees of five in Megasthenes: navy; transport/supplies; infantry; cavalry; chariots; elephants—distinct from six municipal committees.
  • Senapati, nayakas and specialised officers: patti/padati—infantry; ashva—horses; ratha—chariots; hasti—elephants. Maula: hereditary troops; bhritaka: hired soldiers; forest/allied contingents also used.
  • Durga includes fortified centres and siege preparedness; danda/bala is the armed/coercive limb. The chapter discusses the army under both—avoid swapping the definitions.

Justice

  • King supreme; dharmasthas associated with civil adjudication; pradeshtas with criminal/order functions; local adjudication also involved elders.
  • Four legal bases: dharma—normative/sacred law; vyavahara—legal procedure/evidence; charitra—custom; rajashasana—royal command. The source's extra “royal proclamations” under dharma is misplaced.
  • Fines, forfeiture, corporal and capital penalties; status-based inequalities coexisted with Ashoka's stated pursuit of fair procedure. Pillar IV's three-day respite does not abolish execution.
  • Bandhanagara: prison; local detention/police establishments also existed. Ashoka urged officials to avoid unjust imprisonment and harsh treatment.

7. Economy, taxes and occupational organisation

Agriculture and revenue

  • Agriculture mainstay: rice, wheat, barley, millets, pulses, oilseeds, sugarcane; irrigation and new settlements expanded production. Kshetra/kshetrika: field/landholder; upavasa: tenant in the cited terminology.
  • State domains and private holdings coexisted; sitadhyaksha supervised crown agriculture. Settling cultivators on uncultivated land enlarged the revenue base; sharecroppers and dependent labour worked state/private lands.

Taxes paid in cash and kind, not cash alone. Army/officials, roads, forts, irrigation and welfare consumed revenue. Grain reserves and relief records matter; no credible inference that famine never occurred.

Crafts, commerce and regulation

  • Textiles: Varanasi, Mathura, Bengal, Gandhara, Ujjain. State enterprises and private activity coexisted; mines, metals, forests, pastures and trade regulated.
  • Officers: sutradhyaksha—textiles; akaradhyaksha—mines; lohadhyaksha—metals/iron; panyadhyaksha—trade/pricing; samsthadhyaksha—markets; rupadarshaka—coin inspection; pautavadhyaksha—weights/measures; vivitadhyaksha—pastures; sunadhyaksha—slaughterhouses.
  • Pataliputra—internal routes; Taxila—north-west/Central Asia; Tamralipti—eastern port; Bharukachchha/Broach—western port. Land, river and maritime exchange linked regions and external markets.
  • Shrenis/pugas: occupational/corporate associations; jyeshthaka a headman. Production, quality, collective interests and exchange important; do not import every later guild-banking function uncritically into this period.
  • Punch-marked silver coins facilitated exchange; moneylending and interest attested. Iron use expanded but predated the Mauryas; they did not invent iron technology. Labour included karmakaras (wage workers), enslaved people and pledged/dependent workers.

8. Society and slavery

  • Varna hierarchy persisted alongside occupational change and urban groups. Shudras participated in cultivation/crafts; Chandalas and Shvapakas, described as marginal/excluded groups, reveal inequality. Ashoka's edicts do not provide a full census of caste or social practice.
  • Dasas and bhatakas/bhritakas appear in ethical instructions. Megasthenes' “no slaves” claim cannot establish absence of unfree labour; institutions differed from Greek slavery.
  • Arthashastra differentiates forms of dependence, protection, redemption/manumission and restrictions. A woman enslaved to a master who fathers her child and the child's status are important PYQ themes.
  • 2022 PYQ: retain supplied key (b), statements 2 and 3; statement 1 on judicial punishment has generated interpretive disagreement. Do not generalise this exam key into a claim that every historical form of penal servitude was absent.

Achaemenid connections: royal inscriptions, polish, bell/lotus forms and animal capitals invite comparison. Persian columns often fluted, based and part of buildings; Ashokan pillars usually smooth, free-standing and monolithic in the shaft. Connections coexist with distinctive Indian forms; not simple copying.

Learning centres included Taxila, Varanasi and Ujjain; “university” is a modern shorthand, not proof of a modern institution. Kalpasutra is traditionally associated with Bhadrabahu; surviving compilation/transmission is later and layered.

10. Decline: use several explanations

  • Succession and overextension: loss of strong central authority after Ashoka; provincial autonomy, distance and regional diversity made cohesion difficult.
  • Provincial oppression: Taxila narratives and Kalinga instructions point to tensions over officials; evidence of concern, not a direct measurement of rebellion everywhere.
  • Fiscal strain: large army, bureaucracy and expansion costs; D.D. Kosambi stressed economic pressure. Plausible interpretation, not a surviving imperial balance sheet.
  • Regional resource bases: agricultural/iron/craft expansion helped new powers consolidate beyond Magadha; not a sudden single transfer of technology.
  • North-western pressures: Hellenistic/Bactrian developments and later invasions interacted with declining control. Do not repeat the claim that the Greeks first entered India in 206 BCE: Alexander came earlier; Antiochus III's 206 BCE expedition is a distinct event, while Bactrian independence preceded it.
  • “Brahmanical reaction” / “pacifism weakened army”: debated older explanations, associated with writers including Haraprasad Shastri. Respect for Brahmanas, non-Buddhist gifts and continued coercive institutions weaken a simple anti-Brahmana/disarmed-empire thesis.
  • The absence of a Great Wall does not explain Mauryan collapse. China's wall-building, Scythian movement and South Asian invasions cannot be compressed into one direct cause.

2022 GS I: lion and bull figures

Question: Discuss the significance of the lion and bull figures in Indian mythology, art and architecture.

Plan: distinguish religious meanings from political authority → lion/Buddha and Sarnath, bull/fertility and Rampurva → wider examples such as Nandi/Shiva and lion imagery in temples → explain continuity, patronage and changing contexts. Do not restrict an all-India question to Mauryan monuments.

2015 History Optional: West Asian and Mediterranean contacts

Plan: Seleucus agreement/envoys → trade and routes → Greek/Aramaic inscriptions and Hellenistic rulers → artistic comparison and selective adaptation → limits: diplomatic/ethical claims are not evidence of military rule over Mediterranean kingdoms.

2014 History Optional: dhamma and military strength

Question: Discuss different interpretations of historians about the nature of Ashoka's “dhamma”. Did his principle of “dhamma-vijaya” render the Mauryan empire militaristically weak?

Plan: Buddhist inspiration, public ethic, welfare/cohesion → Kalinga and restrained conquest → retained army, punishment and forest warning → multicausal decline after Ashoka. A correlation with later decline does not establish pacifism as its cause.
