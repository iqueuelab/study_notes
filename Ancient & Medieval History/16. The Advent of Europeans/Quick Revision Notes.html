The Advent of Europeans

1. From maritime trade to territorial power

  • Europeans entered an existing Indian Ocean trading network. Direct access to spices, textiles and profits, royal rivalry and missionary activity encouraged ocean voyages.
  • Prince Henry patronised Portuguese exploration; Bartolomeu Dias rounded the Cape in 1488 after departing in 1487. Vasco da Gama reached Calicut/Kozhikode in May 1498, under the Zamorin.
  • “First Europeans” here means the first of the early modern European maritime powers: Europe–India contacts existed much earlier. A factory meant a trading post/warehouse, not necessarily a manufacturing plant.
  • Arrival/charter dates differ: Portuguese arrival 1498; English company 1600; Dutch VOC 1602; Danish company 1616; French company 1664. English company formation preceded Dutch formation, although Dutch settlement dates differ.

  • Cartaz: naval pass regulating shipping, routes and cargo; coercion, seizures and fortified ports supported commercial control. Portuguese supremacy never meant complete control of every Indian Ocean route.
  • 1632: Shah Jahan's forces took Hooghly. 1661: Bombay included in the Anglo-Portuguese marriage treaty; Charles II married Catherine of Braganza in 1662. 1668: Crown leased Bombay to the English Company for £10 annually.
  • 1663: Dutch captured Cochin; 1739: Marathas took Bassein and Salsette. Portuguese enclaves Goa, Daman and Diu remained until 1961.
  • Decline: limited resources, corruption, religious coercion/local resistance, conflict with Asian powers, Dutch/English competition and diversion of resources towards Brazil.
  • Legacy: Goa printing press 1556; tobacco, potatoes and other American crops spread through Portuguese and other exchange routes; maritime/military techniques, churches, language and missionary education. Garcia da Orta's medicinal-plant work, 1563, contributed to European botanical knowledge; India already possessed medical traditions.
  • Trap: Albuquerque did not conquer all Ceylon. Do not credit Portuguese with introducing every firearm or military technology to India.

3. Dutch: spice trade and Indian textile bases

  • Cornelis de Houtman, 1596: reached Java, not India. VOC established 1602; combined commercial capital with powers to fortify, make treaties and wage war.
  • Masulipatnam/Machilipatnam, 1605: earliest Dutch factory in India. Pulicat, 1610: important Coromandel headquarters and Fort Geldria; headquarters later shifted to Nagapattinam, 1690.
  • Other centres: Surat, Broach, Cochin, Chinsura (Fort Gustavus), Kasimbazar, Patna and Balasore. Trade included textiles, indigo, silk, rice, saltpetre and opium; Indian goods financed Southeast Asian spice commerce.
  • Batavia founded 1619: do not describe this as conquest of all Java. Amboyna incident, 1623: execution of English merchants and others intensified Anglo-Dutch rivalry.
  • Biderra/Bedara/Chinsura, 1759: English Company defeated Dutch forces in Bengal. Dutch trading possessions did not disappear immediately; final transfers followed later agreements.
  • PYQ distinction: earliest overall = Masulipatnam; earliest among Surat/Pulicat/Cochin/Kasimbazar = Pulicat. Coromandel grants were linked to Vijayanagara successors/local rulers, not the Gajapatis.

Farrukhsiyar's farman, 1717

  • John Surman's embassy, 1715, included physician William Hamilton; Surman was the envoy, not Governor of Calcutta.
  • Farmans confirmed Company privileges: Bengal duty concessions for ₹3,000 annually, additional village-rental rights around Calcutta; Surat arrangement for ₹10,000 annually; southern trading/rental concessions. Often called the Company's “Magna Carta”.
  • Dastak = duty-free transit pass for entitled Company goods. Servants used privileges to evade duties on private trade, depriving the Nawab of revenue and disadvantaging Indian traders.
  • Do not read a Company exemption as a legal exemption for every English trader or every private cargo.

Pondicherry trap: earlier Portuguese and Dutch presence preceded the durable French settlement. The Dutch seized it in 1693, and the English occupied it in 1761; therefore “English never occupied Pondicherry” is false.

7. Bengal: Plassey, Buxar and diwani

From commercial privilege to conflict

  • Bengal's fertile agriculture, textiles, silk, saltpetre and trade made it a rich base. Nawab sequence: Murshid Quli Khan → Shuja-ud-din → Sarfaraz Khan → Alivardi Khan → Siraj-ud-Daulah.
  • Siraj succeeded Alivardi in 1756; conflicts involved unauthorised fortifications, abuse of dastaks and English shelter to Krishna Ballabh.
  • Court rivals included Ghaseti Begum, Shaukat Jang and Mir Jafar. Ghaseti was Alivardi's daughter, not his widow.
  • Siraj captured Kasimbazar and Fort William, June 1756. Prisoner deaths associated with the “Black Hole” account remain contested in number and detail; do not treat Holwell's figures as settled facts.
  • English survivors withdrew to Fulta; Clive and Admiral Watson recovered Calcutta early in 1757. Company officials then conspired with Mir Jafar, Jagat Seth and other court/financial interests.

After Mir Jafar's death in 1765, Najm-ud-Daulah became the nominal Nawab. Company control over appointments, finance and military power hollowed out the nizamat.

Dual Government, 1765–72

  • Diwani: revenue and civil administration/justice; nizamat: executive authority, policing and criminal justice. Robert Clive established the dual arrangement.
  • Company initially exercised diwani through Indian deputies, not a fully developed direct British collectorate from the first day. Muhammad Reza Khan combined deputy roles in Bengal; Shitab Rai handled Bihar revenue.
  • Company and servants extracted revenue, forced unfavourable trading terms and financed exports/wars from Indian resources, reducing the need for bullion imports.
  • Bengal famine, 1770: crop failure interacted with extraction and administrative neglect. Mortality was catastrophic; the often quoted one-third is a historical estimate, not a secure census count.
  • Plassey vs Buxar: political foothold and resources vs decisive regional military victory leading to formal diwani. Neither made later resistance impossible.

8. Mysore: four wars, three treaties

  • Wodeyar Mysore grew increasingly independent amid Vijayanagara's decline after Talikota, 1565; the dynasty predated that battle.
  • Hyder Ali strengthened army/artillery with European expertise, established an arms factory at Dindigul and controlled valuable Malabar trade. Tipu Sultan continued military and administrative reform and sought foreign alliances.

  • Third-war Company gains: Malabar, Baramahal and Dindigul; Nizam/Marathas also gained. Coorg was detached from Tipu and placed under its Raja with Company protection; do not call every detached area an immediate British district.
  • Tipu sent envoys towards Ottoman Turkey (1786 mission) and France; a French revolutionary/Jacobin association and Tree of Liberty at Seringapatam symbolised the connection. Such contacts alarmed Wellesley but did not deliver a French army in 1799.
  • After 1799: Krishnaraja Wodeyar III installed; Purnaiya became diwan. Company retained strategic regions including Canara, Coimbatore, Wayanad and Seringapatam; Nizam received a share; Tipu's family sent to Vellore.
  • 1831: Bentinck's government assumed Mysore administration on alleged misgovernment; 1881: restored under Ripon. British paramountcy remained.
  • Memory: Madras—Mangalore—Seringapatam—Tipu's death. Hostages = Third War; death = Fourth War. Lord Wellesley was Governor-General; Arthur Wellesley was the soldier.

9. Marathas: three wars and a divided confederacy

Chiefs: Peshwa—Poona; Scindia—Gwalior; Holkar—Indore; Bhonsle—Nagpur; Gaekwad—Baroda. The Peshwa's nominal leadership coexisted with independent interests and armies.

First Anglo-Maratha War, 1775–82: Warren Hastings

  • Madhav Rao I died 1772 → Narayan Rao succeeded, murdered 1773 → Raghunath Rao claimed power → Nana Phadnavis and other chiefs supported infant Madhav Rao II.
  • Surat, 1775: Bombay authorities promised Raghunath Rao military support for Salsette/Bassein and revenue concessions. Calcutta Council opposed the arrangement; Colonel Upton negotiated Purandar, 1776: recognise infant Peshwa, withdraw support to Raghunath Rao; Company retained Salsette and specified revenue advantages.
  • Renewed intervention led to Talegaon/Wadgaon, January 1779: Maratha forces used terrain, supply denial and mobile attacks to force Bombay army's surrender. Mahadji Scindia was prominent. The source's 1776 date is wrong.
  • Wadgaon convention, 1779: demanded reversal of Company territorial gains; Hastings rejected it. Goddard captured Ahmedabad and Bassein, 1780; Popham took Gwalior, 1780, not 1781.
  • Salbai, 1782: settlement with Scindia; Madhav Rao II recognised, Raghunath Rao pensioned, most gains restored, English retained Salsette. Approximately twenty years of peace followed.
  • Evaluation: Marathas preserved independence in the short term; Company escaped a difficult two-front war and gained freedom to concentrate on Mysore. Maratha participation against Mysore varied: they joined the Third War alliance but were neutral in the Fourth.

Second Anglo-Maratha War, 1803–05: Lord Wellesley

  • Nana Phadnavis died 1800; Peshwa–Scindia–Holkar rivalry intensified. Vithoji Holkar killed 1801; Yashwant Rao Holkar defeated Peshwa/Scindia at Poona, 1802.
  • Peshwa Baji Rao II fled and signed Bassein, 31 December 1802: subsidiary force, territory yielding ₹26 lakh for upkeep, British resident, restrictions on diplomacy and European employment. Restored by British troops in 1803.
  • Significance: British foothold at Poona and strategic deployment across Deccan; nominal confederacy head became dependent. Other Maratha chiefs did not become automatically bound by his treaty.

Pindaris and Third Anglo-Maratha War, 1817–18: Lord Hastings

  • Pindaris: mobile plundering bands, associated with irregular military service; mixed social/religious backgrounds. Leaders Karim Khan, Wasil Muhammad, Chitu. Demobilisation and warfare enlarged their role; they were not a single caste or regular salaried army.
  • Raids: Mirzapur/Shahabad 1812; Nizam's territories 1815; Northern Circars 1816. Preparations in 1816 led to the main suppression campaign 1817–18, overlapping the Maratha war.
  • Karim Khan surrendered and received an estate; Wasil Muhammad died in captivity; Chitu's reported death by tiger belongs to later accounts. British pressure for anti-Pindari cooperation also tightened control over Maratha chiefs.
  • Scindia's Gwalior treaty, 1817, constrained him; he stayed out of the principal anti-British fighting. Do not list him as a leading battlefield opponent alongside the Peshwa.

Why Marathas lost: confederate rivalries, leadership instability, financial/logistical weakness, uneven coordination and Company diplomacy. Some Maratha armies had modern European-trained infantry/artillery: defeat cannot be explained as “old weapons versus modern weapons” alone. 1818 established Company paramountcy over much of India; Punjab/Sindh and several other regions were not yet annexed.

10. Sindh: commerce, frontier strategy and annexation

  • Indus navigation and access to Afghanistan/Persia attracted Company interest. Initial Thatta factory, 1758–75, belonged to the Kalhora phase; later Talpur Amirs, of Baluch origin, ruled Sindh.
  • Tilsit, 1807, heightened British fears of Franco-Russian cooperation. Minto's missions: Metcalfe—Lahore; Elphinstone—Kabul; Malcolm—Persia; Hankey Smith—Sindh. Do not treat an invasion of India as an executed treaty programme.

The famous “Peccavi / I have sinned” is a satirical pun later associated with Napier, not a verified telegram he sent.

11. Punjab: buffer state to annexation

  • Ranjit Singh built a strong Sikh state and balanced frontier diplomacy; Bentinck met him at Ropar, 1831. Indus/Sutlej commercial arrangements followed in the early 1830s.
  • Tripartite Treaty, 1838: Company, Ranjit Singh and Shah Shuja, in the context of the Afghan intervention.
  • Ranjit's death 1839 led to court/army struggles. Kharak Singh and Nau Nihal Singh died 1840; Sher Singh assassinated 1843; causes of the first two deaths are contested, not all simply proven murders.
  • Child Duleep/Dalip Singh succeeded 1843, with Rani Jindan as regent. Wazir Hira Singh killed 1844; Jawahar Singh killed 1845. Khalsa army influence grew as British frontier preparations increased mistrust.

  • Lahore treaty, March 1846: Jalandhar Doab ceded, ₹1.5 crore indemnity imposed, army reduced, Duleep retained under British influence; Henry Lawrence became Resident.
  • Inability to pay the full indemnity led to cession of hill territories. Separate Amritsar treaty, March 1846: Kashmir transferred to Gulab Singh for ₹75 lakh, not ₹50 lakh.
  • Bhairowal, December 1846: regency council of eight Sikh chiefs under British Resident; Jindan excluded from regency; occupation expenses charged to Punjab. Effective British control increased without immediate annexation.

Second Anglo-Sikh War, 1848–49: Dalhousie / Gough

  • Multan uprising under Mulraj, with broader resistance involving Chattar Singh Attariwala and Sher Singh, developed into war. Resentment of British interference and Jindan's treatment contributed.
  • Ramnagar, November 1848 → Chillianwala, January 1849: stiff resistance and heavy losses; Gujrat, February 1849: Gough's decisive victory; surrender followed.
  • 29 March 1849: Punjab annexed under Dalhousie. Duleep pensioned; went to Britain later, 1854, not immediately with Jindan in 1849.
  • Board of Administration: Henry Lawrence, John Lawrence, Charles Mansel. 1853: Board abolished; John Lawrence became Chief Commissioner. John Lawrence briefly became the first Lieutenant-Governor in January 1859; Robert Montgomery succeeded him in February 1859.
  • Mains argument: succession instability mattered, but so did the north-west frontier strategy, unequal treaties, Company intervention and Dalhousie's annexation policy. Heavy British casualties disprove an effortless or inevitable conquest.

Subsidiary Alliance: terms and consequences

  • Permanent Company troops maintained by the protected ruler; Resident stationed at court. War, peace and external alliances required British approval; employment of other Europeans restricted.
  • Nominal promise of internal non-interference coexisted with practical interference. Local military strength declined; subsidy arrears could trigger territorial cessions; Company deployed larger armies at Indian states' expense.
  • Important stages: Hyderabad 1798; Mysore 1799; Awadh 1801; Peshwa—Bassein 1802; Scindia arrangements 1803–04; Holkar/Rajput settlements 1817–18. Tanjore's 1799 arrangement transferred administration; avoid treating every treaty as identical.

Lapse: high-yield dates

  • Satara 1848; Jaitpur and Sambalpur 1849; Jhansi and Nagpur 1854. Their rulers' deaths/adoption disputes may precede the final annexation year.
  • Awadh 1856 = alleged misgovernment, not absence of an heir. Punjab 1849 = military conquest, not lapse. Sindh 1843 = Napier's conquest.
  • Annexations disrupted courts, armies, pensions, patronage and revenue arrangements, contributing to grievances before 1857.

13. Why Company power expanded

  • Political: regional rivalries/succession disputes; Company isolated opponents through treaties, Residents and alliances. Avoid judging eighteenth-century rulers by a fully developed modern national identity.
  • Military: disciplined Indian sepoy forces, regular pay, training, artillery, naval support and coordinated command; Indian opponents also possessed sophisticated forces and won significant engagements.
  • Fiscal and logistical: commercial credit, bankers, coastal supply bases, Bengal revenue, and integration of civil/military administration sustained campaigns.
  • Leadership and diplomacy: Clive, Hastings, Cornwallis, Wellesley, Coote and Lake exploited opportunities; Hyder, Tipu, Nana Phadnavis, Scindia and Holkar offered substantial resistance.
  • Global context: British shipping, finance and state support reinforced Company power. Industrial Revolution began in eighteenth-century Britain, not the sixteenth century; early victories cannot all be attributed to industrial weapons.
  • Assessment: trade, revenue, war and diplomacy reinforced one another. Conquest was uneven and contingent, with Indian soldiers, financiers and allies central to the outcome.

2015 History Optional: Punjab, about 150 words

Question: Critically examine: “Annexation of Punjab was part of a broad north-west frontier policy set in motion after the exit of Maharaja Ranjit Singh.”

Answer plan: define strategic concern (Afghanistan, routes, buffer) → Ranjit's death and court instability → British frontier mobilisation and First War → Lahore/Bhairowal made protection into intervention → Multan rising, Second War and annexation → evaluate strategy alongside local conflict, Company agency and annexationism. Avoid presenting inevitable conquest as the only possible outcome.

2014 History Optional: Plassey, about 150 words

Question: Critically examine: “Plassey did not complete the British conquest of India. Had the English been convincingly defeated in any subsequent battle in India, then the battle of Plassey would have remained as a minor episode in the history of India.”

Answer plan: Plassey secured a dependent Nawab, resources and foothold → Buxar/diwani consolidated regional power → Mysore and Maratha wars required further victories; Sikh resistance continued into 1849 → recognise Bengal finance as a durable advantage while explaining that one victory did not predetermine every later result.

Practice recall

  • War → treaty: Carnatic Aix-la-Chapelle / Pondicherry / Paris; Mysore Madras / Mangalore / Seringapatam / 1799 defeat; First Maratha Salbai; Second Maratha Deogaon, Surji-Arjungaon, Rajpurghat.
  • Governor-General: Clive—Dual Government; Warren Hastings—First Maratha/Second Mysore; Cornwallis—Third Mysore; Wellesley—Fourth Mysore/Second Maratha; Lord Hastings—Third Maratha; Ellenborough—Sindh; Hardinge—First Sikh; Dalhousie—Second Sikh.
  • Early successes, limited gains: Hyder's 1769 treaty did not ensure British help; Marathas' 1779 success did not prevent later Company recovery. Explain coalition breakdown, uneven resources, renewed diplomacy and changed strategic circumstances.
  • Sequence by beginning: Carnatic wars 1746 → Plassey 1757 → First Mysore 1767 → First Maratha 1775. Carnatic wars overlapped Plassey: do not imply all ended before 1757.
