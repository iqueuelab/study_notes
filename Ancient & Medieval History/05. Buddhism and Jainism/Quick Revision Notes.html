Buddhism and Jainism

1. Why new religious movements emerged

Mid-first millennium BCE: Buddhism, Jainism and other shramana traditions grew amid the mahajanapadas and the second urbanisation, c.600–300 BCE. They challenged Vedic ritual authority and offered paths of ethical conduct and liberation. They were independent traditions, not simply branches of a single reform organisation.

Urban centres to recognise: Rajagriha, Shravasti, Varanasi, Vaishali, Champa, Kaushambi, Ujjaini and, later, Pataliputra. Do not assume all were equally developed throughout 600–300 BCE. Confucius and Greek thinkers provide a wider background of inquiry; Zoroaster's dates are disputed, so do not put all these teachers in one securely dated sixth-century cohort.

2. Buddha: life, places and symbols

Buddha = awakened one; Siddhartha Gautama = Shakyamuni / Tathagata. Traditional accounts combine history with sacred biography. 563–483 BCE is the conventional chronology used in the chapter; other chronologies place his life/death later. Retain the sequence without treating these exact years as settled archaeological facts.

3. Buddhist teaching: the shortest complete map

Three jewels: distinguish from the Jaina three jewels

Buddha = teacher/awakened one → Dhamma = teaching/path → Sangha = community of practitioners, especially the monastic order. Lay followers are upasakas and upasikas; monks and nuns are bhikkhus and bhikkhunis.

Four Noble Truths

Eightfold Path: remember 2 + 3 + 3

TrainingRight factorsPractical meaning
Wisdom / panna-prajnaView (samma-ditthi); intention/thought (samma-sankappa)Understand reality; cultivate renunciation, goodwill and harmlessness

Middle Path: avoids both sensual indulgence and extreme self-mortification. It is not indifference between ethical and unethical conduct. The first sermon connects the Middle Way, Four Truths and Eightfold Path. Primary discourse, SN 56.11.

Five precepts and renunciation

Abstain from killing → taking what is not given → sexual misconduct → false speech → intoxicants causing heedlessness. Lay sexual ethics concern misconduct; monastic discipline requires celibacy. Renunciants also follow rules concerning eating after noon, entertainment/adornment, luxurious beds and money; the complete Vinaya is much larger than “five plus three” rules.

Karma, self and liberation

  • Anicca/anitya: impermanence. Anatta/anatman: no permanent, independent self. Buddhism accepts karma and rebirth without a transmigrating eternal atman.
  • Karma is intentional action, not merely priestly ritual; ethical intention, speech and bodily action have consequences.
  • Nirvana: extinguishing greed/craving, hatred and delusion; achievable during life. Not physical death, annihilation of an eternal soul, or a temporary heavenly holiday. Final passing of the liberated is parinirvana.
  • Buddhism does not ground liberation in a creator God, but traditional cosmologies contain devas, heavens, hells, hungry ghosts and other rebirth realms. The source table's “no heaven or hell” claim is wrong. Canonical examples, Itivuttaka.
  • The ten realms sequence in the chapter—Buddha, bodhisattva, pratyekabuddha, shravaka, devas, humans, asuras, animals, pretas and hell beings—is a later classificatory scheme, not the one universal early Buddhist cosmology. The last are hell beings, not a social class of “depraved men”.
  • Ahimsa is central; early monastic acceptance of certain donated meat means all Buddhists were not uniformly vegetarian. Rules differ by tradition; this does not license killing specifically to feed monks.
  • Teachings as a raft: use the path to cross suffering; do not turn attachment to doctrine into another obstacle. Upaya = skilful means, especially developed in Mahayana thought.

4. Sangha, women and social limits

The Sangha spread teaching through itinerant preaching, rainy-season residence, collective discipline and lay patronage. It offered spiritual recognition beyond inherited varna; Upali, traditionally a barber, stands beside Brahmana disciples Sariputta, Mahamoggallana and Mahakassapa and Kshatriya figures Ananda and Aniruddha. Chunda, associated with the final meal, is traditionally a smith.

  • Mahaprajapati Gautami: first nun in the traditional account; Ananda intercedes for women's admission. Nuns such as Khema, Uppalavanna/Utpalavarna, Dhammadinna, Sumedha and Sumangalamata illustrate women's religious agency; Therigatha preserves elder nuns' verses.
  • Ambapali/Amrapali of Vaishali, Addhakasi of Kasi and Padmavati of Ujjaini illustrate inclusion of women from socially stigmatised backgrounds in Buddhist narratives.
  • Traditional Vinaya accounts impose eight garudhammas: seniority of monks over nuns; residence near monks during rains; fortnightly instruction; joint post-rains review; joint procedure for serious offences; two-year training before full ordination; no abuse of monks; asymmetric admonition. These show institutional inequality despite equal spiritual capacity. Their textual formation/historicity is debated; predictions blaming women for shortening the religion's life are not verified historical causation.
  • Not a modern abolition programme: debts, slavery and military service could restrict admission without release/permission. Monastic communities depended on donations and generally recognised property, family obligations and political authority.
  • Buddhist criticism targets birth-based spiritual superiority; some texts still privilege Kshatriya rank. Avoid the claim that every social distinction vanished.

Buddhism and Brahmanical/Upanishadic traditions

Shared questions: karma, rebirth, discipline, knowledge and liberation; there were debates and borrowing as well as disagreements. Upanishads developed within Brahmanical traditions, not simply outside them as a united anti-Brahmana movement.

5. Buddhist literature and scholars

Canonical status varies by school. Texts were orally transmitted, edited and written down over time; the whole Tripitaka was not physically composed on leaves at the Buddha's death. Tipitaka is Pali; Tripitaka is Sanskrit: “three baskets”. The Pali canon belongs to Theravada; other Buddhist canons differ.

Early schools and later vehicles

  • Sthavira = elder; Thera is the Pali cognate. Theravada is a particular surviving lineage, not identical to every Sthavira school or all “Hinayana”. “Hinayana” is a polemical Mahayana label, not an appropriate neutral name for all early Buddhism.
  • Mahasanghika: early school group; Lokottaravada an offshoot. Its exalted conception of Buddha helped the background of later Mahayana, but Mahasanghika ≠ Mahayana.
  • Sarvastivada: dharmas exist across past, present and future in its distinctive analysis; an early Buddhist school, not simply a subdivision of present-day Theravada.
  • Sautrantika: stresses sutra authority over certain Abhidharma positions; Sammitiya: early Buddhist school. Both are Buddhist, not Jaina.

Two truths—conventional and ultimate—are important to Mahayana philosophy and Vajrayana, not exclusive to the latter. Sandhya-bhasha = symbolic “twilight language”. Vajrayana is not reducible to acquiring magical power; Tara has peaceful and wrathful forms, not only fierce ones. Ashoka predates these later vehicle labels; Kanishka's patronage does not establish one uniform sect for his whole empire. Harsha later supported Buddhist institutions alongside other traditions.

Bodhisattvas and paramitas

Bodhisattva: one committed to awakening for the benefit of beings; Mahayana stresses compassionate assistance to all. Paramitas = perfections on this path: six commonly listed are generosity, ethical conduct, patience, energy, meditation and wisdom; an expanded ten adds skilful means, aspiration/resolution, power and knowledge. These lists vary between traditions.

7. Stupas, caves and monastic vocabulary

Stupa = mound/heap, developed from funerary/relic traditions; not exclusively Buddhist in origin. In Buddhism it can hold relics, commemorate sacred events or serve as a votive object. Ashokan patronage helped make it a major sacred monument.

Medhi = raised terrace/drum or circumambulatory platform, depending on the structure; vedika = railing. Brick cores, stone casing, plaster and sculpted elements differ by site and building phase. Chhatra is the umbrella; yashti is its supporting mast. NCERT structure and terminology.

8. Spread, contribution and decline of Buddhism

Why it spread

Accessible ethics + teaching in vernacular linguistic settings + organised Sangha + merchants/lay donors + rulers + trade/mission networks. The language of the preserved Pali canon should not be equated mechanically with the Buddha's exact spoken dialect. Sanskrit later became an important Buddhist language, alongside many others.

  • Royal/elite supporters included rulers of Magadha and Kosala, republican groups, Ashoka, later Kushans, Harsha and regional patrons. Monks/nuns and small donors also mattered; spread was not solely a royal programme.
  • Sri Lankan tradition credits Mahinda/Mahendra and Sanghamitta/Sanghamitra with major missions under Ashoka; Sona and Uttara—Suvarnabhumi, Mahadeva—Mahishamandala, Maharakkhita—Yona country. These are chronicle traditions, and exact modern boundaries of mission destinations are debated. The chapter confuses names in the Himalayan mission: Majjhima is associated with the Himalayan region; Dhammarakkhita with Aparantaka.
  • Monks, merchants and translators linked India, Sri Lanka, Central Asia, Southeast Asia and China. Kumarajiva reached Chang'an in 401 CE; Faxian/Fa-Hien and Xuanzang/Hiuen Tsang later travelled to India. Traditions of an initial first-century Chinese invitation name Kashyapa Matanga and Dharmaratna, not a secure itinerary for every similarly named monk.
  • The source's “Dharmadeva went to China in 1001 CE” is not used as a firm date: identification/chronology needs verification.
  • Contributions: ethical reflection, compassion, monastic education, manuscript/translation networks, stupas/caves/sculpture and donor inscriptions. Indian Buddhist World Heritage examples include Sanchi, Ajanta, Mahabodhi at Bodh Gaya and Nalanda; their histories are distinct, not all Ashokan constructions.

Why it declined in many Indian regions

A long, uneven process—not disappearance everywhere from one year. Patronage and trade patterns changed; some large endowed monasteries became vulnerable; devotional Brahmanical traditions competed, absorbed Buddhist figures and offered alternative networks; institutional concentration and regional politics mattered. Warfare and attacks on monasteries, especially in the late twelfth–early thirteenth centuries, intensified losses in parts of northern/eastern India.

Claims that Sanskrit, image worship, “corrupt monks” or any single religion alone caused decline are insufficient. Buddhism remained vigorous under some later rulers; Gupta rulers were not uniformly anti-Buddhist. Accounts of persecution by Pushyamitra, Mihirakula and Shashanka have different evidential strengths and should not be fused into one continuous empire-wide policy. Buddhist traditions survived in Himalayan and other communities and later revived; “completely extinct in India” is false.

9. Mahavira and the Jaina tradition

Jina = conqueror of passions; Tirthankara = ford-maker who teaches a path across rebirth. Jainism recognises 24 Tirthankaras in each half-cycle. Mahavira is the 24th, not the first founder of an entirely new tradition. Historicity is firmer for the last teachers than for the whole sacred lineage.

Mahavira: traditional life sequence

  • 599–527 BCE is the traditional chronology used here; other historical chronologies differ. Broadly a contemporary of Buddha; do not infer exact synchronisms from every traditional date.
  • Born at Kundagrama near Vaishali, in the Jnatrika/Naya Kshatriya clan; father Siddhartha, mother Trishala. Linked by tradition to the Lichchhavis and Chetaka, with varying kinship accounts. Vaishali was a centre of the Vajji confederacy; do not call it simply the capital of monarchical Videha.
  • Shvetambara tradition: wife Yashoda, daughter Priyadarshana; renunciation at 30 after a householder's life. Digambara tradition: unmarried; other details also differ.
  • After 12 years of austerity, attained kevalajnana, traditionally aged 42, beside Rijupalika/Rijubalika, near Jrimbhikagrama, associated with a householder Samaga.
  • Final liberation, traditionally aged 72, at Pavapuri/Pava near present-day Patna. Keep this separate from the Buddhist sacred geography; similarly named places create traps.
  • Ganadharas: chief disciples/transmitters of the teaching. Do not assume every disciple was already omniscient throughout his entire discipleship.

10. Jaina doctrines: soul, karma and many-sided truth

Jiva = soul/conscious living substance. Jainism accepts enduring individual souls and no creator of the universe. The world operates through its own eternal constituents/order; universal soul is not its creator. Gods are recognised as beings within samsara, below the spiritually liberated Jinas in the relevant hierarchy.

Three jewels / Triratna

Samyag-darshana = right faith/perception → samyag-jnana = right knowledge → samyag-charitra = right conduct. These are not the Buddhist Buddha–Dhamma–Sangha triad.

Mahavratas: full ascetic vows. Anuvratas: limited householders' vows, not a Buddhist sect. Sallekhana: religious discipline of gradual fasting and detachment approaching death under prescribed conditions; not a recommendation that every follower must fast to death.

Life and cosmology

  • Time cycles between ascending utsarpini and descending avasarpini, each with six aras/stages; the present traditional half-cycle is descending. No supported historical year is assigned to mythic cycles.
  • Living beings include gods, humans, hell beings and animals/plants. Ekendriya = one-sensed life; nigoda = extremely minute forms of life in Jaina doctrine. Earth-, water-, fire-, air- and plant-bodied living beings explain the exceptionally wide scope of ahimsa. Not a modern scientific assertion that every physical atom is an individual soul.
  • Dravya = substance; guna = quality; paryaya = mode. Six substances commonly counted: jiva, pudgala/matter, dharma/motion-medium, adharma/rest-medium, akasha/space and kala/time. Here dharma/adharma do not mean merit/sin.
  • Additional terms: pudgala—matter/atomic aggregates; chaitanya—consciousness; virya—energy; mohaniya—deluding karma; gunasthanas—stages of purification; avadhijnana—limited supersensory knowledge, not omniscience; arhat/arihant—embodied conqueror with perfect knowledge; siddha—fully liberated; basadi—Jaina temple/establishment, especially Karnataka; eva simply means “indeed”, not a separate substance.
  • Strict ahimsa encouraged vegetarianism and avoidance of livelihoods causing injury. Trade became important, but all Jains were not prohibited from every form of cultivation or confined to commerce in actual history; Jaina patrons included rulers and diverse communities.

11. Jaina literature, councils and spread

Texts and transmission

Agama / Siddhanta = sacred teaching/textual tradition; the early material was orally transmitted, especially in Ardhamagadhi Prakrit. Later authors used other Prakrits, Sanskrit, Apabhramsha, Kannada, Tamil and more languages. The two principal traditions do not recognise an identical surviving canon.

  • Angas: traditionally 12, but the twelfth, Drishtivada, is lost; 11 survive in the Shvetambara canon. Acharanga/Acaranga and Sutrakritanga are major early texts. Do not claim 12 complete surviving Angas. Jainpedia—Angas.
  • Purvas = ancient/previous teachings; shruta-kevalin = master of the scriptural tradition, distinct from the kevalin's direct omniscience. Angabahya = texts outside the Angas.
  • Conventional Shvetambara categories: 12 Upangas, 10 Prakirnakas (miscellanies), 6 Chedasutras (discipline), 4 Mulasutras (basic instruction), plus Nandi and Anuyogadvara; accepted collections differ among subtraditions.
  • Kalpasutra is an important Shvetambara text with Tirthankara lives and monastic material. Tattvarthasutra, associated with Umasvati/Umasvami, is a systematic doctrinal work respected across the main traditions, with variant versions.
  • Digambaras hold the original Angas lost and preserve a different scriptural corpus; this is not absence of literature.
  • Divya-dhvani / samavasarana: teaching of an omniscient Jina in a universal preaching assembly; Digambara and Shvetambara accounts differ over the nature of the sound/language.

Bhadrabahu–southern migration and Sthulabhadra–Magadha are associated in later traditions with a twelve-year famine and emerging differences. Chandragupta Maurya's Jaina renunciation at Shravanabelagola is a later traditional association. Do not treat a single famine as a fully documented instantaneous split; Digambara/Shvetambara identities crystallised gradually over the early centuries CE.

Spread: Gangetic beginnings → Karnataka and other southern regions, Tamil-Brahmi Jaina associations by the last centuries BCE, Kalinga, and western/central centres in Gujarat, Rajasthan and Malwa. Kharavela of Kalinga, conventionally first century BCE, patronised the Jaina tradition; he was not a fourth-century BCE king. Mercantile networks, householders, temples, libraries, literary activity and regional patrons helped continuity. Jainism received less extensive early transregional royal patronage than Buddhism, but “no royal patronage” is false.

Ajivikas

  • Makkhali Gosala, traditionally once a companion of Mahavira; a distinct shramana movement. Traditions speak of many competing teachings—“62” is a literary enumeration, not a verified census.
  • Niyati = destiny/determinism: liberation unfolds through an ordained sequence of rebirths; human effort cannot change that sequence in the account attributed to them. Yet they practised severe austerities.
  • Their own writings are largely lost; much doctrine survives through rival Buddhist/Jaina accounts, requiring caution about hostile characterisations.
  • Mauryan patronage, especially third century BCE cave dedications at Barabar/Nagarjuni, provides important evidence; inscriptions of Ashoka and Dasharatha matter more than polemical anecdotes.
  • Later southern references continue into medieval times, sometimes cited as late as the fourteenth century CE; identification/continuity is debated. Do not mistake Ajivikas for Ajita Kesakambalin's materialism.

Mains: build analytical answers

  • 2016 GS1—Stupa art: introduce monument as sacred/narrative space → Jatakas and Buddha-life symbols → folk motifs such as trees, animals and fertility figures → compassion, generosity, renunciation and the Dhamma → donors and circumambulation connect belief with social participation. Use Sanchi/Bharhut/Amaravati; do not read every motif as only Buddhist or claim all art was royal.
  • 2015 History optional—post-Vedic economy and religions: explain surplus/iron/cattle → urban crafts, coins and merchants → status conflict/patronage → ethical/renouncer answers; qualify economic determinism using intellectual debates, personalities and differing doctrines.
  • 2014 History optional—Buddha today: compassion, non-harm, responsible speech/livelihood, moderation and mindfulness address violence, exclusion and consumption; critically add structural injustice, public policy and material deprivation—individual calm alone cannot resolve them.
  • 2014 GS1—Taxila and Nalanda: Taxila was an ancient learning centre at a strategic northwestern crossroads with renowned teachers and many disciplines; teaching often organised around individual teachers. Nalanda developed a more integrated residential monastic institution. Avoid projecting the modern degree-granting university onto either without qualification.
  • 2011 History optional—Jaina tenets and humanity: ahimsa → peaceful coexistence/ecological care; anekantavada/syadavada → dialogue and qualified judgement; aparigraha → restraint; satya/asteya → integrity; end with practical limits and the distinction between ascetic and lay vows.
