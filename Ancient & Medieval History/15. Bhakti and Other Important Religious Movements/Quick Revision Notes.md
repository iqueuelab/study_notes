# Bhakti and Other Important Religious Movements

## 1. Bhakti: the central idea

**Bhakti = devoted love for the Divine.** Personal devotion, grace and surrender offered accessible ways to seek liberation; expressions differed across regions, communities and philosophical schools.

- Earlier devotional strands appear in Sanskrit and other traditions; image worship, Puranic religion and temple patronage expanded over time. Gupta-period patronage was important, but did not invent all Vishnu worship.
- Tamil poet-saints made devotion a powerful public tradition from about the **sixth century CE**. Northern and regional movements flourished particularly during the **twelfth–seventeenth centuries**.
- Buddhism/Jainism formed part of the earlier religious setting; **Mahayana did not originate with the Buddha in the sixth century BCE**. Avalokiteshvara is a bodhisattva, not simply a name for the Buddha's “gracious form”.
- **Common themes, not universal rules:** heartfelt devotion over empty formalism; spiritual guidance; singing and remembrance; vernacular expression; dignity and fellowship.
- Not all Bhakti saints rejected images, temples, Vedic authority, caste institutions or pilgrimage. **Religious accessibility did not automatically abolish social inequality.**

### Why did movements spread?

| Factor | Contribution |
|---|---|
| Religious resources | Tamil hymns, Vaishnava/Shaiva traditions, Puranas, Vedanta and Nath practices offered varied vocabularies. |
| Social criticism | Some saints challenged caste exclusion, ritual authority and elite privilege. |
| Economic change | Growing towns, artisanal activity and mobility in the thirteenth–fourteenth centuries widened audiences; this is one historical explanation, not a single proven cause. |
| Cultural exchange | Bhakti and Sufi communities interacted in a shared social world; affinities do not mean Bhakti began solely as an Islamic influence. |
| Language and performance | Poetry, oral transmission, gatherings and music carried ideas beyond Sanskrit-trained elites. |
| Institutions and patronage | Temples, mathas, panths, congregations and royal/local support helped preserve and spread teachings. |

## 2. South India: Alvars and Nayanars

| Tradition | Devotion / people | Texts and significance |
|---|---|---|
| **12 Alvars** | Vishnu; Periyalvar, **Andal**, Nammalvar, Tondaradippodi and others. | **Nalayira Divya Prabandham**: approximately 4,000 Tamil hymns, assembled by about the tenth century; revered as the **Tamil Veda**. |
| **63 Nayanars** | Shiva; Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar, **Karaikkal Ammaiyar** and others. | **Tevaram**: hymns of Appar, Sambandar and Sundarar; **Tiruvachakam**: associated with Manikkavachakar in the wider Tamil Shaiva canon. |

- Flourished under **Pallavas, Pandyas and Cholas**; temples and rulers supported devotional activity.
- Saints came from **diverse social backgrounds**, including Brahmins, artisans and marginalised communities—not uniformly from one caste group.
- **Andal:** woman Alvar, intense Vishnu devotion; **Karaikkal Ammaiyar:** woman Shaiva saint, ascetic imagery. **Manikkavachakar:** divine grace and surrender in Tamil poetry.
- Some hymns polemicised against Jains/Buddhists; competition for patronage mattered. Calling those religions simply “fatalistic” misrepresents their doctrines.
- **Limit:** spiritual inclusion and participation coexisted with temples, ritual authority and continuing caste disabilities. Neither “complete social revolution” nor “no challenge at all” captures every tradition.

## 3. Saguna and Nirguna: compare carefully

| Dimension | **Saguna** | **Nirguna** |
|---|---|---|
| Divine conception | God with attributes/form; often Rama, Krishna or Shiva. | Divine beyond material form and attributes. |
| Examples | Tulsidas, Surdas, Mirabai, Chaitanya. | Kabir and Guru Nanak are commonly placed in this comparative category. |
| Worship | Personal deity, loving service, hymns; images/temples may matter. | Remembrance of the Name, inward experience and criticism of external formalism. |
| Social positions | Varied: devotional inclusion could coexist with hierarchy. | Often sharper criticism of caste distinctions and priestly claims; historical outcomes still varied. |

**Shared:** devotion, guidance, accessible poetry, ethical concerns and fellowship. **Monotheism is not exclusive to Nirguna**, and “Saguna = every saint defended caste” is too sweeping. Sikhism developed a distinct religious tradition; the comparison does not reduce it to a Hindu sect.

## 4. Philosophers: doctrine, relation and route

| Thinker / conventional dates | School | Main relation / path |
|---|---|---|
| **Shankara, c.788–820** | **Advaita**: non-dualism. | Brahman is ultimate reality; ignorance produces mistaken identification. Liberating knowledge is central. The empirical world is not absolutely independent; “unreal” does not mean ordinary experience never occurs. |
| **Ramanuja, traditional 1017–1137** | **Vishishtadvaita**: qualified non-dualism. | Brahman/Narayana is personal; souls and matter are real, dependent modes, often explained through **body–soul** relation. Bhakti, grace and **prapatti** (surrender). |
| **Madhva, traditional 1238–1317** | **Dvaita**: dualism. | Vishnu, individual souls and matter are really distinct; souls/world depend on God. Difference does **not** mean “no relationship whatsoever”. |
| **Nimbarka, dating disputed** | **Dvaitadvaita / bhedabheda**. | Difference and non-difference; souls/world distinct yet dependent on Brahman. Radha–Krishna devotion and grace. Not an Akbar contemporary. |
| **Vallabhacharya, 1479–1531** | **Shuddhadvaita**: pure non-dualism. | World is a real manifestation of Brahman; **Pushtimarg**, the path of grace, emphasises loving **seva** to Krishna/Shrinathji. Not a doctrine of two independent ultimate realities. |

**Mnemonic:** Shankara—one; Ramanuja—qualified unity; Madhva—real difference; Nimbarka—difference/non-difference; Vallabha—pure unity and grace.

### Texts, institutions and places

- **Shankara:** Kaladi, Kerala; commentaries on **Brahma Sutras, principal Upanishads and Bhagavad Gita**; *Upadeshasahasri*. *Vivekachudamani* and *Bhaja Govindam* are traditionally attributed; authorship of some attributed works is debated. Four mathas associated by tradition: **Sringeri, Dwarka, Puri and Jyotirmath** in the Badrinath region.
- **Advaita analogy:** a rope mistaken for a snake → knowledge removes the error; it does not physically destroy a real snake.
- **Ramanuja:** Sriperumbudur, Tamil region; Alvar/Sri Vaishnava background; **Sri Bhashya, Vedanta Dipa, Gita Bhashya, Vedantasara**. Devotional access widened, while Vedic/social distinctions did not simply vanish.
- **Madhva:** Karnataka/Udupi; associated with **Brahma sampradaya** in traditional Vaishnava classification.
- **Nimbarka:** Radha–Krishna; **Sanaka/Kumara sampradaya**. Sun–ray analogy recalls relatedness and distinction; biography/chronology remain uncertain.
- **Vallabha:** traditionally born at **Champaranya near Raipur**, not Banaras; Telugu family background, later activity in north/west India. Associated with **Rudra sampradaya**, whose earlier lineage is linked to Vishnusvamin; do not make Vallabha its original founder. [Chhattisgarh Tourism](https://tourism.cgstate.gov.in/destinations/Raipur/Champaranya).

## 5. Regional saints: names, works and exam cues

### Karnataka, Assam and eastern India

| Saint | Recall |
|---|---|
| **Basavanna, twelfth century** | Karnataka; minister associated with Kalachuri **Bijjala**, ruling c.1157–1167. Kannada **vachanas**; Lingayat/Virashaiva reform context; opposition to caste exclusion and gender barriers; dignity of work and spiritual experience. |
| **Basavanna's community** | **Anubhava Mantapa**: remembered forum of Sharanas for discussion. “First parliament” is a modern analogy, not proof of a modern elected legislature. **Arivu–Achara–Anubhava:** knowledge, conduct, experience. |
| **Lingayat practice** | Personal **ishtalinga**, Shiva devotion; challenges to pollution/rebirth hierarchy; widow remarriage and post-puberty marriage supported in the reform tradition; burial rather than normative Brahmanical cremation. Practices/identities vary. |
| **Vidyapati, c.1352–1448** | Maithili poet; Shiva devotion and the **Ugna** tradition; also celebrated for Radha–Krishna love poetry. |
| **Shankaradeva, 1449–1568** | Assam/Brahmaputra valley and Koch region; Vishnu–Krishna devotion, **Ekasarana/Mahapurushiya** tradition; Assamese poetry and drama; **satras** and **namghars**. Satras = monastic institutions; namghars = congregational prayer/recitation halls. |
| **Jayadeva, twelfth century** | *Gita Govinda*: Radha–Krishna love; important devotional/literary background to eastern Vaishnavism. |
| **Chaitanya, 1486–1533** | Bengal–Odisha; Krishna devotion, ecstatic **sankirtan**, love and accessible collective worship; Haridas illustrates participation across inherited religious boundaries. |
| **Chaitanya tradition** | Six Goswamis of Vrindavan systematised devotional traditions; **Krishnadas Kaviraj—Chaitanya Charitamrita**. Associated with **achintya-bhedabheda** in Gaudiya tradition, not simply identical to Madhva's Dvaita. Social hierarchies persisted despite devotional inclusion. |
| **Narsinh Mehta, c.1414–1481** | Gujarat; Radha–Krishna songs; **Vaishnava Jan To** associated with him, later cherished by Gandhi; compassion is the moral cue. |
| **Annamacharya, 1408–1503** | Telugu poet/composer; **Venkateshwara of Tirumala**, sankirtanas; dignity beyond caste exclusion. |
| **Tyagaraja, 1767–1847** | Carnatic composer, primarily Telugu **Rama** devotion; **Pancharatna Kritis**, associated with Nata, Gaula, Arabhi, Varali and Sri ragas. Music as devotion; not Annamacharya's contemporary. |

**Carnatic Trinity:** **Tyagaraja, Muthuswami Dikshitar, Syama Sastri**. The chapter's map also names **Purandaradasa** in the Karnataka devotional/music tradition. Do not confuse the Maharashtra saint Samarth Ramdas with the Telugu composer **Bhadrachala Ramadasu**.

### Maharashtra: Vitthala and the Varkari tradition

**Centre:** **Pandharpur**, Vitthala/Vithoba, associated with Krishna; Marathi poetry, kirtan and pilgrimage. Bhagavata and Nath traditions contributed to its religious environment.

| Saint / conventional dates | Works and contribution |
|---|---|
| **Jnaneshwar/Dnyaneshwar, 1275–1296** | **Jnaneshwari**, Marathi Bhagavad Gita commentary; *Amritanubhava*, *Haripath*, abhang tradition; accessible spiritual teaching. |
| **Namdev, c.1270–1350** | Marathi **abhangs**, Vitthala devotion, community singing; hymns in **Guru Granth Sahib**. Tailor association in tradition; conversion/bandit stories are hagiography. |
| **Eknath, c.1533–1599** | Marathi religious literature; *Bhavarth Ramayan*, **bharuds**, recension of *Jnaneshwari*. Householder devotion; criticism of social exclusion. |
| **Tukaram, c.1608–1649/50** | Marathi **abhangs**, preserved in the *Gatha*; Vitthala, kirtan, compassion, forgiveness and criticism of hypocrisy. An abhanga is not simply a Hindi **doha**. He lived after Eknath's death. |
| **Samarth Ramdas, 1608–1681** | Rama/Hanuman devotion; **Dasbodh, Manache Shlok, Karunashtakas**; spiritual discipline and action. His precise relationship with Shivaji is debated; do not make all later guru-legends settled fact. |

- **Varkari:** Vitthala-centred devotional/pilgrimage tradition; **Dharkari** is a textbook label linked to Ramdas's active/heroic devotional emphasis. “Emotional versus rational” is a schematic contrast, not a fixed character judgement.
- Social breadth is illustrated by saints associated with diverse occupations: **Sena—barber; Savata—gardener; Gora—potter; Narhari—goldsmith; Chokhamela—marginalised Mahar background; Janabai—domestic work; Kanhopatra—performer**. They were not all necessarily Namdev's simultaneous companions.
- **Karmamela**, Chokhamela's son: devotional poetry confronts humiliation and exclusion. Tukaram's poetry joins devotion with solidarity with the distressed; spirituality did not erase the continuing reality of caste.

### Northern devotional currents

| Saint / period | Key teachings and texts |
|---|---|
| **Ramananda, broadly fourteenth–fifteenth centuries** | North Indian Rama–Sita devotion; vernacular teaching; Ramanandi tradition; often presented as a link with southern Sri Vaishnavism. Exact dates and disciple lists are contested; not Ramanuja's direct contemporary disciple. |
| **Kabir, fifteenth–early sixteenth century** | Weaver-associated Banaras tradition; formless Divine, **Ram/Rahim** beyond sectarian claims; criticised caste, untouchability, empty ritual and hypocrisy; inward sincerity over book learning alone. |
| **Kabir's corpus** | **Bijak**, verses in Guru Granth Sahib and other collections; **ulatbansi** = paradoxical/upside-down expression; later **Kabir Panth**. Birth and initiation stories are traditions, not securely documented biography. |
| **Ravidas/Raidas, fifteenth–sixteenth centuries** | Leather-working association; devotion and dignity beyond caste; hymns in Guru Granth Sahib. Some traditions link him to Mirabai. |
| **Dadu Dayal, 1544–1603** | Gujarat origins in tradition; Rajasthan activity; Nirguna devotion, **nipakh** (non-sectarian outlook); **Dadu Panth** and **thambas**. **Kabir cannot have been his disciple:** Kabir belonged to an earlier generation. |
| **Tulsidas, sixteenth–early seventeenth centuries** | **Ramcharitmanas in Awadhi**; Rama devotion; spiritual accessibility alongside inherited social ideals. |
| **Surdas, fifteenth–sixteenth centuries** | Braj Krishna poetry: **Sursagar, Sursaravali, Sahitya Lahari**; associated with the Vallabha tradition. Exact life dates vary. |
| **Mirabai, fifteenth–sixteenth centuries** | Krishna as divine beloved; bhajans, Rajput/Mewar context, resistance to prescribed familial roles; biography and dates largely mediated through later traditions. |

Ramananda's traditional disciple lists include **Kabir, Ravidas, Sena, Sadhana (butcher), Dhanna (peasant), Pipa (Rajput ruler)** and others; **Guru Nanak is not securely established as his direct disciple**. Nabhadas/Tulsidas and Kabir are often used to illustrate different later tendencies, not a uniform organisation under one founder.

**Radha-Vallabha tradition:** **Hit Harivansh**, sixteenth century; do not memorise the chapter's unsupported 1585 founding date as settled.

## 6. Women, inclusion and limits

| Woman saint | Recall |
|---|---|
| **Andal** | Tamil Vishnu devotion; woman Alvar. |
| **Karaikkal Ammaiyar** | Tamil Shiva devotion and ascetic identity. |
| **Akka Mahadevi, twelfth century** | Karnataka vachanas; Shiva as **Chennamallikarjuna**; challenge to bodily/social conventions. |
| **Janabai, thirteenth–fourteenth centuries** | Maharashtra; Namdev household tradition; everyday labour and caste/gender barriers enter devotional poetry. |
| **Mirabai** | Krishna devotion asserts spiritual agency beyond expected royal/household roles. |
| **Bahinabai, seventeenth century** | Marathi devotional abhangs; tensions between household duties and spiritual life. Distinguish her from the later modern Marathi poet Bahinabai Chaudhari. |

- Poetry made domestic labour, family tension, absence and longing spiritually meaningful. Some renounced household roles; others practised devotion within them.
- Inclusion widened women's expressive space, but acceptance often required struggle in male-dominated institutions; the divine-husband idiom could both challenge and reuse patriarchal imagery.
- **Assessment:** new voices and spiritual dignity mattered; neither gender hierarchy nor caste exclusion disappeared uniformly.

## 7. Sufism: inward discipline within Islam

**Sufism** encompasses Islamic mystical/devotional traditions concerned with purifying the self, love, remembrance and nearness to God. **Suf = wool**, recalling early ascetic clothing; “purity” is an associated ideal, not the literal translation of that root.

- Early ascetic/mystical figures include **Rabia al-Adawiyya**, **Bayazid Bistami** and **Junayd**; early traditions developed from the eighth century, with major literature/institutions by the eleventh–twelfth centuries.
- **Ali Hujwiri**, eleventh century, Lahore: **Kashf al-Mahjub**, a Persian Sufi treatise.
- Spiritual training, repentance, prayer, charity, fasting and ethical conduct mattered. Love of God could be expressed through service to people; not every order held identical views on music, rulers or legal practice.
- **Fana** = effacement of the ego; **ishq** = intense divine love; **ruh** = spirit/soul; **qurb** = nearness. **Hulul** (indwelling/incarnation language) was controversial and is not a universally accepted Sufi doctrine.

### Essential glossary

| Term | Correct recall |
|---|---|
| **Silsila / tariqa** | Chain/lineage or order; tariqa = spiritual path/method. |
| **Pir / shaikh / murshid; murid** | Spiritual guide; disciple. **Wali** = saint/friend of God, not simply “teacher”. |
| **Khanqah** | Hospice/lodge and centre of communal spiritual life; not the pir himself. |
| **Dargah; ziyarat** | Saint's shrine/tomb; visitation/pilgrimage. |
| **Sama; qawwali; qaul** | Devotional audition/listening; musical performance tradition; a form of devotional utterance/song associated with Chishti performance. Practices were contested. |
| **Tauba; wara; zuhd** | Repentance; scrupulous avoidance of the doubtful; renunciation/ascetic detachment. |
| **Jud; faqr** | Generosity; spiritual poverty/dependence on God. |
| **Sabr; shukr** | Patience/endurance; gratitude. |
| **Khauf; tawakkul; rida** | Fear/awe; trust in God; contented acceptance of God's will. |
| **Ba-sharia; be-sharia** | Labels for conformity to prescribed law and its perceived rejection/transgression; not an adequate explanation of every Sufi's beliefs. |
| **Wahdat al-wujud** | Unity of being, especially associated with Ibn Arabi's tradition; interpretations differ. |

**Qalandars, Madaris, Malangs and Haidaris** are among ascetic groups discussed under the be-sharia category. Do not equate all Sufis with rejection of Islamic law.

## 8. Four major Sufi orders

| Order | People / centres | Distinctive tendencies |
|---|---|---|
| **Chishti** | **Muinuddin Chishti—Ajmer**; Qutbuddin Bakhtiyar Kaki—Delhi; **Baba Farid—Punjab**; Nizamuddin Auliya and Nasiruddin Chiragh-i Delhi. | Love, service, hospitality, simple living; many leading shaikhs kept distance from courts; sama and vernacular interaction prominent. |
| **Suhrawardi** | Abu Najib/Shihabuddin Suhrawardi in the order's West Asian history; **Bahauddin Zakariya—Multan**. | More acceptance of property, grants and political contacts; religious learning with spiritual discipline; Punjab/Sindh. |
| **Qadiri** | **Abd al-Qadir al-Jilani—Baghdad**; later South Asian networks include Nimatullah/Jilani traditions, **Miyan Mir**, **Mulla Shah Badakhshi**. | Diverse devotional networks; Dara Shikoh and Jahanara associated with Qadiri teachers. Do not date the order's original founding to Mughal India. |
| **Naqshbandi** | **Bahauddin Naqshband—Central Asia**; **Baqi Billah introduced the order in India**, later **Ahmad Sirhindi (1564–1624)**. | Silent remembrance, strong stress on sharia and criticism of religious innovations; Sirhindi opposed aspects of Akbar-era accommodation. Not every later member held identical political views. |

[IGNOU on Naqshbandi history](https://egyankosh.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/82886/3/Theme-4.pdf).

### Chishti names and centres

- **Muinuddin Chishti, d.1236:** **Gharib Nawaz**, Ajmer; later pilgrimage and royal patronage, especially Akbar. **Chisht is in present-day Afghanistan**, not a village in Ajmer. Date of settling at Ajmer varies in accounts; do not confuse later patronage with the saint's lifetime.
- **Baba Farid, c.1175–1265/66:** Fariduddin Ganj-i Shakar; hymns in Guru Granth Sahib; ascetic discipline and distance from power.
- **Nizamuddin Auliya, 1238–1325:** compassion and service; discipline, purity, fasting, devotion and guidance. Kept distance from courts; tensions with some Delhi sultans.
- **Amir Khusrau**, Amir Hasan Sijzi and Ziauddin Barani belonged to his intellectual/devotional milieu. Khusrau is closely associated with Chishti musical traditions; do not ascribe every later musical form to one unqualified invention.
- **Nasiruddin Chiragh-i Delhi:** disciple/successor in Nizamuddin's lineage.
- **Burhanuddin Gharib:** important **fourteenth-century** Chishti expansion into the Deccan.
- **Muhammad Gesu Daraz/Banda Nawaz:** **Gulbarga/Kalaburagi**, not Bijapur.
- Later Naqshbandi figures **Shah Waliullah** and **Mirza Mazhar Jan-i Janan** illustrate variation; two rigid “conservative/liberal” labels cannot describe their full thought.

### Impact and limits

Khanqah hospitality, service, shared shrine visitation, vernacular expression and music helped cultural exchange. Some Sufis influenced rulers; others avoided courtly dependence. **Contact did not erase doctrinal disagreement, political conflict or social hierarchy.** Bhakti–Sufi interactions contributed to shared literary/music cultures; Urdu's emergence had wider historical causes too.

## 9. Sikhism: teachings and institutions

### Guru Nanak, 1469–1539

- Born at **Talwandi/Nankana Sahib**, now Pakistan; **Kartarpur on the Ravi** became a major community centre. Do not transfer Kartarpur's river location automatically to Talwandi.
- One eternal, formless Creator; **Naam**, ethical conduct, honest livelihood, service and equality; rejected empty ritual and inherited religious superiority.
- **Naam japna—remember the Divine; kirat karna—earn honestly; vand chhakna—share.** Householder life can be spiritual.
- **Guru–Shabad–Sangat:** teacher, sacred word and congregation; **langar** expresses shared fellowship. Mardana accompanied hymns on the **rabab**.

### Ten Gurus: sequence and contributions

**Nanak's dates below are his lifetime; subsequent ranges are periods of Guruship, not birth–death dates.**

| Guru / period | Recall |
|---|---|
| **1. Nanak, 1469–1539 (life)** | Founder; Naam, equality, honest work and sharing; congregational life/langar. |
| **2. Angad, 1539–1552** | Earlier name **Bhai Lehna**; standardised/popularised **Gurmukhi**; education, langar and **mall akhara**. |
| **3. Amar Das, 1552–1574** | **22 manjis**, women preachers/piri tradition; strengthened langar; opposition to sati/purdah and support for widow remarriage. |
| **4. Ram Das, 1574–1581** | **Ramdaspur/Amritsar**, conventionally **1577**; four **Lavan** hymns associated with Anand Karaj. |
| **5. Arjan, 1581–1606** | **Adi Granth compiled/installed at Harmandir Sahib, 1604**; developed Sikh centres; executed under Jahangir in **1606**, in the context of religious/political tensions including Khusrau. |
| **6. Hargobind, 1606–1644** | **Miri–Piri**, temporal/spiritual authority symbolised by **two swords**; **Akal Takht, 1606**; armed defence; later Kiratpur centre. |
| **7. Har Rai, 1644–1661** | Maintained community institutions; associated with assistance to Dara Shikoh; Mughal relations became strained. |
| **8. Har Krishan, 1661–1664** | Child Guru; remembered for service during illness in Delhi; died of smallpox in **1664**. |
| **9. Tegh Bahadur, 1664–1675** | Travel/preaching in eastern India; executed in Delhi under Aurangzeb, **1675**; **Sis Ganj Sahib** commemorates the site. Defence of religious conscience central to Sikh remembrance. |
| **10. Gobind Singh, 1675–1708** | Born Patna; **Khalsa at Anandpur, 1699**; Panj Pyare, initiation and collective discipline; **Guru Granth Sahib as enduring Guru, 1708**; died at Nanded. |

- **Harmandir:** construction associated with Guru Arjan; tradition credits **Mian Mir** with laying the foundation. Land-acquisition and foundation narratives have variants; do not collapse town founding, shrine construction and installation of the Granth into one event. [SGPC history](https://new.sgpc.net/sri-harmandir-sahib/).
- **Tegh Bahadur's succession:** SGPC records recognition in **1664**, rather than treating the source's 1665 as the sole date. [SGPC](https://sgpc.net/ten-guru-sahibs/guru-tegh-bahadur-sahib/).
- **Banda Singh Bahadur was commissioned by Guru Gobind Singh**, not Guru Tegh Bahadur, in **1708**. [SGPC account](https://new.sgpc.net/ten-guru-sahibs/guru-gobind-singh-sahib/).
- Khalsa initiation: **khande-di-pahul**, Panj Pyare; **five Ks—kesh, kangha, kara, kachhera, kirpan**. Guru and initiates reciprocally participated in initiation; shared discipline expressed equality and responsibility.
- **Dasam Granth:** corpus traditionally associated with Guru Gobind Singh; compilation/authorship of all its contents is debated. Distinguish it from **Adi Granth/Guru Granth Sahib**.

## 10. Exam traps and answer frameworks

### Prelims recall

| Question cue | Correct association / caution |
|---|---|
| Ramanuja | Devotion/grace and surrender, not logical argument alone. |
| Nimbarka–Akbar; Kabir–Sirhindi | Both proposed contemporaneity/influence claims are false. |
| Tyagaraja vs Annamacharya | Primarily Rama vs Venkateshwara; lived centuries apart. |
| Bijak; Pushtimarg | **Kabir; Vallabhacharya**, not Dadu and Madhva. |
| Babur's takeover, **1526** | Guru Nanak was alive/preaching; Dadu and Tyagaraja were not. |
| Nagarjuna; anekantavada | Nagarjuna = Buddhist philosopher, not a medieval Bhakti proponent; anekantavada = Jainism. |
| Tukaram's Mughal contemporary | Jahangir among the supplied options; Shah Jahan also overlapped his life. Aurangzeb became emperor only in 1658. |
| Shankara → Ramanuja → Chaitanya | Correct relative order. |
| Sikh Guru/script | Angad = Gurmukhi; Arjan follows Ram Das; Gobind Singh commissions Banda. |
| Namdev–Kabir–Ravidas–Sena | Tailor–weaver–cobbler–barber in conventional question associations. |
| Prem Vatika | **Raskhan**. |
| Chishti order's name | Chisht, Afghanistan; not Ajmer. |

### Mains: build argument with examples and limits

- **2021 GS I:** nature and cultural contribution of Bhakti literature → devotional experience, vernacular/oral forms, social criticism, women, music and continuing traditions; explain limits to social change.
- **2018 GS I:** Chaitanya's reorientation → Krishna-centred emotional devotion, sankirtan, participation, Bengal–Odisha–Vrindavan networks, later theology; distinguish devotion from complete caste abolition.
- **2014 GS I:** claim that Sufis/medieval saints failed to modify society → weigh inclusion, vernacular culture and institutions against persistent hierarchy, political conflict and later sect formation; avoid an absolute yes/no.
- **2014 History optional:** Vaishnava saints and literature → Tamil Alvars; Jnaneshwari/Marathi abhangs; Braj Krishna poetry; Awadhi Rama literature; Bengali/Assamese/Telugu examples and their devotional differences.
- **2013 History optional:** historiography of Bhakti → spiritual/religious readings; social-protest and material explanations; vernacular/regional perspectives; caste/gender and critical textual approaches. Assess evidence, later hagiographies and the limits of treating many traditions as one movement.

Sikh Gurus' institutional contributions; Bhakti–Sufi interaction and its limits; Maharashtra's devotional literature. Practice questions remain separate from authenticated year-labelled PYQs; several printed practice options/keys are inconsistent and should not be memorised blindly.
