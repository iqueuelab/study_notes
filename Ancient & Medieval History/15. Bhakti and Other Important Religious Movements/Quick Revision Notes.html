Bhakti and Other Important Religious Movements

1. Bhakti: the central idea

Bhakti = devoted love for the Divine. Personal devotion, grace and surrender offered accessible ways to seek liberation; expressions differed across regions, communities and philosophical schools.

  • Earlier devotional strands appear in Sanskrit and other traditions; image worship, Puranic religion and temple patronage expanded over time. Gupta-period patronage was important, but did not invent all Vishnu worship.
  • Tamil poet-saints made devotion a powerful public tradition from about the sixth century CE. Northern and regional movements flourished particularly during the twelfth–seventeenth centuries.
  • Buddhism/Jainism formed part of the earlier religious setting; Mahayana did not originate with the Buddha in the sixth century BCE. Avalokiteshvara is a bodhisattva, not simply a name for the Buddha's “gracious form”.
  • Common themes, not universal rules: heartfelt devotion over empty formalism; spiritual guidance; singing and remembrance; vernacular expression; dignity and fellowship.
  • Not all Bhakti saints rejected images, temples, Vedic authority, caste institutions or pilgrimage. Religious accessibility did not automatically abolish social inequality.

Why did movements spread?

FactorContribution
Religious resourcesTamil hymns, Vaishnava/Shaiva traditions, Puranas, Vedanta and Nath practices offered varied vocabularies.

Economic changeGrowing towns, artisanal activity and mobility in the thirteenth–fourteenth centuries widened audiences; this is one historical explanation, not a single proven cause.
Cultural exchangeBhakti and Sufi communities interacted in a shared social world; affinities do not mean Bhakti began solely as an Islamic influence.

2. South India: Alvars and Nayanars

TraditionDevotion / peopleTexts and significance
12 AlvarsVishnu; Periyalvar, Andal, Nammalvar, Tondaradippodi and others.Nalayira Divya Prabandham: approximately 4,000 Tamil hymns, assembled by about the tenth century; revered as the Tamil Veda.
63 NayanarsShiva; Appar, Sambandar, Sundarar, Karaikkal Ammaiyar and others.Tevaram: hymns of Appar, Sambandar and Sundarar; Tiruvachakam: associated with Manikkavachakar in the wider Tamil Shaiva canon.
  • Flourished under Pallavas, Pandyas and Cholas; temples and rulers supported devotional activity.
  • Saints came from diverse social backgrounds, including Brahmins, artisans and marginalised communities—not uniformly from one caste group.
  • Andal: woman Alvar, intense Vishnu devotion; Karaikkal Ammaiyar: woman Shaiva saint, ascetic imagery. Manikkavachakar: divine grace and surrender in Tamil poetry.
  • Some hymns polemicised against Jains/Buddhists; competition for patronage mattered. Calling those religions simply “fatalistic” misrepresents their doctrines.
  • Limit: spiritual inclusion and participation coexisted with temples, ritual authority and continuing caste disabilities. Neither “complete social revolution” nor “no challenge at all” captures every tradition.

3. Saguna and Nirguna: compare carefully

DimensionSagunaNirguna
Divine conceptionGod with attributes/form; often Rama, Krishna or Shiva.Divine beyond material form and attributes.
ExamplesTulsidas, Surdas, Mirabai, Chaitanya.Kabir and Guru Nanak are commonly placed in this comparative category.
WorshipPersonal deity, loving service, hymns; images/temples may matter.Remembrance of the Name, inward experience and criticism of external formalism.

4. Philosophers: doctrine, relation and route

Thinker / conventional datesSchoolMain relation / path
Shankara, c.788–820Advaita: non-dualism.Brahman is ultimate reality; ignorance produces mistaken identification. Liberating knowledge is central. The empirical world is not absolutely independent; “unreal” does not mean ordinary experience never occurs.
Ramanuja, traditional 1017–1137Vishishtadvaita: qualified non-dualism.Brahman/Narayana is personal; souls and matter are real, dependent modes, often explained through body–soul relation. Bhakti, grace and prapatti (surrender).
Madhva, traditional 1238–1317Dvaita: dualism.Vishnu, individual souls and matter are really distinct; souls/world depend on God. Difference does not mean “no relationship whatsoever”.
Nimbarka, dating disputedDvaitadvaita / bhedabheda.Difference and non-difference; souls/world distinct yet dependent on Brahman. Radha–Krishna devotion and grace. Not an Akbar contemporary.
Vallabhacharya, 1479–1531Shuddhadvaita: pure non-dualism.World is a real manifestation of Brahman; Pushtimarg, the path of grace, emphasises loving seva to Krishna/Shrinathji. Not a doctrine of two independent ultimate realities.

Mnemonic: Shankara—one; Ramanuja—qualified unity; Madhva—real difference; Nimbarka—difference/non-difference; Vallabha—pure unity and grace.

Texts, institutions and places

  • Shankara: Kaladi, Kerala; commentaries on Brahma Sutras, principal Upanishads and Bhagavad Gita; Upadeshasahasri. Vivekachudamani and Bhaja Govindam are traditionally attributed; authorship of some attributed works is debated. Four mathas associated by tradition: Sringeri, Dwarka, Puri and Jyotirmath in the Badrinath region.
  • Advaita analogy: a rope mistaken for a snake → knowledge removes the error; it does not physically destroy a real snake.
  • Ramanuja: Sriperumbudur, Tamil region; Alvar/Sri Vaishnava background; Sri Bhashya, Vedanta Dipa, Gita Bhashya, Vedantasara. Devotional access widened, while Vedic/social distinctions did not simply vanish.
  • Madhva: Karnataka/Udupi; associated with Brahma sampradaya in traditional Vaishnava classification.
  • Nimbarka: Radha–Krishna; Sanaka/Kumara sampradaya. Sun–ray analogy recalls relatedness and distinction; biography/chronology remain uncertain.
  • Vallabha: traditionally born at Champaranya near Raipur, not Banaras; Telugu family background, later activity in north/west India. Associated with Rudra sampradaya, whose earlier lineage is linked to Vishnusvamin; do not make Vallabha its original founder. Chhattisgarh Tourism.

Maharashtra: Vitthala and the Varkari tradition

Centre: Pandharpur, Vitthala/Vithoba, associated with Krishna; Marathi poetry, kirtan and pilgrimage. Bhagavata and Nath traditions contributed to its religious environment.

Saint / conventional datesWorks and contribution
Jnaneshwar/Dnyaneshwar, 1275–1296Jnaneshwari, Marathi Bhagavad Gita commentary; Amritanubhava, Haripath, abhang tradition; accessible spiritual teaching.
Namdev, c.1270–1350Marathi abhangs, Vitthala devotion, community singing; hymns in Guru Granth Sahib. Tailor association in tradition; conversion/bandit stories are hagiography.
Eknath, c.1533–1599Marathi religious literature; Bhavarth Ramayan, bharuds, recension of Jnaneshwari. Householder devotion; criticism of social exclusion.
Tukaram, c.1608–1649/50Marathi abhangs, preserved in the Gatha; Vitthala, kirtan, compassion, forgiveness and criticism of hypocrisy. An abhanga is not simply a Hindi doha. He lived after Eknath's death.
Samarth Ramdas, 1608–1681Rama/Hanuman devotion; Dasbodh, Manache Shlok, Karunashtakas; spiritual discipline and action. His precise relationship with Shivaji is debated; do not make all later guru-legends settled fact.
  • Varkari: Vitthala-centred devotional/pilgrimage tradition; Dharkari is a textbook label linked to Ramdas's active/heroic devotional emphasis. “Emotional versus rational” is a schematic contrast, not a fixed character judgement.
  • Social breadth is illustrated by saints associated with diverse occupations: Sena—barber; Savata—gardener; Gora—potter; Narhari—goldsmith; Chokhamela—marginalised Mahar background; Janabai—domestic work; Kanhopatra—performer. They were not all necessarily Namdev's simultaneous companions.
  • Karmamela, Chokhamela's son: devotional poetry confronts humiliation and exclusion. Tukaram's poetry joins devotion with solidarity with the distressed; spirituality did not erase the continuing reality of caste.

  • Poetry made domestic labour, family tension, absence and longing spiritually meaningful. Some renounced household roles; others practised devotion within them.

  • Assessment: new voices and spiritual dignity mattered; neither gender hierarchy nor caste exclusion disappeared uniformly.

7. Sufism: inward discipline within Islam

Sufism encompasses Islamic mystical/devotional traditions concerned with purifying the self, love, remembrance and nearness to God. Suf = wool, recalling early ascetic clothing; “purity” is an associated ideal, not the literal translation of that root.

  • Early ascetic/mystical figures include Rabia al-Adawiyya, Bayazid Bistami and Junayd; early traditions developed from the eighth century, with major literature/institutions by the eleventh–twelfth centuries.
  • Ali Hujwiri, eleventh century, Lahore: Kashf al-Mahjub, a Persian Sufi treatise.
  • Spiritual training, repentance, prayer, charity, fasting and ethical conduct mattered. Love of God could be expressed through service to people; not every order held identical views on music, rulers or legal practice.
  • Fana = effacement of the ego; ishq = intense divine love; ruh = spirit/soul; qurb = nearness. Hulul (indwelling/incarnation language) was controversial and is not a universally accepted Sufi doctrine.

IGNOU on Naqshbandi history.

Chishti names and centres

  • Muinuddin Chishti, d.1236: Gharib Nawaz, Ajmer; later pilgrimage and royal patronage, especially Akbar. Chisht is in present-day Afghanistan, not a village in Ajmer. Date of settling at Ajmer varies in accounts; do not confuse later patronage with the saint's lifetime.
  • Baba Farid, c.1175–1265/66: Fariduddin Ganj-i Shakar; hymns in Guru Granth Sahib; ascetic discipline and distance from power.
  • Nizamuddin Auliya, 1238–1325: compassion and service; discipline, purity, fasting, devotion and guidance. Kept distance from courts; tensions with some Delhi sultans.
  • Amir Khusrau, Amir Hasan Sijzi and Ziauddin Barani belonged to his intellectual/devotional milieu. Khusrau is closely associated with Chishti musical traditions; do not ascribe every later musical form to one unqualified invention.
  • Nasiruddin Chiragh-i Delhi: disciple/successor in Nizamuddin's lineage.
  • Burhanuddin Gharib: important fourteenth-century Chishti expansion into the Deccan.
  • Muhammad Gesu Daraz/Banda Nawaz: Gulbarga/Kalaburagi, not Bijapur.
  • Later Naqshbandi figures Shah Waliullah and Mirza Mazhar Jan-i Janan illustrate variation; two rigid “conservative/liberal” labels cannot describe their full thought.

Impact and limits

Khanqah hospitality, service, shared shrine visitation, vernacular expression and music helped cultural exchange. Some Sufis influenced rulers; others avoided courtly dependence. Contact did not erase doctrinal disagreement, political conflict or social hierarchy. Bhakti–Sufi interactions contributed to shared literary/music cultures; Urdu's emergence had wider historical causes too.

9. Sikhism: teachings and institutions

Guru Nanak, 1469–1539

  • Born at Talwandi/Nankana Sahib, now Pakistan; Kartarpur on the Ravi became a major community centre. Do not transfer Kartarpur's river location automatically to Talwandi.
  • One eternal, formless Creator; Naam, ethical conduct, honest livelihood, service and equality; rejected empty ritual and inherited religious superiority.
  • Naam japna—remember the Divine; kirat karna—earn honestly; vand chhakna—share. Householder life can be spiritual.
  • Guru–Shabad–Sangat: teacher, sacred word and congregation; langar expresses shared fellowship. Mardana accompanied hymns on the rabab.
