# Pre-Historic India

## 1. Read the evidence first

- **History**: Greek *historia* = inquiry/knowledge acquired by investigation.
- **Prehistory**: before usable written records; reconstructed through tools, settlements, bones, plant remains, burials and art.
- **Protohistory**: a transitional category—writing is undeciphered, or a society is known through another literate society/oral tradition. **Harappan script remains undeciphered; Mesopotamian texts supply external evidence.** Vedic oral traditions and some Neolithic–Chalcolithic cultures are included in broader textbook usages; definitions vary.
- **Historical period**: written evidence can be read alongside archaeology. These categories do not begin everywhere at one date.

### Sources → what they reveal

| Evidence | Recall and use |
|---|---|
| Vedas | Rig: mainly hymns; Sama: chants; Yajur: sacrificial formulae; Atharva: spells, rites and everyday concerns. Broad textbook composition range c.1500–500 BCE, not one composition date. |
| Upanishads; Sutras | Upanishads/Vedanta: Atman–Brahman, philosophical inquiry. Shrauta and Grihya Sutras: public sacrificial and domestic rites. |
| Epics; Buddhist/Jain texts | Ramayana, Mahabharata; Pali Tipitaka: **Vinaya** discipline, **Sutta** discourses, **Abhidhamma** systematic doctrine. Jain Angas in Prakrit/Ardhamagadhi preserve religious ideas. |
| Law and administration | Dharmashastras, e.g. Manusmriti: prescribed social duties; **prescription ≠ proof of actual practice**. Kautilya's Arthashastra: statecraft/economy; a layered text, not a transparent contemporary Mauryan report. |
| Grammar and literature | **Panini—Ashtadhyayi**; **Kalidasa—Abhijnanashakuntalam, Ritusamhara, Meghadutam**; social/cultural evidence read in literary context. |
| Historical narratives | **Kalhana—Rajatarangini**, 12th-century Kashmir; **Banabhatta—Harshacharita**, court biography of Harsha. Patronage and praise can shape accounts. |
| Tamil literature | Sangam poetry illuminates early Tamil society, economy and polity; broad conventional span c.300 BCE–300 CE. **Silappadikaram and Manimekalai are later/post-Sangam epics**, not securely dated to that entire range. |
| Inscriptions | Epigraphy: engraved stone/metal records; Ashokan policy, Satavahana grants. **Prashasti = eulogy**, not every inscription. Read issuer, purpose and findspot. |
| Coins | Numismatics: economy/trade, rulers, scripts, religion, art and metallurgy. Early **punch-marked silver/copper** coins; Indo-Greek portraits and legends. **Large-scale gold coinage is especially associated with Kushans**. [RBI coin museum](https://systemhealth.rbi.org.in/Scripts/mc_ancient.aspx.html). |
| Excavation | Stratigraphy, houses, tools, pottery and megalithic graves reveal material life; megaliths are especially associated with the south Indian **Iron Age**, not only “before 300 BCE”. |
| Environmental/genetic evidence | Pollen, seeds, fauna and geology reconstruct diet, farming and environment; mitochondrial DNA traces maternal lineages/migrations. Context and dating remain essential. |
| Foreign accounts | **Megasthenes—Indica**, Mauryan society/administration; **Periplus of the Erythraean Sea** (anonymous, 1st century CE) and **Ptolemy—Geography** (2nd century CE): ports/trade. **Faxian: early 5th century CE**, Gupta era; **Xuanzang: 7th century CE**, Harsha/Nalanda—not 500/700 CE as exact visit dates. |

## 2. Stone Age: the comparison to remember

**Tools + subsistence + site context define a culture; dates overlap between regions.**

| Phase | Tool cue | Economy and settlement |
|---|---|---|
| **Palaeolithic / Old** | Handaxes/cleavers → flakes → blades/burins | Hunting–gathering; mobile groups, open sites, caves/rock shelters |
| **Mesolithic / Middle** | **Microliths**, often hafted into composite tools | Hunting, fishing, gathering; temporary/semi-permanent camps; herding/cultivation develop in some later contexts |
| **Neolithic / New** | **Ground/polished celts**, continued chipped tools | Farming + herding, settled villages, storage, pottery in many phases |
| **Chalcolithic / Copper–Stone** | **Copper + stone**, occasional bronze | Mostly rural agropastoral settlements, specialised crafts/exchange |

- Book's broad revision bands: Palaeolithic **c.500,000–10,000 BCE**; Mesolithic **c.10,000–6000 BCE**; Neolithic **c.6000–1000 BCE**; Chalcolithic **c.3000–500 BCE**. **These are teaching ranges, not uniform or definitive boundaries**; Mehrgarh's conventional earlier chronology itself falls outside the Neolithic band.
- **Pleistocene**: repeated glacial/interglacial cycles; India was not entirely ice-covered. **Holocene begins about 11,700 years ago**, broadly overlapping later foragers and early farming societies. “Years ago” is not interchangeable with BCE. [ICS chronology](https://stratigraphy.org/gssps/files/meghalayan.pdf).
- At **Attirampakkam, Tamil Nadu**, a dated transition to Middle Palaeolithic technology occurs around **385 ±64 thousand years ago**, with that sequence continuing to **172 ±41 ka**. This is a **site-specific scientific chronology**, not a universal 385,000–40,000 BCE range. [Research paper](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29388951/).

## 3. Palaeolithic: hunters and gatherers

- Flaked, generally unpolished tools; quartzite widely used, hence the old label **“quartzite men”**—a tool-material label, not a biological population.
- Hunting, gathering wild plant foods and, where available, aquatic resources; no established farming or metal industry. Fire use predates the Mesolithic globally; exact local evidence must be checked.
- **Lower**: heavy handaxes, choppers, cleavers—cutting, digging, skinning. **Middle**: smaller flake tools—scrapers, borers, points. **Upper**: parallel-sided blades, burins, bone tools and ornament/art evidence.
- **Robert Bruce Foote**: Pallavaram handaxe, **1863**; extensive south Indian surveys. Mortimer Wheeler contributed excavation methods and cultural sequencing. H.D. Sankalia's work at **Nevasa** helped identify the “Nevasan” Middle Palaeolithic industry.
- Do not infer a single hominin species, race or exact language capacity from tools. **Homo sapiens originated in Africa about 300,000 years ago**, not at the end of the Upper Palaeolithic; human evolution is branching, not a simple ladder through Neanderthals. [Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-sapiens).

## 4. Mesolithic: microliths, mobility and art

- **Microliths**: small geometric/non-geometric chipped tools, often chert, chalcedony or other cryptocrystalline silica. **Not characteristically polished**; insert in wood/bone handles for arrowheads, spearheads, knives and sickles.
- Warming environments and diverse ecological niches supported smaller-game hunting, fishing and gathering. Some communities became more settled; food production emerged unevenly, not everywhere at once.
- Burial with grave goods suggests ritual/social ideas; it cannot by itself prove a particular belief system.

| Site | Distinctive recall |
|---|---|
| **Bagor**, Kothari river, Rajasthan | Large, well-documented Mesolithic settlement; animal-domestication evidence |
| **Adamgarh**, Madhya Pradesh | Important early herding/domestication evidence; avoid an unqualified worldwide “first” |
| **Mahadaha**, Uttar Pradesh | Bone arrowheads/ornaments; paired male–female burial |
| **Sarai Nahar Rai; Damdama**, Pratapgarh region, UP | Camps, burials and forager life |
| **Langhnaj**, Mehsana, Gujarat | Microlithic occupation and burials; pottery in later contexts. **Langhnaj is not in UP**; Kaimur is a separate region. |
| Chopani Mando, Morhana Pahar, Lekhahia | Belan–Vindhyan/UP region; long forager-to-village transitions, some pottery-bearing contexts |
| Birbhanpur; Paisra/Kuchai; Chhotanagpur | West Bengal and eastern sites |
| Tilwara, Dhaneri, Kanawas; Tarsang, Devnimori | Rajasthan–Gujarat clusters |
| Pachmarhi, Putlikarar; Chitrakot falls | Central Indian distribution |
| Patne, Pachad; Gauri Gundam, Kondapur, Renigunta | Deccan and southern distribution |
| Sanganakallu, Jalahalli; Calicut, Malampuzha, Tenmalai | Karnataka–Kerala microlithic localities; Tirunelveli adds Tamil Nadu |

### Rock art: a ready Mains example

- **Bhimbetka—Vindhyan hills, Raisen district, MP**: multiple periods of occupation/painting; V.S. Wakankar identified the shelters in **1957–58**. **A.C. Carlleyle's 1867–68** discoveries mark early documentation of Indian rock paintings, not their prehistoric creation dates.
- Other clusters: Kaimur/Mirzapur; Kharwar, Jaora, Kathotia (MP); Sundargarh/Sambalpur (Odisha); Ezhuthu Guha (Kerala).
- **Themes**: group hunts, fishing, dances, honey/fruit gathering, food preparation, family/community scenes. Bison, buffalo, deer, nilgai, elephant, rhino and big cats; bows, arrows, barbed spears, pointed sticks. Some contexts suggest ritual or work roles; these remain interpretations.
- **Aesthetics**: dynamic movement, rhythmic group composition, naturalistic animals and stylised/stick-like humans; narrative economy rather than academic realism.
- Red and white dominate; mineral pigments, e.g. **haematite → red**, with other colours also present. Superimposition helps establish relative sequence; **all Bhimbetka art is not Mesolithic**. [NCERT art chapter](https://ncert.nic.in/textbook/pdf/kefa101.pdf).

## 5. Neolithic: the food-producing transition

- **V. Gordon Childe—“Neolithic Revolution”**: profound change in subsistence and social life; a prolonged, regionally varied process rather than a sudden worldwide event.
- Farming + cattle/sheep/goat herding; hunting/gathering continued. Regional crops include wheat/barley, rice and pulses; southern farming included millets and horse gram. **“Corn” can mean grain, not necessarily American maize**; ragi and horse gram are not maize.
- Ground, pecked and polished stone axes/celts; tools for clearing/cultivating, continued blades/scrapers and bone implements. Early handmade pottery, later wheel use in suitable contexts; black-burnished, grey, mat-/cord-impressed wares vary by region. **Aceramic Neolithic phases exist**.
- Circular/rectangular mud-and-reed houses; storage and more durable villages; spinning/weaving and specialised work develop in suitable contexts. Division of labour and exchange increased; do not assume every village was isolated or entirely self-sufficient.

**Food production → storage → longer settlement → specialised work/exchange → more complex social organisation**. Each link is a tendency, not an inevitable one-way law.

| Region / site | Distinctive evidence |
|---|---|
| **Mehrgarh**, Kachi plain, Bolan pass, Balochistan (Pakistan) | Early farming before urban Harappa; mudbrick architecture, wheat/barley, cattle/sheep/goats, cotton in later evidence, drilled teeth. **Conventional textbook beginning c.7000 BCE**; initially aceramic. [Official site description](https://whc.unesco.org/en/tentativelists/1876). |
| **Burzahom**, Kashmir | Pit dwellings; stone/bone tools, burials involving humans and animals including dogs; distinctive rectangular stone chopper. Do not infer every dog was buried with a master. |
| **Gufkral**, Kashmir | Pit dwellings; stone tools and burial evidence; farming/herding. Kashmir's phases overlap Harappan times without being identical cultures. |
| **Chirand**, Bihar | Rich antler/bone-tool industry; **antlers ≠ true horns** |
| **Koldihwa; Mahagara**, Belan valley, UP | Huts, handmade pottery, rice-related evidence; Mahagara cattle-pen evidence. Koldihwa has Neolithic–Chalcolithic–Iron Age sequence. **Not established as the world's earliest rice cultivation**. |
| **Chopani Mando**, Belan valley | Early pottery-bearing forager/village-transition contexts; not an unquestioned worldwide pottery “first” |
| Senuwar/Senwar, Bihar; eastern sites | Gangetic/eastern traditions, cord-marked pottery in relevant contexts and links towards eastern/southeastern Asia |
| **Maski, Brahmagiri, Piklihal, Tekkalakota**, Karnataka | Cattle-herding settlements; southern Neolithic ash-mound tradition |
| **Budihal**, Karnataka | Community food preparation/feasting and pastoral settlement evidence; **not Andhra Pradesh** |
| **Utnur**, Telangana; Nagarjunakonda, AP | Southern pastoral/farming traditions; ash mounds at Utnur |
| **Paiyampalli**, Tamil Nadu; Hallur, Karnataka | Important south Indian Neolithic localities |
| **Daojali Hading**, Assam; Garo hills, Meghalaya | Northeastern Neolithic; ground tools/cord-impressed traditions. Book's broad later horizon **c.2500–1500 BCE or later**, with site-level variation. |
| Sarai Khola, Potwar (Pakistan) | Northwestern settlement sequence; do not classify all Amri/Kot Diji material as Neolithic—these are prominently Early Harappan sites. |

**Dating caution—Mehrgarh:** a **2025 tooth-enamel radiocarbon study** challenges the conventional chronology, placing its Period I approximately **5250–4650 cal BCE**. Keep the familiar c.7000 BCE textbook date labelled conventional, and this result labelled a research revision; neither is a date for the beginning of all South Asian agriculture. [Primary study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-025-92621-5).

## 6. Chalcolithic: copper with stone

- **Chalcos = copper; lithos = stone.** Rural copper–stone cultures can precede, overlap or follow Harappan phases; **not all are pre-Harappan** and not all pre-Harappan societies were copper-using.
- Widespread in Rajasthan, Gujarat, MP, Maharashtra; also eastern/southern regions. Compare rural non-Harappan communities with the **urban Bronze Age Harappan civilisation**, which also included villages.
- Agriculture, stock-rearing, fishing/hunting: wheat/barley more prominent in many western contexts; rice/fish in eastern ones; regional millets and pulses—lentil, black/green gram, grass pea. Cotton cultivation/spinning in suitable black-soil areas; slash-and-burn used in some settings, not a universal system.
- Cattle, sheep, goats, pigs and buffalo; deer hunted. **Copper was not the only known metal**: occasional bronze, gold and silver also occur. Avoid blanket claims that every community lacked dairy use or horses, or that no cultivation implements existed anywhere.
- Painted pottery, **black-and-red ware**, regional red wares and OCP-related horizons are key material markers; decorative styles vary. Potter's wheels and other techniques coexist; no secure universal rule assigns wheel use only to men.
- Mainly mud/wattle-and-daub or mudbrick homes, often thatched; circular and rectangular plans. **Stone foundations/houses at Ahar**; burnt brick occurs at some sites such as **Gilund**—“no Chalcolithic burnt bricks” is too absolute. [University of Mumbai archaeology text](https://mu.ac.in/wp-content/uploads/2022/10/Paper-2-History-of-Indian-Archaeology-English-Version.pdf).
- Copper smelting, stone-working, beads (carnelian, steatite, quartz), shell/bone ornaments, combs, spinning/weaving; exchanges link villages and larger centres. **Ahar copper → neighbouring Malwa/Gujarat exchange** is a useful regional example.
- House size, storage/public works and unequal grave goods suggest social differentiation; they do not prove the same political system everywhere. Inamgaon-type water-management structures suggest coordinated activity.
- Female figurines often interpreted as fertility/mother-goddess symbols; bull figurines suggest animal symbolism. **Interpretation ≠ proof of a fixed pantheon, female supremacy or “inferior male gods”.** Grave goods may indicate ritual/afterlife concepts.
- Numerous child burials in western Deccan contexts indicate substantial child mortality, but burial samples do not directly give a population-wide mortality rate.

## 7. PYQ and answer framework

### UPSC CSE Mains 2015 · GS I · Question 2 · 12½ marks · 200 words

“Mesolithic rock cut architecture of India not only reflects the cultural life of the times but also a fine aesthetic sense comparable to modern painting. Critically evaluate this comment.”

- **Interpret the wording through rock-shelter paintings**; Mesolithic art is not the later excavated Buddhist/Hindu/Jain cave-temple architecture.
- **Culture**: hunting technology/group cooperation; fishing/food gathering; dance, family and food preparation; possible ritual and work roles.
- **Aesthetics**: selective lines, naturalistic animals, stylised people, movement, rhythm, composition, red/white contrast and storytelling. Use Bhimbetka examples.
- **Critical balance**: comparison concerns expressive quality, not identical technique, purpose or social setting. Different periods overlap; ritual/gender readings need corroboration.
- **Conclusion**: both a material record of community life and an accomplished visual language; protect fragile sites.

### Practice: avoid the printed-key traps

- **Q4**: the claim that tiny flaked blades/burins define Neolithic technology and that horse domestication is a universal Neolithic marker is unsafe; the printed **(a)** is not a reliable answer. Pottery alone does not make the supplied options sound.
- **Q5**: “all pre-Harappan cultures are Chalcolithic” is false; retain only the qualified statement about some pre-/post-Harappan copper–stone cultures.
- **Q6**: “paintings depict religious beliefs” is an interpretation; the question's single-answer wording is not rigorous.
- **Q9**: megaliths are especially linked with the southern **Iron Age**; the supplied options/key obscure this.
- **Q15**: “copper was the only metal known” is false; copper is the diagnostic metal, not the sole one.
- **Q16**: Gilund is an exception to the general scarcity of burnt brick; a blanket statement cannot establish the printed answer.
- **Q18**: agriculture/domestication yes; iron is not a defining Neolithic technology. **Only i (a)**; ignore the handwritten tick on (c).
- Other core recall: **microliths—Mesolithic; food production—Neolithic; Ahar/Gilund—Chalcolithic; Burzahom—Neolithic; Bagor—Mesolithic; Belan—all three Stone Age phases**.

### Four short Mains plans

- **Stone Age phases**: tool sequence + changing subsistence + one diagnostic site per phase; finish with overlapping regional dates.
- **Neolithic significance**: five examples—farming, herding, settled/storage-based villages, ground tools, pottery/crafts; add gradual regional transitions and aceramic exceptions.
- **Chalcolithic and Harappa**: separate technological category from chronological relationship; use Ahar–Banas, Malwa, Jorwe; avoid universal “all” statements.
- **Pottery as evidence**: manufacture/firing, decorative styles, diet/storage, exchange and relative dating; add limitations—pots alone cannot identify language, ethnicity or a fixed religion.

## 8. Last-minute recall

- **Lower = handaxe/cleaver → Middle = flakes → Upper = blades/burins**.
- **Meso = micro; Neo = food production + ground/polished tools; Chalco = copper + stone**.
- **Bagor—Kothari | Navdatoli—Narmada | Jorwe/Daimabad—Pravara | Inamgaon—Ghod**.
- **Burzahom—pits + human/animal burials | Chirand—antler tools | Budihal—feasting | southern Neolithic—ash mounds**.
- **No writing ≠ no history; no surviving evidence ≠ proof of absence.**
