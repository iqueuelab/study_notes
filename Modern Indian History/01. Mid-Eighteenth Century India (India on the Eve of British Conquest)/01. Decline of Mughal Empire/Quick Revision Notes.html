Decline of Mughal Empire

1. The eighteenth century: transition, not inevitable conquest

  • 1707: Aurangzeb's death ended the era of the Great Mughals; decline had begun during his reign. Effective imperial power contracted long before the dynasty's final removal in 1857–58.
  • Three overlapping processes: Mughal decline → regional autonomy → Company expansion. European companies initially sought trade; British conquest was not an inevitable outcome visible in 1707.
  • Avoid a blanket “dark age”: political fragmentation coexisted with regional prosperity, commerce and cultural activity. India was not uniformly economically backward.
  • Regional classification in the chapter: successor states—Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad; new powers—Marathas, Sikhs, Jats, Afghans; established/independent kingdoms—Mysore, Kerala, Rajput states. These categories describe different trajectories, not identical origins.
  • Mughal legitimacy, revenue practices and administrative traditions survived in successor states and Company territories.

2. Early Later Mughals: rulers and associations

  • Bahadur Shah I / Muazzam / Shah Alam I (1707–12): defeated Azam at Jajau, June 1707, and Kam Bakhsh near Hyderabad in January 1709. More conciliatory than Aurangzeb; made peace with Rajput chiefs, Churaman Jat and Chhatrasal Bundela.
  • Sikhs: sought accommodation with Guru Gobind Singh; later personally campaigned against Banda Bahadur at Lohgarh. Sikh resistance continued. Lohgarh was associated with Banda's headquarters/fortification; do not attribute its construction simply to Guru Gobind Singh. Haryana Gazetteer
  • Shahu: captive since 1689, released in 1707. The chapter associates his return with Bahadur Shah's conciliatory era; Maharashtra Gazetteers specifically attribute the release to Azam Shah, on Zulfiqar Khan's advice.
  • Bahadur Shah's lavish jagir grants/promotions weakened finances. Khafi Khan's Shah-i-Bekhabar means a heedless/unaware ruler; “headless king” is a misleading literal gloss.
  • Jahandar Shah (1712–13): throne secured through Zulfiqar Khan, his powerful wazir. Defeated by nephew Farrukhsiyar near Agra; Zulfiqar executed.
  • Zulfiqar cultivated Rajput/Maratha support: Jai Singh II—Malwa; Ajit Singh—Gujarat. Confirmed the 1711 Daud Khan Panni–Shahu arrangement for chauth/sardeshmukhi, collected by Mughal officials and passed to Marathas; conciliated Jats/Bundelas, suppressed Sikhs, abolished jizya.
  • Checked excessive grants/offices but extended ijarah—revenue farming: contractors paid a fixed sum to State and extracted revenue from cultivators; encouraged over-assessment/oppression. Ijarah was not a system invented from nothing in this reign.
  • Farrukhsiyar (1713–19): installed by Sayyid Abdullah Khan—wazir; Sayyid Husain Ali Khan—Mir Bakshi. Their predominance lasted 1713–20.
  • Sayyids pursued accommodation with Hindu chiefs, removed jizya/pilgrimage taxes, reconciled Marathas/Rajputs/Jats; Sikh suppression continued. Banda Bahadur captured at Gurdas Nangal, near Gurdaspur, 1715; executed at Delhi, 1716.
  • Farrukhsiyar tried to escape Sayyid control; they deposed, blinded and killed him in 1719, supported by Peshwa Balaji Vishwanath. Important instance of nobles directly disposing of an emperor.
  • Rafi-ud-Darajat (1719): very short reign; died of tuberculosis. Rafi-ud-Daula / Shah Jahan II (1719): another short-lived Sayyid nominee; also died of illness. Do not confuse him with Shah Jahan III.

3. Court factions and kingmakers

  • Four broad groups: Turanis—Central Asian; Iranis—Persian; Afghans; Hindustanis—India-born Muslims, often allied with Rajputs/Jats. Turanis/Afghans largely Sunni; Iranis largely Shia.
  • Factions were fluid interest networks, not permanently united ethnic/religious blocs. Personal ambition, patronage and access to revenue were central.
  • Chapter associations: Irani—Asad Khan/Zulfiqar Khan; Turani—Ghazi-ud-Din Feroz Jung and Chin Qilich Khan / Nizam-ul-Mulk; Hindustani—Sayyid brothers.
  • Earlier nobles backed competing princes; later powerful nobles increasingly selected, controlled and removed emperors. No fixed primogeniture rule meant recurrent succession wars.
  • Kingmakers: Zulfiqar → Sayyid brothers → Nizam-ul-Mulk/Muhammad Amin → Imad-ul-Mulk, followed by growing Rohilla, Maratha and Company control.

4. Farman of 1717: the Company connection

  • Farrukhsiyar's 1717 farman granted important East India Company commercial privileges, notably customs concessions for its Bengal trade.
  • William Hamilton, Company surgeon, treated the emperor during the diplomatic mission; this helped the embassy secure concessions. Recall Hamilton → Farrukhsiyar → 1717, rather than the chapter's uncertain treatment-year detail.
  • Privileges strengthened Company trade; their interpretation and misuse became recurring disputes with Bengal Nawabs. Commercial privilege was not a grant of sovereignty over Bengal.

5. Muhammad Shah: regional breakup and invasion

  • Muhammad Shah / Roshan Akhtar / Rangeela (1719–48): initially a Sayyid nominee; their power was broken in 1720 through rival nobles including Nizam-ul-Mulk/Muhammad Amin Khan. Do not memorise that both brothers were killed together in 1720.
  • Long reign failed to restore imperial coordination. Personal court indulgence is one explanation in the chapter; structural pressures are equally important.
  • Successor-state anchors: Murshid Quli Khan—Bengal, 1717; Saadat Khan / Burhan-ul-Mulk—Awadh, 1722; Nizam-ul-Mulk / Qamar-ud-din / Asaf Jah—Hyderabad, 1724. These were stages of autonomy under continuing Mughal titles, not identical declarations of independence.
  • Baji Rao I's Delhi raid, 1737 exposed northern vulnerability. Nadir Shah's invasion, 1738–39, delivered a devastating blow; Abdali's first invasion followed in 1748.

6. Persian and Afghan invasions: distinguish them

  • Nadir Shah—Persian ruler: frontier neglect, Mughal disunity and wealth attracted invasion. At Karnal, 1739, Muhammad Shah's army was defeated; Khan-i-Dauran died, Saadat Khan was captured.
  • Delhi's massacre and plunder stripped the treasury; Peacock Throne and Koh-i-Noor carried away. Chapter estimates loot at ₹70 crore, enabling three years' Persian tax remission; treat this as a historical estimate, not a modern purchasing-power figure.
  • Territories west of the Indus, including Kabul, lost; finances/prestige collapsed and the north-west became more exposed. Poets Mir and Sauda lamented Delhi's devastation.
  • Nadir assassinated, 1747; Ahmad Shah Abdali / Durrani founded Afghan power, taking the title Durr-i-Durrani, “pearl of pearls”. He was not Nadir's successor as ruler of Persia.
  • Chapter counts seven invasions, 1748–67. Key sequence: 1748 first repulsed; 1749 pressure on Punjab/Muin-ul-Mulk; 1752 Punjab and Multan ceded; 1756–57 Delhi plundered; 1759–61 campaign culminating in Panipat; further expeditions up to 1767. Campaign totals vary with how expeditions are counted.
  • After Muin-ul-Mulk's death, Adina Beg's Punjab appointment intensified conflict. In 1757, Abdali recognised Alamgir II—emperor; Imad-ul-Mulk—wazir; Najib-ud-Daulah—Mir Bakshi; son Timur Shah—Afghan representative, linked to the Mughal family by marriage.
  • 1758: Raghunath Rao's Marathas expelled Afghan influence from Delhi, overran Punjab/Lahore, displaced Timur and installed Adina Beg under Peshwa Balaji Baji Rao.
  • 14 January 1761, Third Battle of Panipat: Abdali defeated Marathas; checked their northern ascendancy, but did not restore effective all-India Mughal rule. Marathas later recovered influence at Delhi.
  • Najib-ud-Daulah: Rohilla chief, Abdali ally, founder of Najibabad; distinguish his son Zabita Khan and grandson Ghulam Qadir.

7. Later rulers: from court puppets to Company protection

  • Ahmad Shah Bahadur (1748–54): weak ministry dominated by mother Udham Bai / Qibla-i-Alam and Javid Khan; Punjab/Multan ceded to Abdali in 1752. Imad-ul-Mulk / Ghazi-ud-Din Feroz Jung III emerged as kingmaker; emperor deposed/blinded in 1754 with Maratha support.
  • Alamgir II / Aziz-ud-din (1754–59): Jahandar Shah's son, raised by Imad. Plassey—23 June 1757 during his reign; Abdali's Delhi sack and subsequent Maratha intervention. Murdered on Imad's orders in 1759.
  • Shah Jahan III / Muhi-ul-Millat (1759–60): Imad's short-lived puppet; descended from Kam Bakhsh. His claim overlapped Shah Alam II's; therefore do not force the succession into a perfectly non-overlapping list.
  • Shah Alam II / Ali Gauhar (1759–1806; chapter also uses 1760): spent early reign outside Delhi because of hostile court politics and insecure authority; later recognised by Afghan/Maratha powers.
  • Buxar, 1764: Shah Alam II + Mir Qasim of Bengal + Shuja-ud-Daulah of Awadh defeated by Company. Allahabad settlement, 1765: Company obtained Diwani of Bengal, Bihar and Orissa; emperor received ₹26 lakh annual payment and Kora/Allahabad, residing at Allahabad.
  • 1771–72: Marathas under Mahadji Shinde / Scindia enabled his return to Delhi, completed in January 1772. He relied on Maratha protection; campaigns against Zabita followed in 1772–73.
  • 1788: Ghulam Qadir seized palace and blinded Shah Alam; Mahadji restored him later that year. Recall “fugitive emperor” → “blind emperor”; Rohilla family members' influence occurred at different times, not all throughout his exile.
  • 1803: Company under Lord Lake captured Delhi from Scindia's forces during Second Anglo-Maratha War; emperor came under British protection.
  • Aftab: Shah Alam's poetic pen-name. Saying “Sultanat-e-Shah Alam, az Dilli ta Palam” captures his reduced authority—Delhi to Palam.
  • Akbar II (1806–37): penultimate emperor; conferred Raja on Ram Mohan Roy, sent him to Britain to seek improved imperial allowance. 1835: Company coinage ceased carrying the Mughal emperor's name.
  • Bahadur Shah II / Zafar (1837–57): poet-emperor, accepted as symbolic sovereign by 1857 rebels; captured after Delhi fell. Tried/deported to Rangoon, 1858; died November 1862.
  • 1857–58: dynasty removed. 1 November 1858, Queen Victoria's Proclamation marks Crown takeover from Company; do not confuse it with Zafar's death or treat it as the beginning of Mughal political weakness.

8. Why the Empire declined: interacting causes

  • Overextension/Deccan wars: Aurangzeb's prolonged campaigns outran communications and administrative capacity; war expenditure, devastated crops and disrupted collections weakened the centre. The “Deccan ulcer” analogy compares this drain with Napoleon's Spanish war.
  • Political/religious conflict: Aurangzeb's policies strained some Rajput, Sikh, Jat and Maratha relationships. Revolts also involved local power, land and revenue; a purely Hindu–Muslim explanation is inadequate.
  • Weak imperial coordination: personal-monarchy dependence, incompetent successors, succession wars, factional patronage and puppet rulers damaged continuity and command.
  • Nobility/army: luxury and competition for offices distracted elites; fragmented contingents, weak supervision, unwieldy camp followers, uneven military adaptation and poor coordination reduced effectiveness.
  • Jagirdari crisis: too many claimants for remunerative assignments, gap between expected/realised revenue and administrative/military costs → competition for good jagirs → factional conflict, under-maintained contingents and regional autonomy.
  • Agrarian distress: heavy revenue demand, revenue farming, frequent transfers and war damage encouraged short-term extraction; cultivators' capacity to pay declined, flight/rebellion rose.
  • Zamindars: hereditary local rights, armed followings and collection roles made them influential intermediaries; names included Rais, Rajas, Khuts, Thakurs, Deshmukhs. Shifting loyalties could support local rebellions or provincial autonomy.
  • Fiscal erosion: long wars, court costs, invasion/plunder and provinces retaining revenues depleted the imperial treasury. Shah Jahan's building expenditure is a chapter example, not a sufficient explanation of eighteenth-century collapse.
  • Regional ascendancy: Marathas expanded north; Sikh/Jat/Rajput and other powers asserted autonomy; successful provincial networks diverted resources from Delhi.
  • External shocks: Persian/Afghan invasions revealed and deepened existing weakness; European companies exploited military and court rivalries, converting commercial footholds into political power.
  • Terminology corrections: a mansab was an imperial rank, not simply hereditary office; a jagir was generally a revenue assignment, not automatic permanent land ownership. Zamindar and jagirdar are not interchangeable. “Police state” is a simplifying textbook label, not the full scope of Mughal government.

9. Historians: explanation → association

  • William Irvine; Jadunath Sarkar → personal failings, court/nobility decline; Sarkar emphasised Aurangzeb's religious policy. Their moral/religious framing should be assessed critically.
  • Satish Chandra; M. Athar Ali → mansabdari–jagirdari pressures, shortage of suitable assignments relative to claims and instability in ruling-class arrangements.
  • Irfan Habib → agrarian crisis: excessive extraction, peasant resistance and transfer-driven short-term exploitation undermined revenue and stability.
  • M.N. Pearson; J.F. Richards; P. Hardy → Deccan/Maratha conflict and the burden of prolonged military commitments.
  • Muzaffar Alam; Chetan Singh → regional developments, especially Awadh/Punjab; stronger provincial economies and groups asserted autonomy while the imperial centre weakened.
  • Evaluation: multi-causal, regionally uneven transformation. Political decline at Delhi did not mean economic/cultural collapse everywhere; Mughal administrative legitimacy could survive loss of effective command.

10. PYQ revision

  • UPSC Prelims 2003—Shah Alam II Assertion–Reason: assertion says early imperial years were outside Delhi; reason attributes this to an always-lurking north-west invasion danger. Printed key: (c), A true, R false. Recall his exile and hostile court politics; this answer does not mean Afghan invasions never occurred.
  • UPSC Prelims 2003—Jahandar's early end: (c), defeated by his nephew in battle. Link Jahandar → Farrukhsiyar → Agra, not deposition by his own wazir.

11. One-minute recall / Mains structure

  • 1707 death → 1717 farman → 1737 Delhi raid → 1739 Karnal/sack → 1757 Plassey → 1761 Panipat → 1764 Buxar → 1765 Diwani → 1772 return → 1788 blinding → 1803 Company Delhi → 1857–58 dynasty ends.
  • Distinguish Bahadur Shah I/II; Shah Alam I/II; Ahmad Shah Bahadur/Abdali; Balaji Vishwanath/Baji Rao I/Balaji Baji Rao; Shah Jahan II/III.
  • Mains: distinguish imperial authority from regional economy → political, military, fiscal and agrarian links → invasions as accelerants → historians' contrasting explanations → continuity alongside fragmentation.
