Rise of British Supremacy: A Backgrounder

1. European background: feudalism to commercial expansion

  • Feudalism: land-based hierarchy linking kings, lords/vassals and dependent peasants/serfs; Church authority influential. Military service, rents and customary obligations sustained the system.
  • Fourteenth-century crisis: agricultural constraints, warfare and Black Death (Europe, 1347–51; England, 1348) reduced population and left land uncultivated. Attempts to retain labour/services provoked peasant resistance.
  • Western Europe: serfdom gradually weakened; towns, wage labour and commercial agriculture expanded. Eastern Europe: landlords later strengthened serfdom—Europe followed different paths.
  • Italian commercial towns: Venice, Genoa, Milan and Florence; Mediterranean/Asian commerce and merchant wealth supported urban growth. Marco Polo (1254–1324) associated with travel to China. Italian city-states were not a unified Italian nation-State.
  • Renaissance, fourteenth–sixteenth centuries: revival of classical learning; art, architecture, literature, science and enquiry; began in Italy. Reformation, sixteenth century: challenges to Catholic authority and emergence of Protestant churches; religious and political change.
  • Commercial revolution: expanding markets, currency, banking/credit, communications and towns such as London, Paris and Amsterdam. Rural manufacture/proto-industry preceded mechanised factories.

2. Voyages: motives, routes and turning points

  • Motives: profitable pepper/spice trade, direct access to Asian goods, bypassing Arab/Italian intermediaries, royal competition and Christian missions—“God, Glory, Gold”.
  • Ottoman capture of Constantinople, 1453, increased concern over established routes; it did not instantly close all Europe–Asia trade. Ottoman control of Egypt/Red Sea belongs to a later phase.
  • Navigation, maps, compass, improved ships and gunpowder, backed by centralising monarchies, enabled armed oceanic ventures. Prince Henry the Navigator: Portuguese patron of exploration.
  • Bartolomeu Dias, 1488: rounded southern Africa; Cape of Storms became Cape of Good Hope. His expedition began in 1487.
  • Christopher Columbus, 1492: Italian navigator sailing for Spain reached the Americas, already inhabited; he did not reach India.
  • Vasco da Gama, 1498: reached Calicut/Kozhikode, ruled by Zamorin, via the Cape, under Portugal's Manuel I. Established the direct Portuguese ocean route; earlier Europe–India commercial contacts already existed.
  • Early profits encouraged repeated expeditions. European ships entered an existing Indian Ocean system of Indian, Arab, Persian, African and Southeast Asian merchants.

3. Merchant capitalism and mercantilism

  • Merchant capitalism, especially sixteenth–seventeenth centuries: accumulation through buying, transporting and selling goods; merchant capital linked dispersed producers and distant markets.
  • Joint-stock company: pooled investor capital and risk for expensive voyages. Royal charter: monopoly and privileges; some companies also received powers to fortify, wage war and negotiate treaties.
  • Mercantilism, sixteenth–eighteenth centuries: State wealth associated with bullion and a favourable trade balance; promote exports/manufactures, restrain competing imports, regulate commerce and protect shipping.
  • Colonies supplied raw materials and captive markets; monopoly companies and naval power joined commercial profit to national rivalry. The supposed fixed quantity of world wealth was a mercantilist assumption, not an economic law.
  • Company formation dates: English 1600; Dutch VOC 1602; Danish 1616; French 1664. Distinguish charter dates from first Indian settlements.

4. Industrial Revolution and changing colonial interests

  • Britain, approximately 1760–1840: transition towards mechanised production, factories and steam; cotton textiles, iron and transport central. Scientific enquiry—Copernicus, Galileo, Newton—and practical engineering contributed.
  • Conditions: coal/iron, capital and credit, domestic/overseas demand, labour supply, agricultural/commercial change and institutions. Colonial trade/plunder supplied resources and markets, but were not the sole cause.
  • Hargreaves—spinning jenny, c.1764; Arkwright—water frame, 1769; Crompton—spinning mule, 1779; Cartwright—power loom, 1785. Arkwright's machine was a separate innovation, not simply a mechanised jenny. IMechE timeline
  • James Watt—separate-condenser patent, 1769: improved existing steam engines; he did not first invent steam power in the 1790s. Steamships and railways expanded in the nineteenth century. Science Museum
  • Earlier merchant interest: purchase Indian manufactures, especially textiles, for profitable resale. Industrial interest: sell British factory goods, obtain raw materials, expand markets and investment opportunities.
  • India shifted from a major textile exporter towards greater dependence on imported British cloth and raw-material exports. This was uneven and gradual; all Indian manufacture did not disappear at once.

5. Portuguese: arrival, governors and naval empire

  • 1498 da Gama → 1500 Pedro Álvares Cabral → 1502 da Gama's return. Cabral's voyage also reached Brazil; conflict at Calicut brought retaliation and closer links with Cochin.
  • Feitoria = trading post; armada = fleet. Early centres: Calicut, Cochin/Kochi, Cannanore/Kannur, Quilon/Kollam. Cochin fort, 1503; Cannanore fort, 1505—do not equate the first factory with the first fort.
  • Estado da Índia: Portuguese Asian government; first viceroy appointed 1505. Armed ships, forts and strategic ports supported commerce; no complete monopoly over all Asian coastal trade.
  • Francisco de Almeida, 1505–09: first viceroy; Blue Water Policy emphasised naval supremacy; associated with Anjediva and coastal fortification.
  • Chaul, 1508: Almeida's son Lourenço killed. Diu, 1509: Almeida defeated an opposing fleet including Egyptian Mamluk and Gujarat forces. Do not merge these two battles.
  • Afonso/Alfonso de Albuquerque, 1509–15: consolidated territorial bases; Goa, 1510, captured from Bijapur Sultanate; Malacca, 1511; Hormuz, 1515. Goa conquest is neither 1499 nor Almeida's Diu victory.
  • Albuquerque encouraged Portuguese–Indian marriages/local participation for permanent settlements; promoted Christianity and restricted sati in his territory. Often called the real founder of Portuguese power in the East.
  • Nuno da Cunha, 1529–38: shifted headquarters Cochin → Goa, 1530. Bahadur Shah of Gujarat, 1535: agreement permitted Portuguese fortification at Diu under Mughal pressure. Diu fort history
  • Cartaz: Portuguese naval pass/licence regulating ships, routes and cargo; seizure/attack threatened non-compliant shipping. Naval coercion and fort networks distinguished their commerce.

6. Portuguese decline and legacy

  • Causes: limited home resources, official corruption, religious coercion and resistance, Dutch/English rivalry, and diversion towards Brazil. Iberian Union, 1580–1640: Portugal linked to Spanish Crown and drawn into its conflicts.
  • Decline in commercial dominance did not mean immediate departure. Goa, Daman and Diu remained Portuguese until Indian military action 1961.
  • Bombay: Anglo-Portuguese marriage treaty 1661, wedding of Catherine of Braganza and Charles II, 1662; Crown transferred Bombay to Company 1668, annual rent £10. Treaty, wedding and Company transfer are separate dates.
  • Missions: Francis Xavier, and Akbar's court visitors Rodolfo Acquaviva and Antonio Monserrate; Christianity, churches, education and language/printing contacts. Also coercion and temple destruction in some controlled territories.
  • Printing press—Goa, 1556. Missionaries produced religious/vernacular works. Henry Heras belongs to twentieth-century Jesuit scholarship, not the sixteenth-century Portuguese mission.
  • Columbian exchange spread American crops through Portuguese and other routes: chillies, tobacco, maize, potatoes, sweet potatoes, cashew, pineapple, papaya, sapota and groundnut. Native Indian black pepper was not a Portuguese introduction.

7. Dutch: VOC, factories and priorities

  • Cornelis de Houtman, 1596: reached Banten, Java, not India. VOC, 1602: united Dutch companies; States-General charter empowered trade, forts, war/peace and overseas administration.
  • Fluyt/fluit: economical cargo vessel with comparatively small crew. Dutch priority was Indonesian spice commerce; Indian textiles helped finance/barter for spices.
  • Masulipatnam, 1605: earliest Dutch factory in India; Petapuli/Nizampatnam, 1606; Pulicat, 1610. Coromandel grants came from rulers linked to Vijayanagara/Chandragiri, not Gajapati rulers.
  • Pulicat: major headquarters, Fort Geldria and gold pagoda coinage; headquarters shifted to Nagapattinam, 1690.
  • Other depots: Surat, Broach/Bharuch, Cambay, Ahmedabad, Cochin, Chinsura, Balasore, Kasimbazar, Baranagar, Patna and Agra. Chinsura—Fort Gustavus.
  • Amboyna, 1623: Dutch execution of English merchants and others intensified rivalry; English withdrew from that spice-trade centre and increased Indian interests.
  • Cochin, 1663: Dutch displaced Portuguese. Bedara/Biderra, Bengal, 1759: Company defeat of Dutch checked their Bengal ambitions; Dutch commercial establishments did not all vanish that day.
  • PYQ trap: Pulicat is earliest among the 2003 question's listed choices, whereas Masulipatnam is earliest overall.

8. English company: charter and early footholds

  • English merchants organised 1599; Elizabeth I's charter, 1600 created the East India Company, initially granting a 15-year monopoly. Shareholders and annually elected 24 directors governed it; popularly “John Company”.
  • Initially sought East Indian spices; India supplied textiles for Asian exchange and European sales. Factory = trading warehouse/offices and settlement, not necessarily a manufacturing plant.
  • William Hawkins: arrived Surat on Hector, 1608; visited Jahangir at Agra, 1609, used Turkish; Portuguese opposition obstructed durable trading concessions. He was not the first English traveller ever to India.
  • Masulipatnam, 1611: first English factory in South India. Thomas Best, Swally/Suvali, 1612: naval victory over Portuguese near Surat; Surat factory permitted, 1613, under Jahangir.
  • Sir Thomas Roe, 1615–19: James I's ambassador at Jahangir's court; negotiated commercial protection. Remember Hawkins + Roe → Jahangir, not Roe → Akbar.
  • Odisha, 1633: factories at Hariharpur and Balasore, first English footholds in Eastern India; distinct from the later Bengal/Hugli factory.
  • Golconda Golden Farman, 1632: trading privilege associated with 500 pagodas annually.

9. English settlements: three presidency centres

  • Madras, 1639: Francis Day and Andrew Cogan; land from Damarla Venkatadri Nayak, representative of the Vijayanagara ruler at Chandragiri. Fort St George, 1640—first English fort in India.
  • Madras privileges included fortification/local administration, coinage and sharing customs income. Vijayanagara political lineage survived Talikota, 1565; it did not instantly disappear.
  • Fort St David, near Cuddalore: purchased 1690; another southern base.
  • Bombay, 1668: Company acquisition from Crown; developed into principal western base, replacing Surat's administrative primacy.
  • Hugli, 1651: Bengal factory with permission of Shah Shuja. Bengal/Bihar/Orissa factories initially supervised through southern Company administration.
  • Job Charnock, Sutanuti, 1690: important Company settlement; not proof that Calcutta had no earlier inhabitants or one sole founder.
  • 1696: fortification; 1698: zamindari rights over Sutanuti, Kalikata and Gobindapur, with permission of Azim-us-Shan. Fort William and Bengal presidency developed around 1700.
  • Three presidency centres: Madras—Fort St George; Bombay—western base; Calcutta—Fort William. Fort William is not at Kochi.

10. Company powers, conflict and the 1717 farman

  • Successive charters added judicial authority 1625, armed shipping 1661, and war/peace/military powers 1683; private commerce acquired quasi-State functions.
  • Child's War, 1686–90, against Aurangzeb: Company defeated; resumed commerce after submission and fine of approximately ₹1.5 lakh. Earlier military confrontation was not automatic British superiority.
  • Bengal concession, 1691: trading privileges against annual ₹3,000 payment.
  • Farrukhsiyar's farman, 1717: John Surman's embassy, with physician William Hamilton; often called the “Magna Carta of the Company”.
  • Bengal: Company duty exemption against ₹3,000 annually, not ₹30,000; permission to rent additional villages. Surat: annual ₹10,000 arrangement; southern grants confirmed trading/rent concessions and recognised Company coinage in specified contexts. Farman details
  • Dastak = duty-free transit pass for entitled Company goods. It did not legally exempt all private trade by Company servants; misuse deprived the Nawab's treasury of revenue and disadvantaged other merchants.
  • Private trade: servants supplemented low salaries; private cargo remained subject to local duties where privileges did not apply. European home trade was reserved to the Company.
  • Murshid Quli Khan and Alivardi Khan resisted abuse; commercial concessions became a source of conflict and later political intervention.

11. Danes and French

  • Danish East India Company, 1616; Tranquebar/Tharangambadi, 1620, near Tanjore—Fort Dansborg. Serampore/Frederiksnagore, 1755, Bengal; missionaries, education and printing important.
  • Danish possessions came under Crown administration 1777; Tranquebar and Serampore sold to British, 1845. Denmark never became the main contender for an Indian empire.
  • French company, 1664: Colbert under Louis XIV, not Louis IV; stronger State sponsorship than the early English company.
  • Surat factory, 1668 following the 1667 expedition; Masulipatnam, 1669. Tamil Nadu Open University
  • Pondicherry: Bellanger, 1673; François Martin, 1674, developed the settlement. Sher Khan Lodi, Bijapur's local representative, provided the grant; not himself king of Bijapur.
  • Chandernagore/Chandannagar: Bengal base consolidated in the late seventeenth century. Other French centres: Mahe, Karaikal, Yanam, with trading links at Balasore/Kasimbazar; Mauritius and Réunion oceanic bases.
  • Dutch seized Pondicherry, 1693; Treaty of Ryswick, 1697; return to France, 1699. Treaty date and actual restoration differed. Portuguese and other Europeans had earlier establishments there. Puducherry history
  • Anglo-French/Carnatic rivalry, 1746–63: Dupleix's forces occupied Madras, 1746. British captured Pondicherry, 1761; Paris, 1763, allowed restoration of French settlements, subsequently returned, but curtailed imperial ambitions.
  • French enclaves survived this defeat; transfer to India de facto 1954, de jure 1962. Trading arrival, military defeat and final transfer are different milestones.

12. Trade, monopoly and transition to territorial power

  • European demand: pepper/spices → cotton textiles and silk, plus indigo and saltpetre. Indian exports attracted gold/silver because European merchandise did not balance purchases.
  • Saltpetre = potassium nitrate, KNO₃ / shora: gunpowder ingredient; Bihar/Patna important; heavy cargo could also serve as ballast.
  • Indian merchants, bankers and producers remained active; European arrival did not immediately extinguish their networks. British restrictions on imported Indian textiles protected domestic manufacture, including eighteenth-century Calico Acts.
  • After territorial acquisition, especially Bengal diwani, 1765, Indian revenues financed Company exports and military expansion, reducing reliance on imported bullion. Asian networks included the later opium–China trade.
  • Adam Smith, Wealth of Nations, 1776: criticised monopoly; British manufacturers increasingly sought open Indian markets.
  • Charter Act, 1813: ended Company's Indian trade monopoly except tea and China trade. 1833: ended remaining commercial monopoly/trading activities. Company continued governing until 1858. UK Parliament
  • Mains chain: European commercial expansion → chartered armed trade → Indian political alliances/revenue → military expansion → colonial economy. Company advantage combined shipping, finance, diplomacy and Indian resources; conquest was not predetermined.
