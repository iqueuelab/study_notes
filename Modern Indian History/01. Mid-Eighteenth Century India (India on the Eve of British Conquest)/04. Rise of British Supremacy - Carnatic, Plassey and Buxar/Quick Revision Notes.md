# Rise of British Supremacy: Carnatic, Plassey and Buxar

## 1. Commercial rivalry becomes political power

- Declining effective Mughal authority and regional succession disputes enabled Company intervention. **Trade monopoly, cheaper purchases, protection of settlements and fiscal resources** linked commerce to military power.
- **Carnatic Wars, 1746–63:** English–French struggle centred on the Coromandel Coast; French base **Pondicherry**, English base **Madras**. European wars and local succession conflicts both mattered.
- **Bengal, 1757–65:** **Plassey → Buxar → diwani** transferred effective power from Nawabs to Company. Southern victories and Bengal resources reinforced each other; conquest was gradual, not completed in 1757.
- Regional powers were active participants with changing interests, not merely passive victims or rulers lacking modern “patriotism”.

## 2. First Carnatic War, 1746–48

- **Trigger: War of Austrian Succession, 1740–48**; Britain and France on opposing sides. English attacks on French shipping extended hostilities to India despite Dupleix's initial neutrality proposal.
- **Dupleix—French governor, 1742–54**; sought naval aid from **La Bourdonnais, governor of Mauritius**. French captured **Madras/Fort St George, September 1746**; young **Robert Clive** among those caught in the conflict.
- **Anwaruddin, Nawab of Carnatic**, ordered French withdrawal. His son's forces under **Mahfuz Khan** confronted the French near **St Thomé/Mylapore on River Adyar, 1746**.
- **Captain Paradis** led a much smaller French force including Indian soldiers to victory. Lesson: drill, coordination, artillery and command could outweigh numbers; not proof of innate European superiority.
- **Aix-la-Chapelle, 1748:** mutual restitution—**Madras restored to English; Louisbourg in North America restored to French**. No decisive overall winner.
- Dupleix recognised that military support in Indian succession disputes could yield territorial and political concessions.

## 3. Second Carnatic War, 1749–54

- **Hyderabad:** after **Asaf Jah I/Nizam-ul-Mulk died, 1748**, succession contested by **son Nasir Jung** and **grandson Muzaffar Jung**.
- **Carnatic:** **Chanda Sahib**, Dost Ali's son-in-law, released from Maratha captivity; challenged **Anwaruddin**, whose son was **Muhammad Ali**.
- **French supported Muzaffar Jung + Chanda Sahib**; **English supported Nasir Jung + Muhammad Ali**. Remember the two linked succession disputes.
- **Ambur, 1749, near Vellore:** French-backed alliance defeated/killed Anwaruddin. Chanda Sahib became Nawab; Muhammad Ali withdrew to **Trichinopoly/Tiruchirappalli**. French received villages near Pondicherry.
- **Nasir Jung killed, 1750 → Muzaffar Jung installed → Muzaffar killed, 1751 → Salabat Jung installed with Bussy's support**. Nasir was killed by an allied Indian chief, not personally by Dupleix.
- **Marquis de Bussy:** maintained French influence at Hyderabad; obtained **four Northern Circars, 1753—Mustafanagar, Ellore, Rajahmundry, Chicacole**. Grants around Pondicherry and **Masulipatnam in present Andhra Pradesh**, not Odisha, strengthened French finances.
- **Clive captures Arcot, August 1751:** diversion to relieve pressure on Muhammad Ali at Trichinopoly; defended it through the subsequent siege. **Kaveripak, 1752**, another English success.
- **Stringer Lawrence relieved Trichinopoly, 1752**; French forces surrendered. Chanda Sahib was killed after falling into **Tanjore/Thanjavur** hands, not by the ruler of Travancore. Muhammad Ali secured the Nawabship.
- **Dupleix recalled, 1754**, amid high war expenses and commercial concerns; **Godeheu** succeeded him.
- **Pondicherry agreement, 1754:** Godeheu and English governor **Saunders** agreed to restrict intervention in Indian rulers' disputes. The supplied text gives 1755; use **1754** for the agreement ending this war. English gained the stronger Carnatic position; Bussy's Hyderabad influence initially survived. ## 4. Third Carnatic War, 1756/57–63

- **Trigger: Seven Years' War, 1756–63**. Some Indian timelines date the Third Carnatic War from **1757**; learn the European trigger separately.
- **Count de Lally arrived, 1758**; captured **Fort St David**, but siege of Madras failed. Problems: funds/ammunition, naval support, strained relations with officers and local allies.
- **Bussy recalled from Hyderabad** to southern operations; French influence weakened. English forces from Bengal occupied **Rajahmundry/Masulipatnam** and made arrangements with **Salabat Jung**.
- **Wandiwash/Vandavasi, 22 January 1760:** **Eyre Coote defeated Lally**; **Bussy captured**. Decisive military turning point in English–French rivalry.
- **Pondicherry surrendered, 16 January 1761**; French sought Mysorean help, but it did not reverse the outcome. Other positions, including **Gingee and Mahe**, also fell.
- **Paris, 1763:** French settlements restored for commerce under restrictions; ambitions for territorial supremacy curtailed. French presence continued—the treaty did not expel every French trader from India.
- **Lally executed in France, 1766**, after prosecution for the defeat.

## 5. Why the English defeated the French

- **Naval superiority:** more reliable reinforcements, shipping and control of sea communications; French support intermittent.
- **Finance and commerce:** stronger English commercial networks/credit; **Bengal wealth after Plassey** financed southern campaigning. French finances strained by continental wars and expensive intervention.
- **Organisation:** French Company more dependent on royal sponsorship; English directors pursued commercial interests with greater operational autonomy. English Company still depended on British naval/State support—“wholly independent of State” is inaccurate.
- **Leadership and coordination:** Dupleix's recall, withdrawal of Bussy and Lally's conflicts weakened continuity; English commanders and Indian allies coordinated more effectively at decisive moments.
- **European priorities:** France's continental commitments diverted resources, while Britain emphasised maritime/colonial competition. Avoid absolute claims that Britain had no European interests or that French political institutions alone caused defeat.
- **Mains balance:** explain interacting finance, logistics, diplomacy and leadership; Bengal access was crucial, not a single universal explanation.

## 6. Bengal before Plassey

- **Hugli factory, 1651—Shah Shuja**; other factories at **Kasimbazar and Patna**. **1698:** Company acquired zamindari of **Sutanuti, Kalikata, Gobindapur**; Bengal administration centred on **Fort William around 1700**.
- **1717 Farrukhsiyar farman:** Company trading concessions; **₹3,000 annual Bengal payment**. **Dastaks**, intended for entitled Company goods, were misused in servants' private trade; revenue loss and unequal competition provoked Nawabs.
- **Murshid Quli Khan:** combined provincial/revenue authority in **1717**. **Alivardi Khan, 1740–56:** restrained European fortification; grandson **Siraj-ud-Daulah succeeded, April 1756**.
- Bengal supplied textiles/silk and other valuable exports; merchant-bankers and landed interests influenced provincial politics. “Independent” Nawabs retained formal imperial connections.

## 7. Siraj–Company conflict and the Plassey conspiracy

- **Court rivals:** **Shaukat Jung, Purnea**, and **Ghaseti Begum**; factions involving **Jagat Seths, Mir Jafar, Rai Durlabh, Raj Ballabh, Umichand/Amir Chand**, and others weakened Siraj's position.
- Company provocations: **dastak abuse; unauthorised Fort William fortification; shelter to Krishna Das, Raj Ballabh's son; political support to Siraj's rivals**.
- **Fort William fell, 20 June 1756**; governor **Roger Drake** fled to **Fulta/Falta**. Siraj renamed Calcutta **Alinagar**; **Manik Chand** placed in charge.
- **“Black Hole” account:** **J. Z. Holwell** claimed confinement of 146 people with only 23 survivors. Numbers, circumstances and Siraj's direct responsibility are disputed; do not memorise the narrative as uncontested evidence. It became a justification for British aggression.
- English expedition from Madras under **Clive**, with naval support under **Admiral Charles Watson**, recovered Calcutta **January 1757**.
- **Treaty of Alinagar, 9 February 1757:** restored commercial privileges, permitted fortification and provided compensation. It did not end Company's plans for a change of Nawab.
- Company and disaffected court interests arranged **Mir Jafar's installation**. Other participants included **Manik Chand, Jagat Seths and Khadim Khan**; roles and motives varied. French **Chandernagore captured, March 1757**, weakened a potential rival.

## 8. Plassey, 23 June 1757: conspiracy and consequence

- **Location:** Palashi/Plassey on Bhagirathi in Bengal. **Clive's Company forces versus Siraj's much larger army**; Company army itself included many Indian sepoys.
- **Mir Madan and Mohan Lal resisted**; Mir Madan killed. **Mir Jafar and Rai Durlabh withheld effective support**; Siraj withdrew and his position collapsed.
- Siraj later captured and killed on orders associated with **Miran, Mir Jafar's son**; **Muhammad Ali Beg** identified as the killer in the supplied account.
- **Mir Jafar installed, 1757**; Company received **24-Parganas zamindari**, commercial privileges, compensation and large payments/gifts to officials. Separate payments to Company and individuals—do not treat every quoted gift as the whole settlement.
- Company became **kingmaker**; resident **William Watts** and a Company military presence strengthened influence. **Clive's first Bengal governorship is conventionally dated 1758–60**; political ascendancy began with Plassey in 1757.
- **Significance:** limited fighting produced enormous political/fiscal consequences. Wealth/credit financed southern wars and expansion; merchants' commercial power acquired a territorial base.
- **Plassey did not itself grant diwani**; the imperial revenue grant followed **1765**. Private-trade abuses also did not become universally lawful simply through victory.
- Interpretation associated with **K. M. Panikkar:** collaboration of financiers and commanders was central. Explain interests and institutions beyond Siraj's personality or “treachery” alone.

## 9. Mir Jafar to Mir Qasim

- **Mir Jafar, 1757–60:** faced unpaid forces, local opposition and Company's escalating demands; sought Dutch support. **Bedara/Biderra, November 1759:** English defeated Dutch intervention.
- **Henry Vansittart succeeded Clive, 1760**. Company replaced Jafar with son-in-law **Mir Qasim, October 1760**, expecting greater payments.
- **Mir Qasim, 1760–63:** ceded **Burdwan, Midnapore and Chittagong**; promised share in **Sylhet chunam/lime trade**; paid large gifts to Company officials.
- Reforms: capital **Murshidabad → Monghyr/Munger**; bureaucracy/revenue reorganisation; European-style military training and gun manufacture. Sought effective provincial autonomy within existing political arrangements.
- Conflict with **Ram Narayan, Bihar deputy**, backed by English interests; Qasim removed him. His execution belongs to the later conflict, not proof of a separate sovereign Bihar kingdom.
- **Private-trade abuses:** sold/misused dastaks, coercive purchases and duty evasion → Nawab's revenue loss + Indian merchants' disadvantage.
- **Vansittart–Qasim compromise, 1762:** private inland trade to pay **9% duty** and respect Nawab's authority; rejected by Calcutta Council, whose members benefited from private trade.
- **Qasim abolished inland duties, 1763**, giving Indian merchants parity. Company's **Ellis attacked Patna**; war followed. Qasim defeated and fled to Awadh.
- **Mir Jafar restored, 1763–65**, before Buxar—not first restored after Buxar. **Ghulam Husain's Siyar-ul-Mutakhkherin** valued Qasim's administration; historical assessment should distinguish reform from the violent war.

## 10. Buxar, 22 October 1764

- **Allied powers:** **Mir Qasim + Shuja-ud-Daulah, Nawab of Awadh + Shah Alam II, Mughal emperor**. **Company commander: Hector Munro**—not Clive.
- Trade/revenue conflict escalated into a wider regional confrontation. Mir Qasim's alliance is central; he had fallen out with Shuja and was no longer personally directing the allied battlefield army.
- Company victory in substantial, contested fighting confirmed its military strength; command, cohesion and disciplined forces mattered more than numbers alone.
- **Plassey:** opened Bengal through conspiracy/court intervention. **Buxar:** defeated a broader coalition, extended Company influence into north India and enabled the **1765 settlements**.
- Bengal Nawab dependent; Awadh at Company's mercy; emperor negotiated under protection. Victory did not mean immediate annexation of all three powers.

## 11. Allahabad settlements and diwani, 1765

- **Clive's second governorship, 1765–67**; defined Company's relations with defeated powers.
- **Awadh settlement, 16 August 1765:** **Shuja-ud-Daulah** paid **₹50 lakh indemnity**; surrendered **Kora and Allahabad** for emperor; **Balwant Singh of Banaras** confirmed in his estate; Company protection/troop arrangements increased dependence.
- **Awadh restored as buffer**, avoiding an exposed direct Company frontier towards Maratha/Afghan powers. This precedes Wellesley's later standard subsidiary-alliance system.
- **Imperial diwani grant, 12 August 1765:** **Bengal, Bihar and Orissa** revenues to Company; **₹26 lakh annually to Shah Alam II**; emperor received Kora/Allahabad and protection. [NIOS: Bengal settlement](https://digital.nios.ac.in/content/315en/315_History_Eng_Lesson16.pdf)
- “Orissa” refers to the imperial provincial grant, not immediate Company acquisition of all modern Odisha; coastal Cuttack remained outside effective Company possession until **1803**.
- **Najm-ud-Daulah succeeded Mir Jafar, February 1765**. Nawab's defence/foreign relations and control over appointments came under Company; administration handled through a **naib-subahdar** acceptable to it.
- Initial allowance for Nawab's nizamat expenses approximately **₹53 lakh annually**; Company obtained legal revenue authority while already exercising practical political control.

## 12. Dual government in Bengal, 1765–72

- **Diwani:** revenue/financial functions—Company. **Nizamat:** executive, policing/criminal justice—nominal Nawab. **Reality: Company controlled both through dependent intermediaries**, retaining power while shifting administrative responsibility.
- **Muhammad Reza Khan:** deputy diwan in Bengal, also naib-nazim; **Raja Shitab Roy:** Bihar deputy diwan. Existing Indian machinery collected revenue under Company direction.
- Clive's reasons: maximise commercial revenue; avoid full administrative cost/personnel burden; retain a familiar Nawab façade; reduce resistance by Indian/European powers and scrutiny from Britain.
- Consequences: weak accountability, oppressive revenue extraction, corruption and coercive trade; peasants and artisans carried the burden.
- **Drain mechanism:** Bengal revenues purchased Indian goods for overseas sale, replacing bullion remittances from Britain. Company political power also enforced unfavourable prices/monopolistic buying.
- Raw-silk encouragement versus restrictions on woven-silk production favoured British industry. Claims of systematic Company cutting of weavers' thumbs are not established facts; coercion itself is well documented.
- **Bengal famine, 1770:** crop failure/drought compounded by extraction, commerce and administrative failures; avoid attributing it to only one cause.
- **Warren Hastings ended dual government, 1772**, bringing revenue management more directly under Company. This eighteenth-century arrangement is distinct from constitutional **dyarchy introduced in 1919**.
