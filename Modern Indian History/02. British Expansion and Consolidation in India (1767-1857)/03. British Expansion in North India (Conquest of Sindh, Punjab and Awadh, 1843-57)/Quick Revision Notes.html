Three routes to annexation

RegionAnnexationKey officialMethod / justification
Sindh1843Ellenborough; Charles NapierTreaty pressure followed by military conquest
Punjab29 March 1849Dalhousie; Hugh GoughSecond Anglo-Sikh War
Awadh1856Dalhousie; OutramAlleged misgovernment; not Doctrine of Lapse

British commercial ambitions, revenue needs and northwest security reinforced each other. Claims of restoring prestige after the Afghan disaster were part of the context, not a complete explanation for every annexation.

Sindh: from friendship to conquest

Talpuras: Baluch ruling house established power in the 1780s under Mir Fateh Ali Khan; subsequent Amirs shared authority (the 'Char Yar' arrangement). Their territories included Hyderabad and strategically important Indus centres.

Why Sindh mattered: Indus trade/navigation, access toward Afghanistan, frontier fears and the desire to repair British prestige. British troops occupied Shikarpur, Bukkur and Karachi during the Afghan campaign and extracted payments despite earlier treaty assurances. Sikh pressure toward Sindh was used as a pretext for unsolicited 'protection'.

Evaluation: coercive treaty revision and disproportionate conquest make the episode an example of expansion under the language of security. Napier's notorious defence of annexation acknowledged its questionable legitimacy; avoid presenting that defence as a neutral historical judgment.

Punjab after Ranjit Singh

June 1839: Ranjit Singh died. Succession struggles followed: Kharak Singh, Nau Nihal Singh and others; 1843: minor Duleep/Dalip Singh installed, with Rani Jindan as regent. Rapid growth of the Khalsa army, fiscal pressure and court factionalism weakened stable authority.

British troop concentrations, the annexation of Sindh and political-agent Broadfoot's conduct intensified suspicion. Hardinge became Governor-General in 1844. Lal Singh was wazir, Tej Singh a leading commander; the Company's commander-in-chief was Hugh Gough.

Causes of war: British territorial/security ambition, Punjab's wealth, succession instability, army-court tensions, mutual mistrust and frontier military preparations. Accusations that Sikh leaders deliberately diverted an uncontrollable army into war are interpretations; do not present personal allegations about Rani Jindan as established causes.

First Anglo-Sikh War (1845-46)

Sikh forces crossed the Sutlej in December 1845; Hardinge declared war on 13 December. British preparations and Sikh action must both be included.

Sikh soldiers resisted fiercely. Alleged betrayal by Lal Singh/Tej Singh figures in accounts, but superior resources, command decisions and battlefield conditions also matter. National Army Museum: First Sikh War.

Treaties: Lahore is not Amritsar

TreatyMain terms
Lahore, 9 March 1846Loss of territories/claims south of Sutlej and Jalandhar Doab; ₹1.5-crore indemnity, territorial cessions in lieu of part; reduction of army; Duleep recognised; Henry Lawrence became Resident
Amritsar, 16 March 1846Specified hill territories including Kashmir transferred to Gulab Singh for ₹75 lakh Nanakshahi, not ₹1 crore as the source states
Bhairowal, 16 December 1846Regency council of eight Sikh chiefs under British Resident; British troops maintained at Lahore; effective British control during the minority; Jindan excluded from governing authority

The source prints Bhairowal as 22 December; distinguish the treaty date 16 December from subsequent arrangements. Lahore and Amritsar had different parties and functions. Treaty of Amritsar, Articles I and III; Sikh Museum Initiative: treaty records.

Why no immediate annexation? The Khalsa was weakened but not eliminated; resistance, administrative cost and military resources favoured indirect control first. 'Respect for Ranjit Singh' alone is inadequate.

Second Anglo-Sikh War (1848-49)

Trigger and campaign

British intervention under the regency, Jindan's removal and growing resentment formed the background. April 1848: British officers Vans Agnew and Anderson were killed at Multan during the replacement of governor Mulraj. Revolt spread; Sher Singh Attariwala became a major rebel leader. Dalhousie used the crisis to pursue annexation.

BattleDateRecall
Ramnagar22 November 1848Inconclusive/costly encounter
Chillianwala13 January 1849Heavy British losses; no decisive settlement
Gujrat21 February 1849Decisive victory under Hugh Gough

Correction: Charles Napier conquered Sindh; Gough, not Napier, commanded at Gujrat. Napier was appointed to replace Gough after criticism, but the decisive battle occurred before he assumed command. National Army Museum: Second Sikh War.

29 March 1849: Punjab annexed; Duleep pensioned, later taken to Britain; Koh-i-Noor surrendered under the annexation settlement. A Board of Administration led by Henry Lawrence, with John Lawrence and Charles Mansel, administered Punjab.

Evaluation: extended frontier access and British strategic control; critics saw a breach of the Company's guardianship obligations toward a minor ruler. British effective control under Bhairowal complicates blaming the child Maharaja for rebellion.

Dalhousie (1848-56): annexations and reforms

Doctrine of Lapse: precise definition

For specified dependent states, British authorities refused to recognise an adopted heir's right to political succession when the ruler died without a recognised natural male heir; state territory could be annexed. Private inheritance/adoption was not simply outlawed. Misgovernment was a separate justification.

Dalhousie systematised earlier practice, not invented it from nothing: Directors' policy from 1834, Mandvi 1839, Jalaun and other small states 1840 preceded him. Names and classifications of minor states vary; do not confuse Jaitpur with Jaipur or Udaipur in central India with Mewar.

Refusal of pensions/titles, including Nana Sahib's pension claim after Baji Rao II, deepened elite grievances.

Reforms and their purpose

  • Administrative centralisation; commissioners for newly acquired territories; military centres shifted toward northern/western frontiers.
  • Wood's Despatch, July 1854: educational structure, provincial departments, grants, training and universities; Bombay, Madras and Calcutta universities followed in 1857.
  • 1853: Bombay-Thane passenger railway and Railway Minute; 1854: separate Public Works Department, postal reforms and expanding telegraph network.
  • Railways, canals, roads, post and telegraph served revenue, commerce and troop movement, while also creating infrastructure later used by Indians. W.B. O'Shaughnessy, rather than Dalhousie alone, is associated with developing the electric telegraph.

Balanced evaluation: durable infrastructure and administrative changes coexisted with aggressive imperial expansion and social/fiscal disruption; annexations helped create grievances feeding the 1857 revolt.

Awadh: dependence to annexation

1857Widespread Awadh resistance; Begum Hazrat Mahal prominent in the later revolt

The 1765 Allahabad settlement is not the same as Wellesley's 1801 subsidiary arrangement. British protection and interference made the Company partly responsible for the condition it later used to justify annexation.

Effects: dismantled court and army → loss of employment/patronage for soldiers, artisans and retainers; summary revenue settlement dispossessed many taluqdars; peasants faced heavier burdens; sepoys with Awadh family/land connections felt directly affected. This explains broad rural mobilisation in 1857 without relying on the source's unsupported precise 'three-fourths of adults' estimate.

North-Western Provinces: avoid a geography trap

  • Ceded and Conquered Provinces: Company acquisitions of 1801-06, broadly in present Uttar Pradesh.
  • 1836: North-Western Provinces organised; distinct from the later North-West Frontier Province.
  • 1856: Awadh annexed but initially administered separately. Administrative association with the North-Western Provinces developed later, notably 1877; do not call it a single merged province from 1856.
  • 1858: Allahabad became an important provincial seat and the venue of the royal proclamation; avoid the loosely repeated 'capital of India for one day' claim.
  • 1902: United Provinces of Agra and Oudh; territorial changes after 1857 included Delhi's transfer toward Punjab administration.

PYQs and mains recall

2004 matching: 1790-92 = Third Anglo-Mysore; 1824-26 = First Anglo-Burmese; therefore pairs 2 and 3, option d. 2007: ruler removed for misgovernment = Awadh, option a.

Mains structure: strategic/commercial motives → unequal treaties/war or administrative pretexts → annexation → social and revenue consequences → connection to 1857. Distinguish Sindh-Napier, Punjab-Gough, Awadh-misgovernment, Jhansi-lapse.
