British Expansion in South India: Conquest of Mysore and the Marathas (1767–1818)

1. Why the Company fought Mysore and the Marathas

  • Regional expansion sought revenue, ports and strategic territory; Maratha chauth/sardeshmukhi claims generated rivalries. These conflicts enabled Company alliances and intervention.
  • Mysore: Hyder/Tipu controlled Malabar pepper/cardamom commerce and threatened Madras; French links and improved military capacity alarmed Company. Tipu's restrictions, 1785 and 1788, curtailed exports/dealings with English traders.
  • Marathas: control near Bombay and Gujarat challenged Company trade and territorial ambition. Cotton exports to China expanded in the late eighteenth century—the chapter's 1874 date is out of period.
  • Company sought cheaper commerce, territorial revenues, military security and exclusion of French influence. Later European/Napoleonic wars intensified these concerns; they do not explain every earlier war.
  • British claims to “restore legitimate dynasties” helped justify intervention, especially restoring Wadiyars after Tipu; restoration coexisted with imposed dependence.
  • Mysore wars: 1767–69, 1780–84, 1790–92, 1799. Maratha wars: 1775–82, 1803–05, 1817–18. First Mysore war favoured Hyder; second ended by mutual restitution, not decisive British victory.

2. First Anglo-Mysore War, 1767–69

  • Hyder Ali, effective ruler from 1761, faced shifting rivalry with Marathas, Hyderabad Nizam and Carnatic Nawab; English primarily acted through Madras.
  • Northern Circars: strategic coastal link between Bengal and Madras; Company expansion and its 1766 agreement with Nizam tied territorial rights to military support against Hyder.
  • Nizam, Marathas and Company initially combined against Mysore. Hyder bought off the Marathas and temporarily won over Nizam, breaking the coalition; alliances changed again during the conflict.
  • Hyder advanced to the vicinity of Madras and compelled peace. Treaty of Madras, April 1769: mutual restoration and promise of assistance if a third power attacked.
  • Result: Hyder had the stronger negotiating position; no British conquest of Mysore.

3. Second Anglo-Mysore War, 1780–84

  • Company failed to aid Hyder against Marathas after the 1769 treaty. Capture of French Mahe, 1779, threatened Mysore's military-supply connection; British movement towards Guntur also heightened tensions.
  • American War of Independence, 1775–83, and Anglo-French conflict provided the wider setting. Hyder initially sought a coalition with Marathas/Nizam; British diplomacy subsequently isolated him.
  • Pollilur, 1780: Mysorean victory over Colonel Baillie's force. Porto Novo, July 1781: Eyre Coote defeated Hyder. Do not confuse Baillie with French admiral Suffren.
  • Admiral Suffren brought French naval support 1782. Hyder died, December 1782; Tipu Sultan continued the war.
  • End of European hostilities and exhaustion on both sides encouraged peace. Treaty of Mangalore, March 1784: mutual return of territories/prisoners; no decisive territorial British victory.
  • Often described as an important British–Indian treaty on equal terms; avoid treating “last equal treaty” as an absolute claim about every later agreement.

4. Third Anglo-Mysore War, 1790–92

  • Cornwallis allied with Nizam and Marathas. Tipu sought external support through embassies to Ottoman Constantinople and France.
  • Immediate trigger: Tipu's assault on Travancore's Nedumkotta defences, late 1789, renewed in 1790. Travancore had bought Cranganore/Kodungallur and Ayacottah from Dutch; Tipu disputed their transfer within the Cochin sphere.
  • Cornwallis led the campaign towards Seringapatam/Srirangapatna; Tipu accepted peace.
  • Treaty of Seringapatam, March 1792: lost half his dominions to Company and allies; Company gained Malabar, Baramahal and Dindigul. Nizam and Marathas also received territory.
  • Indemnity ₹3.3 crore (330 lakh sicca rupees); two sons given as hostages pending payment. The chapter rounds this to ₹3 crore; the treaty specifies three crore thirty lakh. Treaty collection Hostages belong to Third War, Tipu's death to Fourth War.
  • Loss of Malabar reduced maritime access/resources. John Shore, 1793–98, then favoured non-intervention; distinct from John Lawrence's later Afghan policy.

5. Fourth Anglo-Mysore War, 1799

  • Governor-General Richard Wellesley, 1798–1805: feared French influence; Tipu's diplomatic contacts and refusal of demanded arrangements supplied the immediate setting.
  • Napoleon's Egyptian expedition, 1798, raised British concern over India. His later 1801 Russian scheme cannot be the chronological trigger of the 1799 war.
  • Tipu's French/Jacobin contacts included the Tree of Liberty/Citizen Tipu association. Monsieur Raymond's French-trained corps belonged to Hyderabad Nizam, not Tipu's army. Telangana district history
  • Nizam entered Company's subsidiary alliance, 1798, and his French corps was disbanded. British armies under General George Harris, including Arthur Wellesley, advanced on Mysore.
  • Seringapatam stormed, 4 May 1799; Tipu killed defending the capital. No timely French rescue.
  • Territory partitioned principally among Company, Nizam and a restored Mysore kingdom; Marathas did not receive the straightforward equal share implied by the chapter.
  • Krishnaraja Wadiyar III, a child of about five, installed; Purnaiah served as diwan. A 1799 subsidiary treaty made restored Mysore dependent; Tipu's family detained/pensioned, initially at Vellore.
  • Company gained strategic coastal access; restored dynasty supplied a legitimacy claim while British control deepened. Wadiyar rule later included a period of direct British administration; it was not uninterrupted autonomous rule to 1947.

6. Subsidiary alliance: mechanism and effects

  • Earlier protective arrangements existed; Wellesley systematically expanded this instrument alongside outright war and assumption of subordinated territories.
  • Indian ruler accepted a Company force and paid a subsidy or ceded territory for its upkeep; received promised protection against external/internal threats.
  • Foreign relations restricted; no independent treaty/war without Company approval. British Resident stationed; employment of European rivals restricted; military independence weakened.
  • Company formally promised non-interference internally, yet Resident and troops enabled growing practical control.
  • Company gains: forces maintained at allies' cost, forward strategic bases, exclusion of French officers, control of interstate diplomacy and expansion of paramountcy.
  • State costs: lost sovereignty, heavy fiscal burden, taxation/territorial loss and displacement of soldiers; protected rulers faced weaker accountability to subjects. Subsidy terms varied—one-third of revenue was not universal.
  • Key agreements: Hyderabad 1798/1800; Mysore 1799; Awadh 1801; Peshwa Bassein, 1802; Scindia further agreement 1804; Nagpur 1816; Holkar 1818.
  • Tanjore 1799, Carnatic 1801, Surat 1800: assumption of administration/control needs distinction from a simple standard alliance. Rajput States were generally brought into treaty relationships, not all annexed outright.
  • Richard Wellesley = Governor-General; Arthur Wellesley = younger brother, soldier, later Duke of Wellington and British prime minister. Do not interchange them.

7. Maratha confederacy and the first war, 1775–82

  • Peshwa—Poona; Scindia—Gwalior; Holkar—Indore; Bhonsle—Nagpur; Gaekwad—Baroda. Nominal leadership did not eliminate independent armies, interests or Scindia–Holkar rivalry.
  • After Madhav Rao I died, 1772, Narayan Rao murdered, 1773. Barbhai council under Nana Phadnavis installed Narayan's posthumous son Sawai Madhav Rao II; Raghunath Rao/Raghoba sought British support.
  • Bombay Company's aim: Salsette/Bassein, commerce and influence at Poona. Bengal government under Warren Hastings initially opposed Bombay's arrangements.
  • Surat, March 1775: Bombay backed Raghoba with troops; he promised Salsette/Bassein and revenue interests in Surat/Broach. Keating's Adas campaign, May 1775, followed.
  • Purandar, 1 March 1776: Colonel Upton, Bengal government and Poona ministers; Surat agreement annulled; Raghoba pensioned; British retained specified territorial benefits. Use 1776, not 1777. Maharashtra Gazetteer
  • French envoy St Lubin at Poona and wider Anglo-French hostilities revived British intervention; disagreement among Company authorities continued.
  • Talegaon/Wadgaon, January 1779: Maratha forces associated with Mahadji Scindia and other commanders trapped Bombay army through terrain, supply denial and mobile attacks.
  • Wadgaon convention, 1779: British to return acquisitions made since 1773; Hastings rejected it. Goddard captured Ahmedabad/Bassein; Popham captured Gwalior, 1780, not 1781; Camac's campaign/Sipri, 1781, pressured Scindia.
  • Salbai, 1782, mediated by Mahadji Scindia: Madhav Rao II recognised; Raghoba pensioned; British retained Salsette/Elephanta and returned most recent conquests, including Bassein; Scindia recovered territories west of Yamuna.
  • Result: negotiated settlement, not decisive British conquest; roughly twenty years' peace between main parties. Company could concentrate on Mysore.

8. Bassein and Second Anglo-Maratha War, 1803–05

  • Madhav Rao II died, 1795; Baji Rao II became Peshwa, 1796; Nana Phadnavis died, 1800. Renewed factional rivalry undermined cooperation.
  • Vithoji Holkar executed, 1801; Yashwant Rao Holkar defeated Peshwa/Scindia at Poona, October 1802. Peshwa fled to Bassein and sought Company help.
  • Treaty of Bassein, 31 December 1802: Baji Rao II accepted subsidiary force/subsidy, restricted foreign relations and exclusion of hostile European officers. Nominal confederacy head became dependent.
  • Daulat Rao Scindia + Raghuji II Bhonsle resisted; Holkar did not initially join, Gaekwad remained apart. Lack of combined action helped Company fight separately.
  • Deccan—Arthur Wellesley: Assaye, September 1803; Argaon/Argaum, November 1803; Gawilgarh, December.
  • North—General Gerard Lake: Aligarh, Delhi, Agra; Laswari, November 1803, against Scindia's forces. Delhi/Shah Alam II came under Company protection; not every northern battle involved a combined Scindia–Bhonsle army.
  • Other operations covered Gujarat, Bundelkhand and Odisha.
  • Deogaon, December 1803—Bhonsle: ceded Cuttack/Balasore and territory west of Wardha; accepted Resident Mountstuart Elphinstone. Distinguish these terms from the fuller Nagpur subsidiary arrangement 1816.
  • Surji-Arjangaon, December 1803—Scindia: surrendered major northern/central holdings, including Ganga–Yamuna doab; Company diplomatic control increased. Burhanpur agreement, February 1804, added subsidiary arrangements; John Malcolm important in negotiations.
  • Holkar war, 1804–05: defeated Monson's retreating force near Kota; unsuccessful attempt on Delhi, defended under Ochterlony; later British successes at Deeg and Farrukhabad.
  • Costly campaigns and European pressure led to Wellesley's recall, 1805; Cornwallis briefly returned, then George Barlow pursued retrenchment.
  • Rajpurghat, 24 December 1805: peace with Holkar; relinquished northern claims, regained much territory; Company withdrew some commitments. Confederacy weakened; Company supremacy expanded, but Holkar was not finally reduced by this war alone.

9. Pindaris and Third Anglo-Maratha War, 1817–18

  • Lord Hastings, 1813–23—different from Warren Hastings. Expansion also included Anglo-Nepal War, 1814–16, and suppression of Pindaris.
  • Pindaris: mobile irregular raiding bands, often linked to Maratha armies; heterogeneous recruits included displaced soldiers/peasants. Plunder financed operations; not a single ethnic/religious community or formal regular army.
  • Leaders: Chitu, Wasil Muhammad, Karim Khan. Raids in Company territories, including Mirzapur/Shahabad, 1812, and Northern Circars, 1816, supplied justification for a large campaign.
  • 1817–18: Hastings led northern forces; Thomas Hislop commanded Deccan army. Bands dispersed/crushed; Karim surrendered, Wasil died in custody and Chitu was reportedly killed by a tiger after flight.
  • Nagpur, 27 May 1816: succession dispute after Raghuji II's death; Appa Sahib/Mudhoji II, Resident Jenkins and subsidiary force. Parsoji nominal ruler amid regency conflict.
  • Gangadhar Shastri, Gaekwad envoy, murdered at Pandharpur, 1815, not Nasik. Trimbakji Dengle implicated; Elphinstone's demands intensified Peshwa–Company conflict. Satara Gazetteer
  • Poona, 13 June 1817: Peshwa accepted further restrictions, territorial cessions and renunciation of confederacy leadership.
  • Gwalior, 5 November 1817: Scindia agreed to assist anti-Pindari campaign and accept restrictions; remained out of the principal anti-British war. This was not a Company conquest of Gwalior.
  • Peshwa—Khadki/Kirkee, 5 November 1817; Bhonsle—Sitabaldi, 26–27 November; Holkar—Mahidpur, 21 December: British victories. Peshwa had attacked/burned Poona residency.
  • Mandsaur, 6 January 1818—Malhar Rao Holkar II: subsidiary dependence, territorial cessions and British influence; Tantia Jog important in the settlement. Holkar the last major Maratha house subjected in this sequence. Jalgaon Gazetteer
  • Koregaon, 1 January 1818: Company detachment resisted Peshwa's larger force; distinguish this engagement from final surrender. Ashti, 20 February 1818: Peshwa's commander Bapu Gokhale killed.
  • Baji Rao II surrendered to Malcolm, June 1818; Peshwaship abolished, pensioned at Bithoor. Territory enlarged Bombay Presidency; Satara under Pratap Singh, Shivaji's descendant, created as a dependent kingdom.
  • Result: Maratha confederacy dismantled; British paramountcy dominant across much of India. Punjab and Sindh among major remaining powers; “every part of the subcontinent already conquered in 1818” is too sweeping.

10. Administrative reforms alongside expansion

  • Warren Hastings: Bengal governor 1772; first Governor-General of Bengal from 1774 under Regulating Act, 1773—not Governor-General of all India. Ended dual government 1772; removed deputy diwans, expanded Company revenue control and shifted treasury Murshidabad → Calcutta.
  • Revenue: five-year/quinquennial farming 1772, highest-bidder collection → excessive extraction/speculation; annual settlements from 1777. Neither was Permanent Settlement.
  • Justice: district diwani adalats—civil; faujdari adalats—criminal; appeals Sadr Diwani/Sadr Nizamat. Hindu/Muslim personal law and Islamic criminal law applied in relevant spheres.
  • Supreme Court, Calcutta, 1774: separate English-law institution; overlapping jurisdictions caused conflicts. Halhed's Code of Gentoo Laws, 1776, translated via Persian from a Sanskrit compilation.
  • Cornwallis, 1786–93: decennial revenue settlement 1790, made Permanent, 1793; zamindars recognised as proprietors subject to fixed State demand. Claimed rental division roughly 89% State/11% zamindar, not a universal tax on every peasant's produce.
  • Cornwallis Code, 1793: separated revenue collection from judicial functions; district judges, reorganisation of courts/police and criminal-law reforms. Intention in homicide mattered; harsher bodily penalties restricted; evidentiary rules changed.
  • Anti-corruption: higher salaries, restraints on private trade and gifts/property declaration. Racial exclusion reserved higher posts for Europeans; elaborate courts and language/cost barriers limited equal access.
  • Lord Hastings' period: Thomas Munro—Madras governor, 1820, extended Ryotwari, direct settlement with cultivators; earlier work with Alexander Read preceded him.
  • Holt Mackenzie's Regulation VII, 1822—Mahalwari, settlement through mahal/village proprietors/community; later developed further. Collector–magistrate combination qualified Cornwallis's separation. IGNOU: agrarian systems

11. Why Mysore and Marathas lost: balanced Mains explanation

  • Diplomacy and fragmentation: rival States/chiefs failed to sustain coordination; Company used succession disputes, separate treaties and selective alliances.
  • Fiscal resources: Bengal revenue, commercial credit, maritime links and British support sustained campaigns. Indian mobilisation was uneven; Maratha tribute networks vulnerable, Mysore's coastal losses damaging.
  • Military organisation: reliable supply, command, drill, artillery and intelligence mattered. Company armies relied heavily on Indian sepoys and allies.
  • Qualification: Mysore modernised arms/rockets and administration; Scindia had European-trained units; Marathas defeated Company at Wadgaon. Defeat was not inevitable or evidence of inherently “static Indians” versus “dynamic Europeans”.
  • Exam method: distinguish capacity from outcome; explain how Company converted particular advantages into victory through institutions, resources and diplomacy.

  • Mains 2010, Dalhousie's annexations: test the claimed contrast with predecessors—Wellesley/Hastings also expanded aggressively; distinguish war (Punjab/Lower Burma), lapse (Satara/Nagpur/Jhansi) and misgovernment claim (Awadh). Evaluate legal justifications versus imperial objectives.
  • Mains 2013, founder of modern India: assess railways, telegraph, postal/public-works and administrative changes alongside annexations and their colonial military/commercial purposes; “founder” needs qualification, not uncritical praise. Detailed Dalhousie study belongs to the later chapter.
  • Mains 2022, Company military success: structure around finance, command, logistics, training, naval support, diplomacy and fragmented opposition; use Wadgaon as counterexample, Mysore modernisation, and Indian composition of Company army.
