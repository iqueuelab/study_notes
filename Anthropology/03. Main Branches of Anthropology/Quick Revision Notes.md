# Main Branches of Anthropology

## 1. Four fields, shared questions

| Branch | Core concern | Main evidence / method |
|---|---|---|
| **Socio-cultural** | Social relationships, institutions, meanings and cultural change. | Ethnography, participant observation, interviews, surveys and archives. |
| **Biological / physical** | Human evolution, biological variation, growth and adaptation. | Fossils, anatomy, genetics, physiology and primate observation. |
| **Archaeological** | Past human life through material remains and context. | Survey, excavation, dating, artefact/environmental analysis. |
| **Linguistic** | Language, communication, thought, identity and social life. | Recorded speech, interaction/discourse analysis, comparison and fieldwork. |

**Applied anthropology spans these fields:** research used in health, development, heritage, conservation, forensic work and policy. The fields overlap; bioarchaeology, for example, connects human remains with archaeological context.

## 2. Socio-cultural anthropology

**Studies society and culture holistically:** beliefs, values, customs, traditions, kinship, economy, politics and institutions in relation to one another.

- **Methods:** sustained fieldwork, participant observation, interviews, surveys and archival research; language competence deepens interpretation.
- **Questions:** How are resources shared? How is authority legitimised? How are identities learned and contested? What changes with migration, climate or development?
- **Applications:** assess displacement and development; support community-defined priorities; understand poverty, inequality and conflict.

### Subfields and answer cues

| Subfield | Focus | Illustrative question |
|---|---|---|
| Economic | Livelihoods, production, exchange and consumption. | How do gifts and reciprocal obligations organise resources? |
| Political | Power, leadership, governance and conflict. | How is authority recognised beyond formal office? |
| Kinship | Descent, marriage, family and relatedness. | Who gains rights to land, care or inheritance? |
| Religion | Belief, ritual and symbolic practice. | How does ritual relate to social identity? |
| Gender | Gendered roles, relations and power. | How are work and decision-making distributed? |
| Medical | Health, illness and care in cultural/social context. | How do trust and stigma influence care-seeking? |
| Urban | City life, migration, neighbourhoods and institutions. | How do migrants maintain or remake social networks? |

### Thinker → emphasis

- **Malinowski:** interconnected institutions and intensive fieldwork.
- **Radcliffe-Brown:** social structure and relations.
- **Lévi-Strauss:** structural relationships underlying cultural systems and classifications.
- **Clifford Geertz:** interpretation of meanings and symbols.
- **Margaret Mead:** variation in socialisation, adolescence and gender.
- **D. N. Majumdar and S. C. Dube:** Indian communities understood in their social/cultural setting.

These are **revision paraphrases**, not authenticated verbatim definitions.

### Ethnography, ethnology and social anthropology

| Term | What it does | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ethnography** | Contextual research and its written/other representation, grounded in sustained engagement with a particular setting. | Study a Mongolian pastoral community through herding activities, interviews and ritual observation. |
| **Ethnology** | Comparative/synthetic analysis of cultures using ethnographic and other evidence. | Compare gift exchange or kinship systems across several societies. |
| **Social anthropology** | Explains social relationships and institutions, often using ethnography and comparison. | Examine how kinship shapes access to coastal fishing resources. |

- **Ethnography generates contextual accounts; ethnology compares; social anthropology investigates social organisation.** All can involve description, theory and interpretation.
- Ethnography is not mechanically “only descriptive”; comparison is not restricted to ethnology. British social and American cultural traditions differ historically, but overlap extensively today.

### Why early anthropology emphasised “primitive societies”

- **Historical setting:** late-nineteenth/early-twentieth-century colonial expansion; evolutionists such as **Morgan and Tylor** imagined a universal ladder of development.
- **Salvage anthropology:** documentation of languages and practices believed to be disappearing under colonial contact and change.
- **Contributions:** rich ethnographic records; comparative evidence on kinship, religion and institutions; later contextual approaches associated with **Boas** challenged ethnocentrism.
- **Limitations:** living communities treated as survivals of Europe's past; cultures portrayed as isolated/static; romanticisation and research serving colonial power; inadequate attention to voices, rights and agency.
- **Contemporary position:** study all societies, including cities, migration and global networks; work collaboratively and recognise change, internal diversity and community priorities.

**Do not equate “prehistoric” with living Indigenous communities.** Prehistoric refers to a relation to written records; “primitive” is a historical and often derogatory label, not a biological rank or a reliable measure of complexity. Cultural relativism aids understanding without excusing harm.

## 3. Biological / physical anthropology

**A biocultural, evolutionary study of humans and their relatives:** origins, variation, inheritance, growth, health and environmental adaptation.

| Area | Scope and methods | Recall example |
|---|---|---|
| **Palaeoanthropology** | Fossils, teeth, comparative anatomy, dating and genetic evidence reconstruct human evolution. | Australopithecus and early Homo; changes in locomotion and anatomy. |
| **Primatology** | Behaviour, ecology, anatomy and genetics of primates; field and laboratory studies. | Social organisation and cognition; compare with humans without treating living apes as human ancestors. |
| **Human biology / variation** | Population variation, growth/development, physiology and environmental responses. | Skin pigmentation, nutrition and high-altitude adaptation. |
| **Human genetics** | Genes, inheritance, population relationships, migration and disease-related variation. | Combine genetic findings with environmental/cultural context. |
| **Forensic anthropology** | Human skeletal evidence in legal identification and trauma investigation. | Estimate age/sex/stature and assess trauma with uncertainty; not every cause of death can be established from bones. |
| **Bioarchaeology** | Human remains interpreted within archaeological/social context. | Past diet, disease, mobility, demography and unequal life conditions. |
| **Health and nutrition** | Biological and cultural determinants of growth and health. | Food access, childhood growth and the effects of inequality. |

**Methods:** field observation, anthropometry, laboratory genetics/physiology, skeletal analysis and fossil study. Primatology and conservation connect human research with the wider living world.

### Classical and new physical anthropology

| Dimension | Classical tendency | New physical anthropology |
|---|---|---|
| Main question | Describe/classify bodily differences, often into racial types. | Explain population variation and evolutionary processes. |
| Evidence | Anthropometry, craniometry and osteology; limited fossil evidence. | Population genetics, evolutionary synthesis, ecology, physiology and expanded fossil data. |
| Model | Often typological/static; racial ranking occurred in influential traditions. | Population-based, dynamic and biocultural. |
| Qualification | Measurement itself is not obsolete. | New techniques do not automatically remove bias; sampling, consent and interpretation matter. |

- Developed especially around the **mid-twentieth century**, alongside population genetics and the modern evolutionary synthesis.
- **Human variation does not form a ladder of superior/inferior races.** Study ancestry, populations, environment and social histories without treating racial categories as fixed biological essences.

### Scholars and contributions

| Scholar | Safe association |
|---|---|
| **Franz Boas** | Human variation, growth and environmental influences; critique of racial determinism. Especially associated with founding American anthropology; “father” labels are context-dependent. |
| **Earnest A. Hooton** | Physical anthropology, skeletal variation and human evolution; racial/criminal-typological claims need historical criticism. |
| **William W. Howells** | Human evolution and quantitative study of cranial/population variation; not simply reducible to population genetics. |
| **Don Brothwell (1933–2016)** | Human skeletal remains, palaeopathology, past health and human ecology. [University of York](https://www.york.ac.uk/palaeo/news-events/news/2016/brothwell/). |
| **C. Owen Lovejoy** | Hominin anatomy and locomotor biomechanics, especially bipedality. The source's death date “2000” is incorrect. [Kent State profile](https://www.kent.edu/anthropology/dr-c-owen-lovejoy). |

## 4. Archaeological anthropology

**Reconstructs and explains human life through material remains:** artefacts, buildings, tools, pottery, bones, plant remains, landscapes and **middens** (refuse deposits).

- **Nature:** interdisciplinary; anthropology joins geology, chemistry, biology and environmental studies. Context and association are essential.
- **Temporal reach:** prehistoric through historical and relatively recent societies. Written evidence complements rather than eliminates archaeology.
- **Aims:** reconstruct past lifeways; explain change and variation; relate past processes to present questions; protect heritage and examine whose past is represented.

### Scope and themes

| Area | Focus |
|---|---|
| Prehistoric archaeology | Societies without usable contemporary written records. |
| Historical archaeology | Material evidence interpreted alongside documents and other records. |
| Underwater archaeology | Submerged sites, shipwrecks and associated material; specialised recording/recovery. |
| Cultural resource management (**CRM**) | Identify, assess and protect heritage threatened by construction or other change. |
| Experimental archaeology | Replicate processes to test interpretations of manufacture, use and preservation. |
| Bioarchaeology | Human remains, health, diet, demography and social conditions. |

Key themes: **cultural change; trade/contact/diffusion; settlement patterns; art/ritual/symbolism; human–environment relations**. Similar-looking objects do not alone establish the same cultural meaning.

### Old, new and later interpretive approaches

| Approach | Emphasis | Qualification |
|---|---|---|
| Traditional / culture-historical | Description, classification, chronology and reconstruction of cultural sequences. | Not every earlier archaeologist accepted a single unilinear evolution model. |
| **New Archaeology / processual**, especially 1960s | Explain processes; explicit hypotheses, systems/ecology, quantitative analysis and testing. | Interpretation still depends on sampling, preservation and assumptions. |
| Later post-processual approaches | Meaning, agency, power, identity and the researcher's standpoint. | Not synonymous with processual “New Archaeology”; different approaches can inform one investigation. |

### Scholar → contribution

- **V. Gordon Childe:** Neolithic/Urban Revolutions, technology and social transformation; bridges description and explanation.
- **Lewis Binford:** processual archaeology, scientific explanation and links between observable remains and past behaviour—not merely “understand the present”.
- **James Deetz:** historical archaeology, everyday material culture and meaning; do not reduce him to one theoretical label.
- **Timothy Earle:** complex societies, political economy, inequality and political power.
- **H. D. Sankalia and B. N. Misra:** important Indian prehistoric field research, including central Indian sites such as Bhimbetka/Adamgarh in the wider research tradition.
- **N. K. Bose:** major Indian social/cultural anthropologist; not interchangeable with the prehistoric archaeologists above.
- **Ruth Benedict:** culture-and-personality/*Patterns of Culture*; the source incorrectly places her among archaeological theorists. [Columbia University](https://anthropology.columbia.edu/content/benedict).

India's early hominin evidence, including the Narmada fossil, matters to evolutionary research; it does not establish India as an independent origin point of all modern humans or settle every fossil's species attribution.

## 5. Linguistic anthropology

**Studies language as cultural practice and social action:** how people communicate, construct identities and meanings, and reproduce or challenge relationships.

- **Language ↔ culture:** each influences the other; study speech in actual settings.
- **Communication and interaction:** conversation, discourse, face-to-face cues, social relationships and institutional settings.
- **Cognition:** acquisition, categorisation, meaning and the relationship between language and thought.

| Area | Main question |
|---|---|
| Sociolinguistics | How does language vary with class, gender, ethnicity, identity and situation? |
| Ethnolinguistics | How does language express cultural categories, values and ritual meanings? |
| Discourse analysis | How does connected language create meaning, accomplish goals and negotiate relations? |
| Cognitive linguistics | How are language, conceptualisation and mental processes related? |
| Historical linguistics | How do languages change, relate and form language families? |

### Scholar → concept

| Scholar | Recall |
|---|---|
| **Franz Boas** | Documentation of Indigenous North American languages and attention to language/culture. |
| **Edward Sapir** | Language, culture and linguistic variation; associated with linguistic relativity. |
| **Benjamin Lee Whorf** | Linguistic categories and habitual thought/perception. |
| **Dell Hymes** | Ethnography of speaking; speech events and socially situated communicative competence. |
| **John J. Gumperz (1922–2013)** | Interactional sociolinguistics and **contextualisation cues**; corrects the source's “1927–2022”. [University of California memorial](https://senate.universityofcalifornia.edu/_files/inmemoriam/html/JohnJosephGumperz.html). |

**Sapir–Whorf caution:** linguistic relativity explores influences on habitual thought; it is not proof that language rigidly determines everything a speaker can think. The label brings together related ideas rather than one jointly written formula.

**Application:** language documentation, migrant communication, education, legal/medical encounters and understanding misunderstandings across speech communities.

## 7. Last-minute recall

- **Ethnography = contextual account; ethnology = comparison; social anthropology = relationships/institutions.**
- **Classical physical = description/types; new physical = populations/processes.**
- **Old archaeology = classification/sequence; processual = explanation/testing; post-processual = meaning/agency/power.**
- **Boas—context; Malinowski—fieldwork; Radcliffe-Brown—structure; Geertz—meaning.**
- **Childe—revolutions; Binford—processual; Hymes—speaking; Gumperz—contextualisation.**
