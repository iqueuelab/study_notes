Main Branches of Anthropology

1. Four fields, shared questions

BranchCore concernMain evidence / method
Socio-culturalSocial relationships, institutions, meanings and cultural change.Ethnography, participant observation, interviews, surveys and archives.
Biological / physicalHuman evolution, biological variation, growth and adaptation.Fossils, anatomy, genetics, physiology and primate observation.
ArchaeologicalPast human life through material remains and context.Survey, excavation, dating, artefact/environmental analysis.
LinguisticLanguage, communication, thought, identity and social life.Recorded speech, interaction/discourse analysis, comparison and fieldwork.

Applied anthropology spans these fields: research used in health, development, heritage, conservation, forensic work and policy. The fields overlap; bioarchaeology, for example, connects human remains with archaeological context.

2. Socio-cultural anthropology

Studies society and culture holistically: beliefs, values, customs, traditions, kinship, economy, politics and institutions in relation to one another.

  • Methods: sustained fieldwork, participant observation, interviews, surveys and archival research; language competence deepens interpretation.
  • Questions: How are resources shared? How is authority legitimised? How are identities learned and contested? What changes with migration, climate or development?
  • Applications: assess displacement and development; support community-defined priorities; understand poverty, inequality and conflict.

Subfields and answer cues

SubfieldFocusIllustrative question
EconomicLivelihoods, production, exchange and consumption.How do gifts and reciprocal obligations organise resources?
PoliticalPower, leadership, governance and conflict.How is authority recognised beyond formal office?
KinshipDescent, marriage, family and relatedness.Who gains rights to land, care or inheritance?

MedicalHealth, illness and care in cultural/social context.How do trust and stigma influence care-seeking?

Thinker → emphasis

  • Malinowski: interconnected institutions and intensive fieldwork.
  • Radcliffe-Brown: social structure and relations.
  • Lévi-Strauss: structural relationships underlying cultural systems and classifications.
  • Clifford Geertz: interpretation of meanings and symbols.
  • Margaret Mead: variation in socialisation, adolescence and gender.
  • D. N. Majumdar and S. C. Dube: Indian communities understood in their social/cultural setting.

These are revision paraphrases, not authenticated verbatim definitions.

Ethnography, ethnology and social anthropology

TermWhat it doesExample
EthnographyContextual research and its written/other representation, grounded in sustained engagement with a particular setting.Study a Mongolian pastoral community through herding activities, interviews and ritual observation.
EthnologyComparative/synthetic analysis of cultures using ethnographic and other evidence.Compare gift exchange or kinship systems across several societies.
Social anthropologyExplains social relationships and institutions, often using ethnography and comparison.Examine how kinship shapes access to coastal fishing resources.
  • Ethnography generates contextual accounts; ethnology compares; social anthropology investigates social organisation. All can involve description, theory and interpretation.
  • Ethnography is not mechanically “only descriptive”; comparison is not restricted to ethnology. British social and American cultural traditions differ historically, but overlap extensively today.

Why early anthropology emphasised “primitive societies”

  • Historical setting: late-nineteenth/early-twentieth-century colonial expansion; evolutionists such as Morgan and Tylor imagined a universal ladder of development.
  • Salvage anthropology: documentation of languages and practices believed to be disappearing under colonial contact and change.
  • Contributions: rich ethnographic records; comparative evidence on kinship, religion and institutions; later contextual approaches associated with Boas challenged ethnocentrism.
  • Limitations: living communities treated as survivals of Europe's past; cultures portrayed as isolated/static; romanticisation and research serving colonial power; inadequate attention to voices, rights and agency.
  • Contemporary position: study all societies, including cities, migration and global networks; work collaboratively and recognise change, internal diversity and community priorities.

Do not equate “prehistoric” with living Indigenous communities. Prehistoric refers to a relation to written records; “primitive” is a historical and often derogatory label, not a biological rank or a reliable measure of complexity. Cultural relativism aids understanding without excusing harm.

3. Biological / physical anthropology

A biocultural, evolutionary study of humans and their relatives: origins, variation, inheritance, growth, health and environmental adaptation.

AreaScope and methodsRecall example
PalaeoanthropologyFossils, teeth, comparative anatomy, dating and genetic evidence reconstruct human evolution.Australopithecus and early Homo; changes in locomotion and anatomy.
PrimatologyBehaviour, ecology, anatomy and genetics of primates; field and laboratory studies.Social organisation and cognition; compare with humans without treating living apes as human ancestors.
Human biology / variationPopulation variation, growth/development, physiology and environmental responses.Skin pigmentation, nutrition and high-altitude adaptation.
Human geneticsGenes, inheritance, population relationships, migration and disease-related variation.Combine genetic findings with environmental/cultural context.
Forensic anthropologyHuman skeletal evidence in legal identification and trauma investigation.Estimate age/sex/stature and assess trauma with uncertainty; not every cause of death can be established from bones.
BioarchaeologyHuman remains interpreted within archaeological/social context.Past diet, disease, mobility, demography and unequal life conditions.
Health and nutritionBiological and cultural determinants of growth and health.Food access, childhood growth and the effects of inequality.

Methods: field observation, anthropometry, laboratory genetics/physiology, skeletal analysis and fossil study. Primatology and conservation connect human research with the wider living world.

Classical and new physical anthropology

DimensionClassical tendencyNew physical anthropology
Main questionDescribe/classify bodily differences, often into racial types.Explain population variation and evolutionary processes.
EvidenceAnthropometry, craniometry and osteology; limited fossil evidence.Population genetics, evolutionary synthesis, ecology, physiology and expanded fossil data.
ModelOften typological/static; racial ranking occurred in influential traditions.Population-based, dynamic and biocultural.
QualificationMeasurement itself is not obsolete.New techniques do not automatically remove bias; sampling, consent and interpretation matter.
  • Developed especially around the mid-twentieth century, alongside population genetics and the modern evolutionary synthesis.
  • Human variation does not form a ladder of superior/inferior races. Study ancestry, populations, environment and social histories without treating racial categories as fixed biological essences.

ScholarSafe association

4. Archaeological anthropology

Reconstructs and explains human life through material remains: artefacts, buildings, tools, pottery, bones, plant remains, landscapes and middens (refuse deposits).

  • Nature: interdisciplinary; anthropology joins geology, chemistry, biology and environmental studies. Context and association are essential.
  • Temporal reach: prehistoric through historical and relatively recent societies. Written evidence complements rather than eliminates archaeology.
  • Aims: reconstruct past lifeways; explain change and variation; relate past processes to present questions; protect heritage and examine whose past is represented.

Scope and themes

AreaFocus
Prehistoric archaeologySocieties without usable contemporary written records.
Historical archaeologyMaterial evidence interpreted alongside documents and other records.
Underwater archaeologySubmerged sites, shipwrecks and associated material; specialised recording/recovery.
Cultural resource management (CRM)Identify, assess and protect heritage threatened by construction or other change.
Experimental archaeologyReplicate processes to test interpretations of manufacture, use and preservation.
BioarchaeologyHuman remains, health, diet, demography and social conditions.

Key themes: cultural change; trade/contact/diffusion; settlement patterns; art/ritual/symbolism; human–environment relations. Similar-looking objects do not alone establish the same cultural meaning.

Old, new and later interpretive approaches

ApproachEmphasisQualification
Traditional / culture-historicalDescription, classification, chronology and reconstruction of cultural sequences.Not every earlier archaeologist accepted a single unilinear evolution model.
New Archaeology / processual, especially 1960sExplain processes; explicit hypotheses, systems/ecology, quantitative analysis and testing.Interpretation still depends on sampling, preservation and assumptions.
Later post-processual approachesMeaning, agency, power, identity and the researcher's standpoint.Not synonymous with processual “New Archaeology”; different approaches can inform one investigation.

Scholar → contribution

  • V. Gordon Childe: Neolithic/Urban Revolutions, technology and social transformation; bridges description and explanation.
  • Lewis Binford: processual archaeology, scientific explanation and links between observable remains and past behaviour—not merely “understand the present”.
  • James Deetz: historical archaeology, everyday material culture and meaning; do not reduce him to one theoretical label.
  • Timothy Earle: complex societies, political economy, inequality and political power.
  • H. D. Sankalia and B. N. Misra: important Indian prehistoric field research, including central Indian sites such as Bhimbetka/Adamgarh in the wider research tradition.
  • N. K. Bose: major Indian social/cultural anthropologist; not interchangeable with the prehistoric archaeologists above.
  • Ruth Benedict: culture-and-personality/Patterns of Culture; the source incorrectly places her among archaeological theorists. Columbia University.

India's early hominin evidence, including the Narmada fossil, matters to evolutionary research; it does not establish India as an independent origin point of all modern humans or settle every fossil's species attribution.

5. Linguistic anthropology

Studies language as cultural practice and social action: how people communicate, construct identities and meanings, and reproduce or challenge relationships.

  • Language ↔ culture: each influences the other; study speech in actual settings.
  • Communication and interaction: conversation, discourse, face-to-face cues, social relationships and institutional settings.
  • Cognition: acquisition, categorisation, meaning and the relationship between language and thought.
AreaMain question
SociolinguisticsHow does language vary with class, gender, ethnicity, identity and situation?
EthnolinguisticsHow does language express cultural categories, values and ritual meanings?
Discourse analysisHow does connected language create meaning, accomplish goals and negotiate relations?
Cognitive linguisticsHow are language, conceptualisation and mental processes related?
Historical linguisticsHow do languages change, relate and form language families?

Scholar → concept

ScholarRecall
Franz BoasDocumentation of Indigenous North American languages and attention to language/culture.
Edward SapirLanguage, culture and linguistic variation; associated with linguistic relativity.
Benjamin Lee WhorfLinguistic categories and habitual thought/perception.
Dell HymesEthnography of speaking; speech events and socially situated communicative competence.
John J. Gumperz (1922–2013)Interactional sociolinguistics and contextualisation cues; corrects the source's “1927–2022”. University of California memorial.

Sapir–Whorf caution: linguistic relativity explores influences on habitual thought; it is not proof that language rigidly determines everything a speaker can think. The label brings together related ideas rather than one jointly written formula.

Application: language documentation, migrant communication, education, legal/medical encounters and understanding misunderstandings across speech communities.

7. Last-minute recall

  • Ethnography = contextual account; ethnology = comparison; social anthropology = relationships/institutions.
  • Classical physical = description/types; new physical = populations/processes.
  • Old archaeology = classification/sequence; processual = explanation/testing; post-processual = meaning/agency/power.
  • Boas—context; Malinowski—fieldwork; Radcliffe-Brown—structure; Geertz—meaning.
  • Childe—revolutions; Binford—processual; Hymes—speaking; Gumperz—contextualisation.
