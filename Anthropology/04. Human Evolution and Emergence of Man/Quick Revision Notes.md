# Human Evolution and Emergence of Man

## 1. The central idea: biocultural evolution

**Human evolution is the interaction of inherited biological change and socially learned culture.** Biological evolution changes populations across generations; learning changes individuals' behaviour and can alter the environment in which selection operates.

- **Sherwood Washburn:** biocultural feedback and the “new physical anthropology”; study adaptation and evolutionary processes rather than merely classifying human types.
- **Biology enables culture:** bipedal locomotion, dexterous hands, cognitive capacities, communication and prolonged learning/development.
- **Culture changes selection pressures:** tools, cooking, food production, settlement, disease exposure and social organisation reshape survival/reproduction.
- Individuals do not acquire a larger heritable brain by thinking harder. Mutations are not produced because an organism needs a trait; natural selection changes the representation of heritable variants.
- Humans share ancestry with other primates; **we did not descend from living chimpanzees**. Evolution is a branching history, not a ladder ending in humans.

<!-- flow: Biocultural feedback | Inherited variation and developmental capacities | Learned technology, diet, cooperation and communication | Modified environment and ways of obtaining resources | Changed survival and reproductive opportunities | Selection and population change across generations; new cultural possibilities -->

### Examples and limits

| Interaction | Revision point |
|---|---|
| **Dairying ↔ lactase persistence** | Most infants produce lactase; many adults reduce its production. In some dairying populations, variants maintaining adult expression spread. Culture changed selection; milk drinking did not purposefully create the mutation |
| **Cooking / food processing ↔ anatomy** | Softer, more accessible food can change energetic and chewing demands; relevant to teeth, jaws and digestion. Exact timing and contribution to brain evolution remain debated |
| **Tools ↔ hands, cognition and cooperation** | Technology extends action and transmits knowledge; feedback involves anatomy, learning, environment and social behaviour, not a single “tool gene” |
| **Settlement / agriculture ↔ disease** | Population density, animal contact and diet change pathogen exposure and selection. Population resistance, individual immunity and public-health practices are distinct |
| **Long childhood ↔ social learning** | Prolonged dependence creates opportunities/costs of learning and care; cooperative caregiving matters, not necessarily one universal household form |

**Chronology trap:** dairying, adult lactase-persistence selection and most farming/town examples belong mainly to the **Holocene**, not the Pleistocene. They remain useful examples of biocultural evolution.

## 2. Biology versus culture

| Dimension | Biological evolution | Cultural change |
|---|---|---|
| Unit / information | Alleles, genotypes and inherited developmental systems | Learned knowledge, practices, values and technologies |
| Transmission | Primarily genetic parent–offspring transmission | Vertical, horizontal and oblique social learning |
| Mechanisms | Mutation, selection, drift and gene flow; recombination reshuffles variation | Innovation, teaching, imitation, diffusion and differential adoption |
| Pace | Can be rapid or slow; measured across generations | Often faster, but traditions can persist |
| Direction | No predetermined goal or automatic progress | Can be planned or unintended; not necessarily improvement |
| Individual versus population | Individuals develop/acclimatise; populations evolve | Individuals learn; practices spread/change in groups |

**Genotype ≠ phenotype.** Phenotype reflects genotype, environment, development and their interaction. **Plasticity** is a capacity to develop differently under different conditions; not every plastic response is genetically inherited. Humans continue to evolve even when culture buffers environmental stress.

## 3. Pleistocene: setting of much human evolution

**Pleistocene: c.2.58 million–11,700 years ago**; repeated glacial/interglacial cycles, not one continuous worldwide ice cover. **Eocene: c.56–34 million years ago**, relevant to early primate diversification; it did not begin over 60 million years ago.

- Hominin evolution began before the Pleistocene. Bipedalism preceded the largest increases in brain size; australopiths were not a single species or a uniformly “cultureless” stage.
- Pleistocene changes included dispersal of **Homo**, tool diversification, changing diet, fire use, cooperation, shelters/clothing in some settings and eventually **Homo sapiens** dispersal from Africa.
- Fossils and archaeology permit inference, but precise origins of language, marriage, religion or a universal sex-based division of labour cannot be read directly from bones.

| Archaeological association | Useful recall / qualification |
|---|---|
| **Oldowan** | Core-and-flake technology; associated with early Homo and other possible makers. “First tools = only Homo habilis” is too restrictive |
| **Acheulean** | Bifacial handaxes/cleavers; often associated with Homo erectus and later hominins; not every Homo erectus population used it |
| **Middle Palaeolithic / Mousterian** | Prepared-core/flake traditions; important Neanderthal associations, but not exclusive to one species. **Not Lower Palaeolithic**, as the source table implies |
| **Upper Palaeolithic** | Blades, diverse tools and prominent symbolic/artistic records, often associated with Homo sapiens |
| **Mesolithic** | Microliths, varied foraging and regional settlement changes |
| **Neolithic** | Farming/herding and more settled life in many regions; polished tools are a common association, not a universal definition |
| **Bronze Age** | Metallurgy, craft specialisation and urban/state formations in some regions; no global simultaneous sequence |

### Food, fire, brain and social life

- Foraging requires ecological memory, planning and learning. Hunting/scavenging could favour coordination and technological skill; meat was one resource among plant and animal foods.
- Fire offers warmth, predator deterrence, light, cooking and gathering places. Cooking can improve food accessibility and reduce chewing costs; adoption was uneven and its earliest timing is debated.
- Larger brains impose energetic/developmental costs; childbirth reflects pelvic dimensions, fetal size, metabolism and other factors. **“Hunting caused brain growth → women became immobile → compulsory male provisioning → marriage” is a historical model, not an established universal sequence.**
- Maternal care, male investment and help from other caregivers vary. Do not treat a particular gender division, incest rule or marriage form as a direct inevitable biological outcome.
- Brain regions in the chapter: **frontal—planning/executive and motor functions; parietal—somatosensory/spatial integration; temporal—auditory, memory and language-related functions; occipital—vision; cerebellum—coordination and learning**. These functions operate through networks, not isolated one-function boxes.
- Language supports cumulative knowledge and cooperation; **writing is a much later cultural invention**, not a prerequisite for human language. Social learning/tool use also occur in other animals; human cumulative culture has unusual scale and complexity.

## 4. Before Darwin: organise ideas by contribution

| Thinker / tradition | Main idea and evaluation |
|---|---|
| **Mythological accounts** | Indian **panchabhutas**, Dashavatara, Chinese elemental cosmologies, Egyptian/Babylonian creation narratives explain origins within cultural/religious systems. Dashavatara is not fossil evidence or a tested evolutionary mechanism |
| **Thales / Anaximander** | Water/primordial origins; Anaximander speculated on aquatic beginnings of humans. Important naturalistic speculation, not modern population genetics |
| **Xenophanes** | Fossil marine shells suggested past environmental change |
| **Empedocles** | Elements, combination and survival of suitable forms; an early precursor, not Darwinian selection in its modern sense |
| **Aristotle, 384–322 BCE** | Classification and ordered nature/scala naturae; not modern branching common descent. Humans are **viviparous**, not “ovoviviparous” |
| **Lucretius** | *De Rerum Natura*: naturalistic origins and change; separate poetic/philosophical speculation from empirical evolutionary theory |
| **Special creation / fixity** | Species created in present form; biblical chronologies included **James Ussher's 4004 BCE** calculation, published **1650**. Historical belief, not scientific age of Earth; “Jim Usher” is a source error |
| **Hutton / Lyell** | Deep geological time and processes operating over long periods. Hutton's **1785** presentation; Lyell's *Principles of Geology*, **1830–33**, helped supply Darwin's time framework |
| **Maupertuis / Buffon** | Heredity, variation and possible species change; precursors rather than a complete modern synthesis |
| **Cuvier, 1769–1832** | Comparative anatomy, extinction and **catastrophism**; opposed species transformation. Fossil replacement after catastrophes is not evolution by acquired characters |
| **Erasmus Darwin** | Suggested transformation, common ancestry and environmental adaptation; an intellectual precursor, not discoverer of the full Darwin–Wallace mechanism |

**Spontaneous generation** proposed ordinary life emerging directly from non-living material. It is distinct from both evolutionary change among existing organisms and modern research into life's remote origin. The chapter's claim that Galileo was executed is false; he was tried and placed under house arrest.

## 5. Lamarckism: mechanism, importance and criticism

**Jean-Baptiste Lamarck, 1744–1829; *Philosophie zoologique*, 1809.** One of the earliest systematic transformist theories.

<!-- flow: Lamarck's historical explanation | Environmental change creates new needs and habits | Use strengthens organs; disuse weakens them | Acquired modifications assumed inherited | Successive generations change; tendency towards complexity postulated -->

- Classic **giraffe** illustration: repeated stretching → longer neck acquired → descendants inherit it. This explains the historical claim, not the accepted genetic mechanism.
- **Value:** rejected simple species fixity, placed adaptation/environment in the explanation, developed invertebrate classification and a systematic account of transformation.
- **Context:** natural-history observation, Buffon/Erasmus Darwin, fossils/deep time, environmental change and Enlightenment-era ideas about transformation.
- Important works: **Flore française, 1778; Hydrogéologie, 1802; Philosophie zoologique, 1809**. Lamarck and **Treviranus** independently used “biology” in its modern disciplinary sense around **1802**; claims that Lamarck alone coined every relevant term are unreliable. **Eduard Suess**, not Lamarck, coined “biosphere”.
- **Criticism:** bodily use can enlarge a muscle within a lifetime, but does not establish inheritance of the acquired enlargement. No general mechanism links a need/use-induced bodily modification to the same directed inherited change; variation, selection and genetics offer stronger explanations.
- **Weismann's germ-plasm distinction:** soma and germ line differ; tail-cutting examples challenge inheritance of mutilation, though mutilation alone is not a complete test of every environmental-inheritance claim.
- **Epigenetic inheritance does not reinstate all Lamarckism.** Some regulatory marks persist under some circumstances, many reset; distinguish direct developmental exposure from inheritance across unexposed generations. No proof that desired beneficial changes are generally produced and inherited.

### Neo-Lamarckism and experimental claims

| Case | What to remember |
|---|---|
| **Lysenko** | Environmentally induced hereditary change claims received political backing in Soviet agriculture; suppression of genetics is a caution about evidence and political authority |
| **McDougall's rats** | Claimed progressively improved maze/escape learning across generations. Controls and replication matter; does not establish inheritance of learned knowledge |
| **Sumner's temperature experiments** | Body proportions changed with rearing conditions; offspring returning towards usual proportions illustrates difference between plasticity and inherited adaptation |
| **Tower, Kammerer, Lindsey, Griffith claims** | Historical experiments reported in the source on beetles, salamanders and other organisms; not accepted collectively as conclusive proof of acquired-character inheritance. Do not repeat precise outcomes as settled modern findings |
| **Baldwin effect** | Learning can improve survival and change selection on inherited learning capacity or associated traits. **The learned act itself need not become genetically inherited** |
| **Cultural inheritance** | Knowledge transmitted socially; real intergenerational transmission does not mean DNA carries the acquired idea |

## 6. Darwin and Wallace: natural selection

**Charles Darwin (1809–1882)** and **Alfred Russel Wallace** independently developed natural-selection explanations; joint communication **1858**; Darwin's *On the Origin of Species*, **1859**.

<!-- flow: Natural selection | Individuals vary; some variation is heritable | Resources and reproductive opportunities are limited | Heritable variants differ in reproductive success in a given environment | Descendants inherit those variants | Allele and trait frequencies can change across generations -->

- **Fitness = relative reproductive contribution**, not simply strength, longevity or moral superiority. Survival matters through reproduction; competition need not mean physical fighting.
- Mutation generates new alleles; **selection sorts existing heritable variation**. Variants are not generated because beneficial changes are needed.
- **Giraffe contrast:** selection on inherited variation in ancestral populations, rather than neck stretching being automatically inherited. A single simplified feeding story does not explain every aspect of giraffe evolution.
- **Population thinking:** individuals vary; populations change through time. **Common descent and branching diversification** replace fixed ideal types and a predetermined hierarchy.
- **Achievements:** adaptation, common ancestry and diversification within a naturalistic framework; evidence from fossils, anatomy, biogeography and breeding.
- **Limits of original Darwinism:** no adequate Mendelian account of heredity or molecular mutation, incomplete treatment of drift and mechanisms of reproductive isolation. Modern genetics strengthens and revises the framework; not every evolutionary change is adaptive.

## 7. Modern synthesis: Darwinism plus genetics

**1930s–1940s synthesis:** natural selection + Mendelian inheritance + population genetics + systematics + palaeontology. It was a collaborative development, not a single invention in 1937.

| Contributor | Association |
|---|---|
| **Fisher, Haldane, Sewall Wright** | Mathematical population genetics; selection, variation and drift |
| **Theodosius Dobzhansky** | *Genetics and the Origin of Species*, **1937** |
| **Ernst Mayr** | Species, systematics, isolation and speciation |
| **Julian Huxley** | *Evolution: The Modern Synthesis*, **1942** |
| **George Gaylord Simpson** | Palaeontology and evolutionary tempo/pattern |
| **G. Ledyard Stebbins** | Plant evolution, variation and speciation |

**Core:** produce/redistribute variation → selection and stochastic processes change populations → barriers to gene flow can permit divergence and speciation. **Microevolution and macroevolution are connected**, not “genetic change versus unrelated morphological change”.

### Mechanisms: do not confuse their jobs

| Mechanism | Effect / example |
|---|---|
| **Mutation** | New DNA-sequence variation; point changes, insertions/deletions and larger changes. Effects can be beneficial, neutral or deleterious in context. Germ-line mutations can be transmitted in sexually reproducing humans; most somatic changes are not |
| **Recombination** | Crossing over, independent assortment and fertilisation reshuffle existing alleles; creates combinations, **does not by itself require change in allele frequency** |
| **Gene flow** | Alleles move between breeding populations through successful reproduction; movement without genetic contribution is not gene flow |
| **Genetic drift** | Random sampling changes allele frequencies; especially strong in small **effective** populations; can fix/lose alleles irrespective of advantage |
| **Founder effect** | New population founded by a small, non-representative sample; special drift setting, not a guarantee of greater diversity |
| **Bottleneck** | Sharp reduction of population size changes sampling/variation; population may regrow without recovering lost diversity |
| **Natural selection** | Non-random differences in reproductive success associated with heritable traits; context dependent |
| **Hybridisation** | Crossing differentiated populations/species can redistribute variation; may yield fertile, partly fertile or sterile offspring. Not simply any mating between unrelated people |

**Chromosomal changes:** **deletion, duplication, inversion, translocation**, ring/isochromosome formation; number changes include **euploidy/polyploidy** (whole sets) and **aneuploidy** (individual chromosome gain/loss: monosomy, trisomy, nullisomy). Polyploidy can be important in plant speciation; do not assume a common successful human pathway.

### Hardy–Weinberg: the reference model

For a two-allele autosomal locus: **p + q = 1**; expected genotype frequencies **p² (AA) + 2pq (Aa) + q² (aa) = 1**.

- Ideal assumptions: **very large population, random mating, no mutation, no migration/gene flow, no selection**. Large size makes drift negligible. The source omits **no selection**; include it.
- Example: **p = 0.6, q = 0.4 → AA 0.36, Aa 0.48, aa 0.16**.
- It is a null/reference model, not a claim that every natural population is at equilibrium. **Non-random mating can alter genotype proportions without directly changing allele frequencies**; equilibrium deviations and allele-frequency evolution are not identical tests.

## 8. Isolation and speciation

**Biological species concept:** groups of actually/potentially interbreeding natural populations reproductively isolated from other such groups. Useful for many sexual organisms; difficult for fossils, asexual lineages and some hybridising groups.

| Barrier / route | Example / significance |
|---|---|
| **Geographical** | River, island, mountain or distance reduces contact; **allopatric divergence** possible, but geography alone does not guarantee new species |
| **Habitat/ecological** | Different niches/host plants/microhabitats reduce encounters, even within the same broad region |
| **Temporal** | Different flowering/breeding times |
| **Behavioural** | Different courtship signals or mating calls |
| **Mechanical / gametic** | Mating or fertilisation incompatibility |
| **Postzygotic** | Reduced hybrid viability/fertility or later-generation breakdown |

<!-- flow: A possible speciation route | Gene flow reduced between populations | Mutation, drift and selection generate divergence | Reproductive barriers develop | Divergent populations remain distinct even after renewed contact -->

- Geographic, ecological and reproductive isolation are related concepts, not identical steps required in one fixed order. Speciation can also occur without a geographical barrier.
- **Genotype–environment interaction:** Richard **Lewontin** emphasised development and environment; no one-to-one rule that each gene produces one fixed trait.
- Different human populations are **not different biological species**. Human cultural isolation/endogamy can affect frequencies without implying a ladder of “races” or superiority.

## 9. Microevolution, macroevolution and later developments

| Concept | Meaning / correction |
|---|---|
| **Microevolution** | Allele-frequency change within populations; investigated through genomes, pedigrees, blood groups and other variation. Not restricted to a fixed short duration |
| **Macroevolution** | Speciation, extinction, diversification and long-term patterns at/above species level; fossils, phylogenies, comparative and molecular data. Not only past events or only bones |
| **Punctuated equilibrium—Eldredge and Gould, 1972** | Long stasis interrupted by relatively rapid change associated with speciation on geological scales; not instantaneous transformation, and not a concept the original 1940s synthesis had already “settled” |
| **Neutral theory—Motoo Kimura, 1968** | Much molecular substitution explained by drift of selectively neutral/nearly neutral variants; does not deny selection on adaptations |
| **Endosymbiosis—Lynn Margulis** | Mitochondria/chloroplasts descend from bacterial endosymbionts; present organelles are **not free-living prokaryotic organisms** |
| **Kin selection / inclusive fitness—W.D. Hamilton** | Helping can spread when its relatedness-weighted benefits outweigh costs; **rB > C**. Not a conscious plan to preserve genes |
| **Reciprocal altruism / multilevel selection** | Other frameworks for cooperation; distinguish reciprocity from helping relatives. George C. Williams contributed to adaptation/selection debates; do not substitute him for Hamilton's key kin-selection formulation |
| **Evo-devo** | Evolution of developmental mechanisms/gene regulation shapes form; developmental constraints and plasticity matter |

**Mendel's work:** presented **1865**, published **1866**. **Hugo de Vries' mutation theory, 1901–03**, emphasised discontinuous variation; modern mutation genetics is broader than his original saltationist theory.

## 10. Rules and patterns: learn the contrasts

| Term | Meaning | Example / limit |
|---|---|---|
| **Dollo's rule** | Exact reconstruction of a complex ancestral condition through the same historical path is highly improbable | Whales returned to water without becoming ancestral gill-breathing fish. Not a ban on reverse mutation or reappearance of any trait |
| **Cope's rule** | Tendency towards increased body size in some lineages over time | Some mammal trends; not universal. Dwarfing, ecology and flight constraints supply counterexamples; “only unspecialised organisms survive” is false |
| **Gause's principle** | Competitors with identical limiting-resource requirements cannot stably coexist indefinitely under constant conditions | Competitive exclusion; partitioning niches can enable coexistence. **Not a definition of adaptive radiation** |
| **Parallel evolution** | Similar traits independently evolve from comparable ancestral starting points, often in related lineages | Repeated adaptations within related groups; precise distinction from convergence can depend on trait/developmental level |
| **Convergence** | Independent evolution of similar adaptive features in different lineages | Streamlined **shark, ichthyosaur, dolphin**; wings of birds/bats as flight structures. Their underlying forelimb bones are homologous |
| **Adaptive radiation** | Common ancestral lineage diversifies into forms occupying different niches | Darwin's finches; primate ecological diversification |
| **Mosaic evolution** | Different anatomical/functional systems evolve at different rates/times | Hominin bipedal adaptations preceded major brain expansion; not every trait changes together |

**Homology = shared ancestry of the feature; analogy = functional similarity independently acquired.** Convergent wings can share homologous underlying limb bones. Palaemon is a **crustacean**, not an insect; omit it as a model fish-shaped vertebrate.

## 11. Adaptive radiation and primate examples

**Ecological opportunity + heritable variation + differential adaptation + lineage separation → diversification.** New habitats, altered resources or key innovations can create opportunities; no requirement that every ancestor was universally “unspecialised”. **Osborn** associated with early twentieth-century discussion; **Simpson** with adaptive zones and diversification.

### Finch niches

- **Ground finches:** seed-crushing bills vary with seed size/hardness.
- **Warbler finches:** slender insect-foraging bills.
- **Vegetarian/tree finches:** feeding specialisations involving plant foods or insects.
- **Woodpecker finches:** extract invertebrates, including tool-assisted foraging.
- **Cactus finches:** elongated bills for cactus-associated resources.

Beak differences, behaviour and diet reflect ecological diversification; categories are a revision guide, not a fixed complete modern species count.

### Primate niches and movement

| Group / adaptation | Recall |
|---|---|
| **Madagascar lemurs** | Radiation into different dietary/activity/locomotor niches. **Aye-aye:** specialised incisors and thin finger for extractive foraging; **ring-tailed lemur:** mixed terrestrial/arboreal activity; **indri/sifaka:** vertical clinging and leaping |
| **New World monkeys** | Central/South America: **howlers—leaf-rich diet; spider monkeys—fruit-rich diet and suspensory movement**. More than just two families; do not memorise the source's incomplete family list |
| **Tarsiers** | **Southeast Asia**, large eyes and faunivory; not Madagascar lemurs |
| **Chimpanzees / bonobos / gorillas** | African apes; differing diets, locomotion and social patterns. Avoid a rigid “peaceful bonobo versus always violent chimpanzee” caricature |
| **Orangutans** | Southeast Asian great apes, flexible arboreal climbing/suspension; not African apes |
| **Gibbons / siamangs** | **Hylobatidae**, specialised **brachiation**; long arms and mobile shoulders |
| **Humans** | Apes with habitual terrestrial **bipedalism**; heel-to-toe plantigrade walking, **not toe-walking** |

- Modern major primate division: **Strepsirrhini** and **Haplorhini**. Traditional “Prosimii/Anthropoidea” terminology is historically useful but does not capture tarsiers' placement well.
- Grasping hands/feet in many primates, nails/tactile pads, vision, flexible limbs and learning favour arboreal tasks. Locomotion varies: **quadrupedalism, clinging/leaping, suspension/brachiation, knuckle-walking and bipedalism**.
- Mammalian radiation illustrates modified homologous limbs: **arboreal climbers, bats in flight, cursorial runners, fossorial burrowers and aquatic swimmers**. Kangaroos are marsupials, so do not put them inside a list exclusively of placental mammals.
- **Plantigrade:** whole sole; **digitigrade:** digits; **unguligrade:** tips of hooved digits. Artiodactyl/perissodactyl refers broadly to even/odd-toed groups, not every member having exactly two/one toes.
- **Ancestral/derived** describes relationship to a specified ancestor, not inferior/superior. Living apes are independently evolved relatives, not unchanged human ancestors. No universal claim that the human brain is “twelve times any primate brain” is valid.
