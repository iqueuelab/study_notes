Prehistoric Cultures in India

1. The sequence at a glance

Tool technology + subsistence + archaeological context identify a culture. Regional phases overlap; they are not a single nationwide timetable.

PhaseConventional chapter rangeMain recall
Palaeolithic / Old Stone AgeGlobally, roughly 2.5 million years ago to 10,000 BCE in the chapter schemeHunter-gatherers; chipped stone tools. This is not a securely dated Indian starting point.
Lower PalaeolithicEnds around 100,000 BCE in the simplified schemeHandaxes, cleavers, choppers; Acheulian industry.
Middle Palaeolithicc.100,000–40,000 BCE in the simplified schemeFlakes, points, scrapers; prepared cores/Levallois.
Upper Palaeolithicc.40,000–10,000 BCEBlades, burins, bone tools; ornaments and symbolic expression.
Mesolithic / Middle Stone Agec.10,000–7000 BCE in the chapter schemeMicroliths; hunting, fishing, gathering; some animal keeping. Continued much later in several regions.
Neolithic / New Stone Agec.7000–2000 BCE, with regional variationFood production, animal keeping, village life, polished tools.
Chalcolithic / Copper-Stone AgeBroadly c.3000–700 BCECopper + stone; regional farming villages.
Iron AgeBroadly c.1500–200 BCE in the chapter schemeRegional iron use; overlaps later Chalcolithic cultures.
  • Pleistocene broadly frames Palaeolithic developments; Holocene frames much Mesolithic/Neolithic activity. Tool phases do not map exactly onto geological subdivisions.
  • Do not memorise 100,000 BCE as the first Indian Middle Palaeolithic everywhere: Attirampakkam research dates the transition to about 385 ±64 thousand years ago, with that sequence continuing to about 172 thousand years ago. Original study.
  • Chalcolithic villages and the Bronze Age Harappan civilisation overlapped. The Iron Age followed the Mature Harappan phase; it did not precede the rise of the Indus civilisation.

2. Evidence and the pioneers

  • Prehistory: past before usable written records; reconstructed through tools, layers, bones, seeds, houses, burials and art. Harappan writing remains undeciphered → protohistory, rather than the beginning of fully readable Indian written history at 2500 BCE.
  • John Lubbock, Pre-historic Times (1865): introduced the Palaeolithic/Neolithic terminology. “Old/new” refers to archaeological divisions, not human worth.
  • Robert Bruce Foote: found a Palaeolithic handaxe at Pallavaram near Chennai, 30 May 1863; known as the Father of Indian Prehistory. First recognised discovery does not mean the oldest tool by archaeological age. Government Museum, Chennai.
  • H. D. Sankalia: identified Middle Palaeolithic flake industries at Nevasa on the Pravara, Maharashtra, in 1956; associated with the term Nevasian.
  • A. C. L. Carlyle, Cunningham's assistant: early recognition of microliths and rock paintings in the Vindhyan/Mirzapur shelters; pigment-grinding traces, human/animal remains, ash and charcoal supplied context.

3. Palaeolithic life and tools

  • Economy: mobile hunting and gathering of wild foods; open-air camps, river valleys, caves and shelters. Regular farming and pottery are not characteristic of the Indian Palaeolithic.
  • Quartzite was widely used, hence the older label “quartzite men”; limestone, chert and other available rocks were also used. This is a material label, not an ethnic group.
  • Do not assign all early Indians to a single “Negrito race”. That typology is outdated; tools alone cannot establish biological ancestry.
  • Homo sapiens evolved in Africa around 300,000 years ago—not first during the Upper Palaeolithic at 40,000 BCE. Tool industries and biological species are different classifications. Smithsonian.

4. Mesolithic: small tools, diverse livelihoods

  • Post-glacial warming changed vegetation, fauna and water availability. Communities hunted, fished and gathered; some adopted animal keeping and increasingly persistent settlements.
  • Microliths of chert/flint and other rocks could be hafted in wood/bone as arrowheads, spear points or cutting edges. Bone/antler tools supplemented stone.
  • Burials, hut floors and food remains reveal community life and diet. Major Ganga-plain settlements belong to this period; this is not proof that no earlier humans ever entered the entire plain.
  • Pottery is absent from many early assemblages; it occurs in some later contexts, including Langhnaj. Pottery alone does not establish farming.
  • Animal skins could be used as clothing; no secure basis for claiming clothing began only in this phase.

Art: subjects → social evidence

  • Rock paintings depict animals, hunting, dancing, gathering and group activities. Movement, composition, simplified forms and use of shelter surfaces show aesthetic choices.
  • Bhimbetka: UNESCO describes paintings from the Mesolithic through historical periods; do not date all paintings to one phase. UNESCO.
  • Rock art can suggest cooperation, livelihoods and beliefs. It does not by itself prove a fixed gender division of labour or an exact religious doctrine.
  • Mesolithic art did not mark the global beginning of painting. Natural painted shelters also differ from later deliberately excavated rock-cut caves.

5. Neolithic: food production and villages

Neo = new; lithic = stone. “Neolithic revolution” describes major changes in subsistence and society, occurring unevenly rather than simultaneously.

  • Tools: ground/polished axes and adzes, sickles, grinding stones; older microliths and bone needles/points/borers continued.
  • Food: farming and herding supplemented rather than always eliminated hunting/gathering. Surplus storage and processing supported village life.
  • Houses: circular/rectangular mud, reed and wattle-and-daub structures; pit dwellings in Kashmir. House form depended on region and conditions.
  • Crafts: pottery for cooking/storage; grey, black-burnished and mat-impressed wares in regional assemblages. Early phases could be aceramic; handmade and wheel-made traditions did not begin everywhere together.
  • Spinning, weaving, watercraft and wheeled transport developed in different settings; they are not a compulsory package at every early Neolithic site.

6. Chalcolithic: copper did not replace stone

  • Copper + stone tools; predominantly rural settlements near rivers/hills. Farming, herding, hunting and fishing; pottery, beads and craft exchange.
  • Major regional traditions: Ahar–Banas (Rajasthan), Kayatha/Malwa (central India), Savalda/Jorwe (Maharashtra), Ganeshwar–Jodhpura (Rajasthan).
  • Broad chapter range c.3000–700 BCE; Ganeshwar–Jodhpura c.2800–2200 BCE is a conventional regional range, not the starting date of copper use everywhere.
  • Chalcolithic is a technological/cultural category: many villages coexisted with Harappan Bronze Age towns. It is not a uniform stepping stone that everywhere ended before bronze began.
  • Copper-hoard anthropomorph: stylised human-shaped object; Madarpur, Moradabad, Uttar Pradesh is an important findspot.

7. Exam practice and answer framework

CSE Mains 2015 · GS I · 12.5 marks · 200 words

“Mesolithic rock cut architecture of India not only reflects the cultural life of the times but also a fine aesthetic sense comparable to modern painting. Critically evaluate this comment.”

  • Open with painted natural rock shelters, exemplified by Bhimbetka; distinguish them from later excavated rock-cut architecture.
  • Cultural life: hunting, animals, group dancing and gathering suggest subsistence, cooperation and social occasions.
  • Aesthetics: expressive outlines, movement, composition and use of rock contours; simple forms can convey complex action.
  • Critique: scenes are selective; dates/interpretations vary, and “comparable” does not establish the same purpose or historical context as modern painting.
  • End: art is valuable evidence of material and imaginative life, read with tools, burials and environmental remains.

Supplied other-exam questions

1. CDS II 2023 — Levallois technique

Consider the following statements regarding the ‘Levallois Technique’:

1. It refers to making perforated Harappa pottery.

2. It refers to making prehistoric flake tools.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only; (b) 2 only; (c) Both 1 and 2; (d) Neither 1 nor 2.

Answer: (b). Prepared-core flake production; not pottery.

2. CDS II 2023 — Palaeolithic tools

1. Isampur was a well-known center of stone tool manufacture located in Gulbarga district of Karnataka and was situated along a small seasonal stream known as Kamta Halla.

2. A Paleolithic blade is a flake tool, the length of which is more than twice its width.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only; (b) 2 only; (c) Both 1 and 2; (d) Neither 1 nor 2.

Answer: (c), as supplied. Isampur = Karnataka quarry/workshop; blade = elongated flake.

3. CAPF 2023 — Neolithic period

Which one among the following statements with reference to the ‘Neolithic Period’ is not correct?

(a) The characteristic features of the period included ground and polished stone tools, agriculture, animal domestication and pottery.

(b) This period is also known as the ‘New Stone Age’.

(c) The characteristic features appeared almost at the same time in various parts of the subcontinent.

(d) Earliest evidence of agriculture is found from Mehrgarh around 8000 BCE.

Answer: (c), as supplied. Regional transitions were uneven. Option (d)'s date is reproduced faithfully; for revision use the conventional c.7000 BCE beginning of Mehrgarh, with dating discussions kept distinct from an exam's supplied wording.

8. Last-minute recall

  • Handaxe → flake → blade/burin → microlith → polished celt → copper + stone.
  • Isampur: quarry; Nevasa: Nevasian; Patne: ostrich eggshell; Baghor I: possible shrine.
  • Bagor: Rajasthan; Langhnaj: Gujarat; Sarai Nahar Rai–Mahadaha–Damdama: Uttar Pradesh.
  • Mehrgarh: wheat/barley; Burzahom: pits; Koldihwa: rice evidence; Utnur–Budihal: ashmounds.
  • Ahar–Gilund–Balathal: Rajasthan; Navdatoli–Eran: Malwa; Inamgaon–Jorwe–Daimabad: Maharashtra.
  • A tool's shape, a site's age and a hominin's species are separate questions. Not all microliths are exclusively Mesolithic; not all Neolithic communities had pottery from the outset.
