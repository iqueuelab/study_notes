# Prehistoric Cultures in India

## 1. The sequence at a glance

**Tool technology + subsistence + archaeological context identify a culture. Regional phases overlap; they are not a single nationwide timetable.**

<!-- flow: Tool sequence | Lower Palaeolithic: handaxe and cleaver | Middle Palaeolithic: prepared-core flakes | Upper Palaeolithic: blades and burins | Mesolithic: microliths and composite tools | Neolithic: ground/polished celts | Chalcolithic: copper alongside stone -->

| Phase | Conventional chapter range | Main recall |
|---|---|---|
| Palaeolithic / Old Stone Age | Globally, roughly 2.5 million years ago to 10,000 BCE in the chapter scheme | Hunter-gatherers; chipped stone tools. This is not a securely dated Indian starting point. |
| Lower Palaeolithic | Ends around 100,000 BCE in the simplified scheme | Handaxes, cleavers, choppers; Acheulian industry. |
| Middle Palaeolithic | c.100,000–40,000 BCE in the simplified scheme | Flakes, points, scrapers; prepared cores/Levallois. |
| Upper Palaeolithic | c.40,000–10,000 BCE | Blades, burins, bone tools; ornaments and symbolic expression. |
| Mesolithic / Middle Stone Age | c.10,000–7000 BCE in the chapter scheme | Microliths; hunting, fishing, gathering; some animal keeping. Continued much later in several regions. |
| Neolithic / New Stone Age | c.7000–2000 BCE, with regional variation | Food production, animal keeping, village life, polished tools. |
| Chalcolithic / Copper-Stone Age | Broadly c.3000–700 BCE | Copper + stone; regional farming villages. |
| Iron Age | Broadly c.1500–200 BCE in the chapter scheme | Regional iron use; overlaps later Chalcolithic cultures. |

- **Pleistocene** broadly frames Palaeolithic developments; **Holocene** frames much Mesolithic/Neolithic activity. Tool phases do not map exactly onto geological subdivisions.
- **Do not memorise 100,000 BCE as the first Indian Middle Palaeolithic everywhere:** Attirampakkam research dates the transition to about **385 ±64 thousand years ago**, with that sequence continuing to about 172 thousand years ago. [Original study](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature25444).
- Chalcolithic villages and the Bronze Age Harappan civilisation **overlapped**. The Iron Age followed the Mature Harappan phase; it did not precede the rise of the Indus civilisation.

## 2. Evidence and the pioneers

- **Prehistory:** past before usable written records; reconstructed through tools, layers, bones, seeds, houses, burials and art. **Harappan writing remains undeciphered → protohistory**, rather than the beginning of fully readable Indian written history at 2500 BCE.
- **John Lubbock, Pre-historic Times (1865):** introduced the Palaeolithic/Neolithic terminology. “Old/new” refers to archaeological divisions, not human worth.
- **Robert Bruce Foote:** found a Palaeolithic handaxe at **Pallavaram near Chennai, 30 May 1863**; known as the **Father of Indian Prehistory**. First recognised discovery does not mean the oldest tool by archaeological age. [Government Museum, Chennai](https://govtmuseumchennai.org/uploads/topics/16527888885312.pdf).
- **H. D. Sankalia:** identified Middle Palaeolithic flake industries at **Nevasa on the Pravara, Maharashtra**, in 1956; associated with the term **Nevasian**.
- **A. C. L. Carlyle**, Cunningham's assistant: early recognition of microliths and rock paintings in the **Vindhyan/Mirzapur** shelters; pigment-grinding traces, human/animal remains, ash and charcoal supplied context.

## 3. Palaeolithic life and tools

- **Economy:** mobile hunting and gathering of wild foods; open-air camps, river valleys, caves and shelters. Regular farming and pottery are not characteristic of the Indian Palaeolithic.
- Quartzite was widely used, hence the older label **“quartzite men”**; limestone, chert and other available rocks were also used. This is a material label, not an ethnic group.
- Do not assign all early Indians to a single “Negrito race”. That typology is outdated; tools alone cannot establish biological ancestry.
- **Homo sapiens evolved in Africa around 300,000 years ago**—not first during the Upper Palaeolithic at 40,000 BCE. Tool industries and biological species are different classifications. [Smithsonian](https://humanorigins.si.edu/evidence/human-fossils/species/homo-sapiens).

### Tool vocabulary

| Tool / technique | Identification and use |
|---|---|
| Core and flake | Core = stone from which pieces are struck; flake = detached piece, often retouched for use. |
| Handaxe / cleaver | Bifacially worked handaxe; cleaver has a broad transverse cutting edge. Typical Acheulian forms. |
| Chopper / scraper | Chopper: worked cutting edge on a cobble; scraper: retouched edge for tasks such as working hides/wood. |
| Levallois | Prepare the core's shape/surface before striking a flake of intended form; **not perforated Harappan pottery**. |
| Blade | Elongated flake, conventionally at least about twice as long as wide; many have roughly parallel sides. |
| Burin | Chisel-like stone edge used for engraving/working hard material such as bone. **A burin is not itself necessarily a bone tool.** |
| Point / borer | Pointed working end for piercing or other tasks; use depends on wear/context. |
| Microlith | Small stone implement, often around 1 cm, frequently hafted into a composite tool; sizes vary. |
| Celt | Ground/polished axe or adze form, strongly associated with Neolithic toolkits. |

### Lower Palaeolithic: heavy cutting tools

Handaxes, cleavers, choppers and scrapers; much of the phase lies in the Pleistocene. **Acheulian** is characterised especially by bifacial handaxes/cleavers; not every Lower Palaeolithic assemblage is identical.

| Site / region | State | Recall |
|---|---|---|
| Isampur, Hunsgi valley | Karnataka | Limestone quarry/workshop; handaxes, cleavers, flakes and production waste; near seasonal **Kamta Halla**. Direct evidence of tool manufacture. |
| Attirampakkam | Tamil Nadu, near Chennai | Stratified Acheulian tools and later prepared-core technologies; key long sequence. |
| Belan valley | Uttar Pradesh | River-terrace tools; succession across later stone-tool phases. |
| Adamgarh hills | Madhya Pradesh | Handaxes, cleavers and flakes; later Mesolithic occupation/art. |

### Middle Palaeolithic: flakes and prepared cores

Flake-based points, scrapers and borers; generally smaller/lighter tools. Prepared, discoidal and Levallois cores; some Late Acheulian forms continued, so replacement was not instant.

| Site / region | State | Recall |
|---|---|---|
| Nevasa; Chirki–Nevasa basin | Maharashtra | Pravara valley; chalcedony/jasper flakes and scrapers; **Nevasian industry**. |
| Belan valley | Uttar Pradesh | Quartzite/chert flakes, scrapers and borers; stratified continuity. |
| Narmada valley | Madhya Pradesh | Flake tools; **Narmada hominin fossil** is important Middle Pleistocene evidence. Its precise species attribution remains debated. |
| Didwana; Luni basin | Rajasthan | Flakes, points and cores; adaptation to desert-edge/semi-arid settings. |
| Son valley | Madhya Pradesh–Uttar Pradesh region | Gravel-deposit flakes/blades; technological sequences. |
| Tirunelveli basin | Tamil Nadu | Flake/prepared-core technologies; southern distribution. |
| Attirampakkam | Tamil Nadu | Dated early Middle Palaeolithic transition; do not restrict it to the chapter's simplified 100,000–40,000 BCE range. |

### Upper Palaeolithic: blades, bone working and symbolism

Blade–burin industries, finer implements, ornaments and symbolic activity. Bone points, needles and fishing implements occur in broader Upper Palaeolithic toolkits; their presence must be established site by site.

| Site / region | State | Recall |
|---|---|---|
| Bhimbetka, Raisen | Madhya Pradesh | Long occupation sequence, blades/burins and rock art; surviving paintings span several periods. |
| Belan valley | Uttar Pradesh | Blades, scrapers and points; continuity into later phases. |
| Patne, Jalgaon | Maharashtra | Blade industry, beads and **engraved ostrich eggshell**—important early symbolic expression. |
| Baghor I, Son valley | Madhya Pradesh | Stone tools and a stone arrangement interpreted as a **possible shrine**; an interpretation, not proven ritual meaning. |
| Kurnool caves | Andhra Pradesh | Bone tools, blades and animal remains; cave-based hunting economy. |
| Mahadeo hills / Adamgarh | Madhya Pradesh | Blade–burin tools and habitation evidence; regional sequences. |
| Godavari valley | Telangana / Andhra Pradesh | Blade–flake industries using quartz/chert; smaller tools in later assemblages. |
| Chitrakoot region | Uttar Pradesh–Madhya Pradesh | Blades, scrapers, burins; open-air/workshop evidence. |
| Didwana / Luni basin | Rajasthan | Blades, burins and later small-tool traditions in dry environments. |
| Tirunelveli basin | Tamil Nadu | Blade/small-tool traditions; southern spread. |

## 4. Mesolithic: small tools, diverse livelihoods

- Post-glacial warming changed vegetation, fauna and water availability. Communities hunted, fished and gathered; some adopted animal keeping and increasingly persistent settlements.
- **Microliths** of chert/flint and other rocks could be hafted in wood/bone as arrowheads, spear points or cutting edges. Bone/antler tools supplemented stone.
- Burials, hut floors and food remains reveal community life and diet. Major Ganga-plain settlements belong to this period; this is not proof that no earlier humans ever entered the entire plain.
- Pottery is absent from many early assemblages; it occurs in some later contexts, including **Langhnaj**. Pottery alone does not establish farming.
- Animal skins could be used as clothing; no secure basis for claiming clothing began only in this phase.

| Site | State | Recall |
|---|---|---|
| Bagor | Rajasthan | Large Mesolithic settlement: microliths, huts, burials, animal bones; evidence of animal keeping in its sequence. |
| Langhnaj | Gujarat | Microliths, human burials, wild fauna; pottery in the site's sequence. |
| Adamgarh | Madhya Pradesh | Painted shelters and microliths; long cultural sequence. |
| Sarai Nahar Rai | Uttar Pradesh | Human burials, animal bones and microliths; community life in the Ganga valley. |
| Mahadaha | Uttar Pradesh | Burials, habitation floors and microliths; do not infer domestication from settlement alone. |
| Damdama | Uttar Pradesh | Skeletons, microliths and varied fauna; dietary diversity. |
| Teri sites | Tamil Nadu | Red sand dunes, microliths and shells; coastal adaptation. |
| Chopani Mando | Belan valley, Uttar Pradesh | Hut floors, bone tools, microliths and burnt daub with **reed impressions** → wattle-and-daub construction. |
| Birbhanpur | West Bengal | Microliths and habitation deposits; eastern distribution. |
| Padri; Loteshwar | Gujarat | Microliths, fauna and pottery in later sequences; contacts with farming communities. |

### Art: subjects → social evidence

- Rock paintings depict **animals, hunting, dancing, gathering and group activities**. Movement, composition, simplified forms and use of shelter surfaces show aesthetic choices.
- **Bhimbetka:** UNESCO describes paintings from the **Mesolithic through historical periods**; do not date all paintings to one phase. [UNESCO](https://whc.unesco.org/en/list/925/).
- Rock art can suggest cooperation, livelihoods and beliefs. It does not by itself prove a fixed gender division of labour or an exact religious doctrine.
- Mesolithic art did **not** mark the global beginning of painting. Natural painted shelters also differ from later deliberately excavated rock-cut caves.

## 5. Neolithic: food production and villages

**Neo = new; lithic = stone. “Neolithic revolution” describes major changes in subsistence and society, occurring unevenly rather than simultaneously.**

<!-- flow: From foraging to village economies | Cultivation and animal keeping expand | More persistent settlement | Storage, grinding and food processing | Pottery and specialised household crafts | Exchange and more complex community organisation -->

- **Tools:** ground/polished axes and adzes, sickles, grinding stones; older microliths and bone needles/points/borers continued.
- **Food:** farming and herding supplemented rather than always eliminated hunting/gathering. Surplus storage and processing supported village life.
- **Houses:** circular/rectangular mud, reed and wattle-and-daub structures; pit dwellings in Kashmir. House form depended on region and conditions.
- **Crafts:** pottery for cooking/storage; grey, black-burnished and mat-impressed wares in regional assemblages. Early phases could be **aceramic**; handmade and wheel-made traditions did not begin everywhere together.
- Spinning, weaving, watercraft and wheeled transport developed in different settings; they are **not a compulsory package at every early Neolithic site**.

| Site / group | Region | Recall |
|---|---|---|
| Mehrgarh | Balochistan, Pakistan, near Bolan Pass | Early farming village, conventionally from **c.7000 BCE**; wheat/barley, sheep/goats/cattle, mud-brick houses, burials and ornaments. Early levels precede pottery. |
| Burzahom | Kashmir | **Pit dwellings**, bone tools, polished axes; human/animal burials, including dogs. |
| Mahagara | Uttar Pradesh | Circular huts, polished tools and cattle-related evidence; Ganga-valley farming settlement. |
| Koldihwa | Belan valley, Uttar Pradesh | Rice-husk impressions in pottery. Early dates/domestication status are debated: avoid an unqualified “oldest cultivated rice” claim. |
| Belan valley | Uttar Pradesh | Long Mesolithic–Neolithic sequence; animals, pottery, microliths and settlement. |
| Utnur / Utnoor | Telangana | **Ashmound**, huts, stone axes and cattle remains; southern agro-pastoralism. |
| Kallur | Karnataka | Southern Neolithic setting; millets/handmade pottery in the chapter account. Iron belongs to later levels, not the defining Neolithic toolkit. |
| Maski | Karnataka | Stone axes, grains and animal remains; southern Neolithic complex. |
| Sanganakallu–Kupgal | Karnataka | Extensive settlement/tool-production complex; stone tools, cattle and ashmounds. |
| Watgal | Karnataka | Microlithic tools, urn/extended burials and grave pots; reported carbonised **betel-nut seeds**. |
| Budihal | Karnataka | Ashmound, chert working, grinding grooves, bone tools and child burials; evidence interpreted as **community feasting**; wild fruits such as ber and amla. |

- **Southern ashmounds:** accumulated burnt cattle dung is central to interpretation; pastoral, seasonal and possible ritual activities matter. Not simply heaps of domestic hearth ash. Southern Neolithic traditions flourished especially during the **third–second millennia BCE**. [INFLIBNET, Ravi Korisettar](https://ebooks.inflibnet.ac.in/icp02/chapter/87/).
- **Rice trap:** cultivation, consumption and full domestication are different claims; secure dating and plant morphology matter. [Archaeobotanical research](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7773629/).

## 6. Chalcolithic: copper did not replace stone

- **Copper + stone** tools; predominantly rural settlements near rivers/hills. Farming, herding, hunting and fishing; pottery, beads and craft exchange.
- Major regional traditions: **Ahar–Banas (Rajasthan), Kayatha/Malwa (central India), Savalda/Jorwe (Maharashtra), Ganeshwar–Jodhpura (Rajasthan)**.
- Broad chapter range **c.3000–700 BCE**; **Ganeshwar–Jodhpura c.2800–2200 BCE** is a conventional regional range, not the starting date of copper use everywhere.
- Chalcolithic is a technological/cultural category: many villages coexisted with Harappan Bronze Age towns. It is not a uniform stepping stone that everywhere ended before bronze began.
- **Copper-hoard anthropomorph:** stylised human-shaped object; **Madarpur, Moradabad, Uttar Pradesh** is an important findspot.

| Site / culture | State | Recall |
|---|---|---|
| Ahar / Ahar–Banas | Rajasthan | Black-and-red ware, copper, mud houses and stone blades; farming/metallurgy. |
| Balathal | Rajasthan | Fortification, copper tools, cattle remains and storage structures. |
| Gilund | Rajasthan | Ahar–Banas: storage complex, terracotta figurines and seal impressions; exchange/craft activity. |
| Kayatha | Madhya Pradesh | Early central Indian Chalcolithic culture; painted pottery, copper and blades. |
| Navdatoli | Madhya Pradesh | Malwa culture; circular huts, microliths, painted pottery; wheat/barley/pulses and livestock. |
| Eran | Madhya Pradesh | Malwa-related painted pottery, copper and beads. The supplied “Ernabad” is treated as a site-name error. |
| Inamgaon | Maharashtra, Ghod valley | Major excavated Malwa–Jorwe farming settlement; houses, storage, tools and distinctive burials. **Jorwe, not Inamgaon, is the type-site of Jorwe culture.** |
| Jorwe | Maharashtra | Type-site; painted pottery with geometric designs and copper objects. |
| Nevasa | Maharashtra | Malwa/Jorwe pottery, microliths and copper; multi-period occupation. |
| Daimabad | Maharashtra | Large settlement; metal animal figures—**elephant, rhinoceros, buffalo**—and a wheeled vehicle/charioteer; important metalwork. |
| Savalda | Maharashtra | Earlier regional pottery tradition; red painted/handmade wares; precedes Jorwe in regional sequences. |
| Ganeshwar–Jodhpura | Rajasthan | Numerous copper arrowheads/fishhooks and other objects, pottery and microliths; links to wider Harappan-period exchange. |
| Nokoda; Ojiyana | Rajasthan | Ahar–Banas-associated sites named in the chapter. |
| Prabhas Patan / Somnath | Gujarat | Coastal Chalcolithic tradition; black-and-red ware and copper. |
| Tekkalkota / Tekkalakota | Karnataka | Southern Neolithic–Chalcolithic sequence; copper, pottery and fauna. |

## 7. Exam practice and answer framework

### CSE Mains 2015 · GS I · 12.5 marks · 200 words

**“Mesolithic rock cut architecture of India not only reflects the cultural life of the times but also a fine aesthetic sense comparable to modern painting. Critically evaluate this comment.”**

- Open with painted **natural rock shelters**, exemplified by Bhimbetka; distinguish them from later excavated rock-cut architecture.
- **Cultural life:** hunting, animals, group dancing and gathering suggest subsistence, cooperation and social occasions.
- **Aesthetics:** expressive outlines, movement, composition and use of rock contours; simple forms can convey complex action.
- **Critique:** scenes are selective; dates/interpretations vary, and “comparable” does not establish the same purpose or historical context as modern painting.
- End: art is valuable evidence of material and imaginative life, read with tools, burials and environmental remains.

### Supplied other-exam questions

**1. CDS II 2023 — Levallois technique**

Consider the following statements regarding the ‘Levallois Technique’:

1. It refers to making perforated Harappa pottery.
2. It refers to making prehistoric flake tools.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only; (b) 2 only; (c) Both 1 and 2; (d) Neither 1 nor 2.

**Answer: (b).** Prepared-core flake production; not pottery.

**2. CDS II 2023 — Palaeolithic tools**

1. Isampur was a well-known center of stone tool manufacture located in Gulbarga district of Karnataka and was situated along a small seasonal stream known as Kamta Halla.
2. A Paleolithic blade is a flake tool, the length of which is more than twice its width.

Which of the statements given above is/are correct?

(a) 1 only; (b) 2 only; (c) Both 1 and 2; (d) Neither 1 nor 2.

**Answer: (c), as supplied.** Isampur = Karnataka quarry/workshop; blade = elongated flake.

**3. CAPF 2023 — Neolithic period**

Which one among the following statements with reference to the ‘Neolithic Period’ is **not correct**?

(a) The characteristic features of the period included ground and polished stone tools, agriculture, animal domestication and pottery.

(b) This period is also known as the ‘New Stone Age’.

(c) The characteristic features appeared almost at the same time in various parts of the subcontinent.

(d) Earliest evidence of agriculture is found from Mehrgarh around 8000 BCE.

**Answer: (c), as supplied.** Regional transitions were uneven. Option (d)'s date is reproduced faithfully; for revision use the conventional **c.7000 BCE** beginning of Mehrgarh, with dating discussions kept distinct from an exam's supplied wording.

## 8. Last-minute recall

- **Handaxe → flake → blade/burin → microlith → polished celt → copper + stone.**
- **Isampur:** quarry; **Nevasa:** Nevasian; **Patne:** ostrich eggshell; **Baghor I:** possible shrine.
- **Bagor:** Rajasthan; **Langhnaj:** Gujarat; **Sarai Nahar Rai–Mahadaha–Damdama:** Uttar Pradesh.
- **Mehrgarh:** wheat/barley; **Burzahom:** pits; **Koldihwa:** rice evidence; **Utnur–Budihal:** ashmounds.
- **Ahar–Gilund–Balathal:** Rajasthan; **Navdatoli–Eran:** Malwa; **Inamgaon–Jorwe–Daimabad:** Maharashtra.
- A tool's shape, a site's age and a hominin's species are separate questions. **Not all microliths are exclusively Mesolithic; not all Neolithic communities had pottery from the outset.**
