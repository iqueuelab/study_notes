Basic Structure of the Constitution

1. Core rule

  • Article 368 permits amendment, not destruction of the Constitution's identity. Parliament may amend any provision, including Fundamental Rights, subject to the basic structure.
  • Kesavananda Bharati (1973): foundation of the doctrine. The Court identifies protected features through cases; there is no exhaustive, closed list.
  • Neither every existing provision nor every policy is automatically a basic feature. Examine the amendment's effect on constitutional fundamentals.
  • The doctrine limits constituent power and protects constitutional supremacy; it does not make every constitutional provision unchangeable.

2. Evolution: the essential chronology

  • Shankari Prasad (1951): upheld First Amendment; “law” under 13 referred to ordinary legislation, not a constitutional amendment. Parliament could amend Fundamental Rights.
  • Sajjan Singh: upheld Seventeenth Amendment and reaffirmed that approach. Judgment delivered 30 October 1964, reported 1965—the two year labels refer to the same case. Separate opinions raised doubts about altering fundamental constitutional features.
  • Golaknath (1967): treated amendment as “law” for 13; Parliament could not abridge Fundamental Rights. Prospective overruling preserved earlier amendments.
  • 24th Amendment (1971): expressly affirmed Parliament's constituent power in 368; excluded constitutional amendments from 13; made presidential assent obligatory.
  • Kesavananda Bharati (24 April 1973): overruled Golaknath's prohibition, upheld the 24th Amendment, but imposed the basic-structure limit.
  • Indira Nehru Gandhi v. Raj Narain (1975): invalidated 329A(4) inserted by 39th Amendment, which insulated/validated the Prime Minister's election against the judicial process. Do not describe the whole 39th Amendment as struck down.
  • 42nd Amendment (1976): 368(4)/(5) sought to exclude amendment review and declare unlimited constituent power.
  • Minerva Mills (1980): invalidated those clauses; limited amending power itself is basic structure. Also invalidated the extension of 31C protection to every DPSP; protected the balance between Parts III and IV.
  • Waman Rao (1980 decision; reported 1981): reaffirmed the doctrine and distinguished Ninth-Schedule additions by the 24 April 1973 cutoff.

3. Basic features: remember in five groups

  • Constitution and law: supremacy of Constitution; rule of law; limited amending power; separation of powers; judicial review.
  • Democratic government: sovereign, democratic, republican character; parliamentary system; free and fair elections.
  • Union and society: federal character; unity and integrity; secular character; welfare State and socio-economic justice.
  • Person and rights: individual freedom and dignity; equality; essence of Fundamental Rights; harmony/balance of Fundamental Rights and DPSPs.
  • Courts and remedies: judicial independence; effective access to justice; Supreme Court powers under 32, 136, 141, 142; High Court jurisdiction under 226, 227.
  • These are judicially recognised features, not a separate constitutional Schedule. Several overlap; avoid treating a mnemonic as a rigid legal taxonomy.

4. Case map: all chapter cases

  • Kesavananda Bharati (1973): constitutional supremacy, separation, republican democracy, secularism, federalism, sovereignty/unity, individual dignity/freedom, welfare and parliamentary government.
  • Indira Nehru Gandhi (1975): democracy, equality, secular freedom of conscience, rule of law, judicial review and free/fair elections.
  • Minerva Mills (1980): limited amendment power, judicial review and FR–DPSP balance.
  • Central Coal Fields: welfare and socio-economic justice. Bhim Singhji: rule of law.
  • S.P. Sampath Kumar: judicial review; P. Sambamurthy: rule of law and judicial review. Read tribunal arrangements with the later L. Chandra Kumar ruling, not as a blanket exclusion of High Courts.
  • Delhi Judicial Service Association (1991): Supreme Court powers under 32/136/141/142.
  • Indra Sawhney (1992): rule of law; Indra Sawhney II (1999): equality. Kumar Padma Prasad (1992): judicial independence.
  • Kihoto Hollohan (1992): democratic framework and free/fair elections. Its judicial-review exclusion also illustrates the separate need for State ratification where the federal proviso applies.
  • Raghunath Rao (1993): equality and unity/integrity in the privy-purse abolition challenge.
  • S.R. Bommai (1994): federalism, secularism, democracy, unity/integrity, social justice and judicial review.
  • L. Chandra Kumar (1997): High Court review under 226/227; tribunal decisions remain subject to the constitutional courts' scrutiny.
  • All India Judges' Association: judicial independence. Kuldip Nayar (2006): democracy and free/fair elections; every alteration of an electoral rule is not necessarily basic-structure destruction.
  • M. Nagaraj (2006): equality; constitutional identity must survive an amendment.
  • I.R. Coelho (2007): rule of law, separation, judicial review and the essence of rights; Ninth-Schedule protection is not absolute.
  • Ram Jethmalani (2011): Article 32's remedial importance. Namit Sharma (2012): freedom/dignity; its specific Information Commission directions were modified on review in 2013, so do not assume every original direction remains operative.
  • Madras Bar Association (2014): judicial review and High Court jurisdiction under 226/227; tribunal design must respect judicial independence.

5. Ninth Schedule: the exam distinction

  • 31B protects scheduled laws from the specified Part III invalidity; the Schedule is not an immunity from all constitutional scrutiny.
  • For laws inserted after 24 April 1973, courts can examine whether the amendment's effect on rights damages basic structure: Waman Rao → I.R. Coelho.
  • The cutoff concerns insertion into the Ninth Schedule, not merely the original date of the ordinary law.
  • A Fundamental Rights violation and basic-structure destruction are related but not automatically identical tests.
  • Ordinary legislation is ordinarily tested against constitutional provisions and legislative competence; do not mechanically apply the amendment doctrine to every statute as an independent free-standing test.

6. One-minute recall / Mains frame

  • 1951/1964–65: amend Rights allowed → 1967: prohibited → 1971: constituent power express → 1973: amend, but preserve basic structure → 1980: unlimited-power claim rejected.
  • No Article expressly lists basic structure; its foundation is judicial interpretation of amendment power.
  • Mains: need for constitutional change → limited constituent power → key cases/features → democracy, federalism and rights protected → concern about judicial discretion → reasoned, case-specific scrutiny.
