Parliament

1. Structure and representation

  • Parliament = President + Rajya Sabha (RS) + Lok Sabha (LS), Article 79; Part V, 79–122. President belongs to neither House, but assent, summoning/prorogation, LS dissolution, addresses/messages and ordinances link the office to Parliament.
  • LS: popular/lower/first chamber, represents the people. RS: federal/upper/second chamber, represents States/UTs and brings nominated expertise. Hindi names adopted in 1954; both Houses first constituted in 1952.
  • British model: Crown + Lords + Commons; executive–legislature interdependence. US Congress: Senate + House of Representatives; President outside legislature. India's parliamentary model is often called Westminster.
  • RS maximum 250 = 238 elected + 12 nominated. Allocated strength 245 = 225 State + 8 UT + 12 nominated; vacancies can reduce actual membership. Fourth Schedule allocates seats; representation is unequal, broadly population-based (UP 31; Tripura 1), unlike US Senate's two per State.
  • State RS representatives elected by elected MLAs, using proportional representation–single transferable vote (PR–STV). Delhi, Puducherry and J&K have allocated RS representation; UT representatives chosen under the statutory electoral-college system. RS elections use open ballot, unlike the secret presidential ballot.
  • President nominates 12 RS members for special knowledge/practical experience in literature, science, art and social service; sports is not separately listed. US Senate has no nominated members.
  • LS constitutional ceiling 550 = up to 530 State + up to 20 UT seats; allocated elected seats 543 = 524 State + 19 UT. Direct territorial elections; UT direct elections governed by the 1965 Act.
  • Adult suffrage: eligible Indian citizen 18 or older; 61st Amendment, 1988, lowered voting age from 21. Candidate age is different: LS 25, RS 30.
  • Anglo-Indian nomination ceased on 25 January 2020: 104th Amendment (2019) did not extend it. Old maximum 552 included two nominees. Anglo-Indian definition refers to European paternal ancestry, Indian domicile and habitual-resident parentage; this is historical constitutional context, not a present nomination route.

2. LS elections, delimitation and reservation

  • Single-member constituencies + first-past-the-post (FPTP): candidate with most votes wins; need not obtain over 50%. Advantage: clear territorial link and simple ballot. Limitation: seat share may diverge from vote share/minority representation.
  • PR: seeks representation closer to vote shares; major models STV and party lists. RS/Legislative Councils use STV; presidential/VP elections use preferential STV for a single office, not a party-list system.
  • Chapter's reasons against LS PR: complexity/low literacy at framing and risk of fragmented governments. Other textbook criticisms: cost, weaker constituency link, party/group dominance and difficulty organising by-elections. Not universal defects of every PR model; RS vacancies, for example, are filled by bye-elections.
  • 81: seat–population ratio broadly uniform between States; exception for States under six million population. Constituency–population ratios broadly uniform within a State.
  • 82: census-based readjustment by authority/procedure Parliament provides. Delimitation Acts: 1952, 1962, 1972, 2002.
  • 42nd Amendment, 1976: froze allocation/boundaries at 1971 level. 84th, 2001: extended allocation freeze and allowed boundary rationalisation with 1991 data. 87th, 2003: substituted 2001 census for boundary/SC–ST seat readjustment, without changing State-wise LS seat allocation.
  • Allocation freeze is tied to publication of figures of the first census after 2026; it does not automatically end on 1 January 2027. Special statutory delimitation exercises must be distinguished from a nationwide reallocation of seats.
  • SC/ST reserved seats: broadly proportionate to their population; all eligible constituency voters vote, not separate electorates. Eligible SC/ST candidates can also contest general seats, subject to statutory qualifications. 104th Amendment extended reservation to 2030.
  • 106th Amendment, 2023: one-third women's reservation in LS, including within SC/ST reserved seats; also State Assemblies and Delhi Assembly. 330A/334A: operation depends on delimitation after publication of relevant first post-amendment census figures; not immediate reservation in every election. RS is excluded. Official constitutional text.

3. Duration, qualifications and disqualification

  • RS permanent; never dissolved. Members normally six years, approximately one-third retire every two years; unlimited re-election/renomination. Term fixed by RPA 1951, not directly by Constitution. First retirement batches determined by lot; 1952 Term of Office Order.
  • LS five years from first meeting, unless earlier dissolved. During National Emergency, Parliament may extend by law one year at a time, never beyond six months after Emergency ends. Fifth LS was extended twice in 1976 but dissolved 18 January 1977, after five years, ten months and six days. Dissolution is not categorically immune from constitutional judicial review.
  • 84 eligibility: Indian citizen; election-stage allegiance/sovereignty-integrity oath before ECI-authorised person; LS 25/RS 30; parliamentary statutory qualifications. Must be registered parliamentary elector. RS candidate's same-State residence/elector requirement removed in 2003, upheld in Kuldip Nayar, 2006.
  • Reserved-seat qualifications arise under RPA: chapter's broad SC/ST rule has special State/tribal-area exceptions; do not assume any SC/ST member can contest every reserved seat nationwide.
  • 102 disqualifications: office of profit (ministership excepted; Parliament can exempt offices); unsound mind declared by competent court; undischarged insolvency; non-citizenship/foreign citizenship or allegiance; statutory disqualification.
  • RPA disqualifications: specified convictions/electoral offences/corrupt practices; other convictions carrying at least two years' imprisonment; failure to lodge election expenses; specified government contracts; managing agent/manager/secretary in a company with government share ≥25%; dismissal for corruption/disloyalty; specified social offences, including untouchability/dowry/sati. Not every ordinary directorship or economic interest automatically disqualifies; preventive detention alone is not conviction-based disqualification.
  • 103: President decides post-election 102(1) disqualification questions according to ECI opinion; constitutional review remains possible. Election petitions concerning invalid election go to High Court, with appeal to SC.
  • Defection, Tenth Schedule: voluntarily giving up party membership; voting/abstaining against party direction without permission (unless condoned within 15 days); independent member joining a party; nominated member joining after six months from taking seat. Presiding officer decides; Kihoto Hollohan, 1992, permits judicial review. Merger protection requires at least two-thirds of legislature party; old one-third split exemption removed.

4. Vacant seats, oaths and remuneration

  • Both Houses: cannot retain dual membership; on simultaneous election intimate choice within 10 days, otherwise RS seat becomes vacant. Existing member elected to other House vacates earlier seat; elected to two constituencies must choose or lose both under statutory rules.
  • Parliament + State legislature: parliamentary seat falls vacant unless State seat resigned within 14 days, as computed under applicable rules.
  • 101: disqualification; written resignation to Speaker/Chairman, effective upon acceptance (must be voluntary/genuine); House may declare vacancy after 60 days' unauthorised absence. Exclude prorogation and adjournments over four consecutive days in calculating absence.
  • Other vacancies: election voided, expulsion, election as President/VP or appointment as Governor.
  • 99 membership oath: before President/appointed person; Constitution allegiance, sovereignty/integrity and faithful discharge. Distinguish election-stage oath from oath before taking seat.
  • 104 penalty ₹500 per day: knowingly sitting/voting before required oath, while unqualified/disqualified or legally prohibited. Chapter OCR can misread this as ₹7,500.
  • 106: Parliament fixes pay/allowances; pension is statutory, introduced 1976, not constitutional entitlement. 1954 MP Act, 1953 Officers of Parliament Act.
  • Updated rates, effective 1 April 2023: monthly MP salary ₹1,24,000; daily allowance ₹2,500; constituency allowance ₹87,000/month; office expenses ₹75,000/month; base ex-MP pension ₹31,000/month, additional ₹2,500/month per year beyond five years. These replace chapter's ₹1 lakh/₹2,000/₹70,000/₹60,000/₹25,000/₹2,000 figures. Periodic revision uses Cost Inflation Index. Ministry of Parliamentary Affairs.
  • Travel, accommodation, communication and medical facilities governed by law/rules. Presiding officers' salaries/allowances charged on Consolidated Fund, not annually voted. RS Chairman salary ₹4 lakh/month (2018 amendment, effective 1 January 2016); other officers' salary linked to MPs. Chapter's sumptuary figures—Speaker ₹2,000, deputies ₹1,000/month—are separate statutory allowances, not salary totals.

5. Speaker and Deputy Speaker

  • LS elects both from its members, as soon as may be. President fixes Speaker election date; Speaker fixes Deputy election date. No additional office oath beyond MP oath.
  • Vacate on ceasing LS membership, resignation (Speaker → Deputy; Deputy → Speaker), or removal by majority of all then members = effective majority, after 14 days' notice. Speaker-removal leave requires 50 supporting members under LS rules.
  • Speaker survives dissolution until immediately before new LS's first meeting. Deputy is responsible to House, not subordinate to Speaker; acts when Speaker absent/office vacant; has chairmanship privilege when appointed to a parliamentary committee.
  • Speaker maintains order/decorum; interprets constitutional/rule provisions within House; admits business/secret sittings, enforces quorum, supervises committees, appoints their chairpersons under rules, certifies Money Bills, decides defection cases and chairs joint sittings.
  • Ex-officio chair: Business Advisory, Rules, General Purposes Committees, Indian Parliamentary Group and presiding officers' conference.
  • Normally no first-instance vote; casting vote on tie. During consideration of own removal, Speaker cannot preside but may speak/participate and vote initially, not cast tie-breaking vote. Deputy while not presiding is an ordinary voting MP.
  • Independence safeguards: protected tenure; charged remuneration; criticism of conduct through substantive motion; Article 122 protection for procedural irregularity; impartial chair/casting vote; seventh precedence rank with CJI. Judicial review of substantive illegality/unconstitutionality still exists.
  • UK Speaker conventionally leaves party; no fully established equivalent in India. Opposition Deputy Speaker is a convention, not a constitutional entitlement. Ruling-party membership does not excuse partisan adjudication.

6. Panels, pro tem, RS chair and secretariats

  • LS panel ≤10 chairpersons, nominated by Speaker; members preside when Speaker/Deputy absent. Panel powers while presiding match chair. If both offices vacant, President appoints a member under 95, not ordinary panel substitution. If only Speaker vacant, Deputy performs duties.
  • Speaker pro tem: President appoints member (usually senior by parliamentary service); administers oath; pro tem administers new MPs' oaths and facilitates Speaker election. Temporary, ending once Speaker elected; seniority selection is convention.
  • RS Chairman = VP, not RS member. Similar presiding powers, but cannot certify LS Money Bills or chair joint sitting. Normally casting vote only; when own removal considered, may speak/participate but cannot vote at all.
  • Deputy Chairman: RS elects from its members; removal effective majority +14-day notice; resignation to Chairman; office ends on ceasing RS membership. Acts when Chairman absent or VP acts as President; directly accountable to RS.
  • RS Chairman acting President stops chair duties and receives presidential remuneration, not both salaries. RS panel of vice-chairpersons handles absence; both Chairman/Deputy offices vacant → presidential appointee, not routine panel.
  • 98: separate secretarial staff, possible common posts; recruitment/service regulated by law. Each secretariat headed by Secretary-General, appointed by its presiding officer; administrative continuity rather than party leadership.
  • Historical offices: 1919 Act → Speaker/Deputy institution in 1921 (then President/Deputy President of Legislative Assembly): Frederick Whyte / Sachidanand Sinha. Vithalbhai Patel, 1925: first Indian/elected presiding officer. 1935 Act envisaged new names; older names used until 1947 because federal provisions not implemented. G.V. Mavalankar / M. Ananthasayanam Ayyangar: first LS Speaker/Deputy, 1952; Mavalankar also chaired Constituent Assembly (Legislative)/Provisional Parliament, 1946–1952.

7. Leaders, Opposition and whips

  • Leader of LS: PM if LS member, otherwise PM-nominated LS minister. Leader of RS: PM-nominated RS minister. Coordinates House business; deputy may be nominated. US analogue: majority leader.
  • Leader of Opposition: leader of numerically largest opposition party recognised by presiding officer; constructive scrutiny and alternative leadership. Recognition first 1969; statutory pay/status under 1977 Act, Cabinet-minister-equivalent facilities. Neither leader office specified in Constitution; House leader in rules, Opposition leader in statute.
  • 10% trap: chapter discusses party-recognition directions; 1977 Act does not itself prescribe 10% as LoP definition. Do not confuse party/group facilities thresholds, quorum and statutory LoP recognition. Equal-strength opposition parties: chair recognises one leader considering party status.
  • UK Shadow Cabinet: Opposition portfolio counterparts prepare alternative government; Ivor Jennings called Opposition leader alternative PM. US analogue: minority leader.
  • Whip: party-appointed floor organiser, secures attendance/voting discipline; based on conventions, not a named constitutional office. Defection consequences depend on Tenth Schedule, not every disciplinary breach.
  • Government business traditionally coordinated by Parliamentary Affairs Minister in LS and MoS in RS. 1998 Facilities Act: recognised party strength LS 55 / RS 25; recognised group LS 30 / RS 15; telephone/secretarial facilities. These thresholds are for this Act.

8. Sessions and ending parliamentary business

  • 85: President summons; maximum six months between last sitting of one session and first of next. Usual Budget (roughly February–May), Monsoon (July–September), Winter (November–December); calendar/timings vary.
  • Session: first sitting → prorogation (or LS dissolution); recess: between sessions. Sittings within a session may be adjourned for hours/days/weeks.
  • Adjournment: chair ends/suspends sitting to a specified time. Sine die: no reassembly date; chair may recall. Neither ends session or lapses Bills.
  • Prorogation: President ends session, can occur even while House sitting. Pending Bills and moved motions/resolutions do not lapse solely for this reason; pending notices generally lapse except intention to introduce Bills, under applicable rules.
  • Dissolution: ends LS itself—normal expiry or earlier presidential dissolution. RS never dissolved. Most LS business lapses; government assurances continue for examination.
  • Bills that lapse: pending in LS (wherever originated); LS-passed but pending in RS. Do not lapse: RS-pending and not LS-passed; passed both Houses awaiting assent; returned for reconsideration; deadlocked Bill where President notified joint-sitting intention before dissolution.
  • Lame-duck session: outgoing LS meeting after election of successor before its constitution; unsuccessful outgoing members called lame ducks. Historical 1957/1962 sessions passed votes on account; not a regular post-dissolution sitting.

9. Quorum, voting, language and majorities

  • 100 quorum = one-tenth total membership, chair included; at allocated 543/245 seats, LS 55 / RS 25. No quorum → chair adjourns/suspends until restored. Vacancy/unauthorised participation does not automatically invalidate every proceeding.
  • Voice vote: Ayes/Noes, chair declares result. Challenged decision → lobby-clearing/repeated question and division if required; automatic recorder, Aye/No slips or lobby division. Physical count may replace unnecessary division without names recorded. Slips useful on equipment failure/before assigned seats; lobby method largely obsolete in practice.
  • Ordinary division display: green Aye, red No, yellow abstain; secret mode may display only recording status. Not all votes are secret. Casting vote resolves a tie, subject to own-removal exceptions.
  • 120: Hindi/English business; chair can permit mother tongue. Interpretation facilitates participation; translation supplied where unavailable. Official Languages Act 1963 continued English beyond original 15-year period.
  • 88: ministers/Attorney-General may participate in either House, joint sittings and nominated committees. This gives no additional vote: minister votes only in House of membership; non-MP minister/AG does not vote through this entitlement.
  • Simple: more than half present and voting; abstentions excluded. Ordinary/Money/Financial Bills, confidence/no-confidence, censure, adjournment, thanks; Speaker/Deputy elections; RS Deputy election; 356/360 approvals; LS disapproval of National Emergency; LS agreement to VP removal.
  • Effective: more than half all then members (total minus vacancies); removes LS Speaker/Deputy, RS Deputy Chairman; RS initiates VP removal. Examples: RS 245−25 =220 → 111; LS 543−15 =528 → 265.
  • Absolute: more than half total membership irrespective of absence/vacancies; examples 543 →272, 245 →123. Usually a component of special threshold, not standalone rule for routine votes.
  • Special A: majority total membership and at least two-thirds present/voting, separately each House: 368 amendments; SC/HC judges, CAG/CEC removal; SEC protection equivalent to HC judge; National Emergency approval.
  • Special B: at least two-thirds TOTAL membership of each House for President impeachment. Special C: at least two-thirds present/voting in RS for 249 State-List authorisation and 312 All-India Service resolution. Some 368 amendments also require at least half the States' ratification.

10. Questions and devices without voting

  • Question Hour: accountability through questions; usual LS 11–12; RS 12–1, not universally first hour. RS procedure.
  • Starred: oral answer + supplementary questions; list green. Unstarred: written answer, no supplementary; white. Short notice: urgent, notice under ten days, oral; light pink. Private-member questions: their Bill/resolution/business; yellow.
  • Zero Hour: urgent public matters with chair permission; convention/Indian innovation since 1962, not a separate formal Rules heading. Usually after LS Question Hour; RS morning Zero Hour precedes noon questions. No automatic right to interrupt business.
  • Calling attention (1954): urgent matter → authoritative ministerial statement; Indian innovation in Rules, unlike Zero Hour convention; not confidence vote.
  • Half-hour discussion: elucidates factual issue in an answer; no formal motion/vote (chapter practice up to three days/week). Short-duration discussion (1953): urgent public importance, usually two hours; no formal motion/vote (chapter practice two days/week); duration is subject to House arrangements.
  • Point of order: identifies relevant rule/constitutional procedural violation; chair decides, no debate; cannot be a general policy speech.
  • Special Mention in RS / Rule 377 in LS: public issues outside normal question/discussion channels, subject to prescribed procedure.

11. Motions, resolutions and executive confidence

  • Substantive: independent proposal (e.g., removal). Substitute: alternative replaces original if adopted. Subsidiary: depends on main business—ancillary procedural step; superseding displaces current matter; amendment modifies part.
  • Closure: simple—enough debate, vote now; compartments—grouped clauses; kangaroo—selected important clauses, intervening ones treated as passed; guillotine—remaining business put to vote at allotted time's end.
  • Privilege motion: alleged breach, e.g., minister deliberately withholding/misstating facts; not automatically proof of breach.
  • Adjournment motion, LS only: extraordinary interruption for definite, factual, recent, urgent public matter, Union responsibility; leave support 50, debate minimum 2½ hours. One matter; not privilege/sub judice/revival of same-session debate or issue needing distinct motion. Has censure element; distinct from ordinary adjournment of sitting.
  • No-confidence, LS only: entire Council; 50 supporters for leave; no reasons necessary. Passage → whole ministry resigns. Confidence motion: government proves majority; failure defeats ministry.
  • Censure: specific reasons/policies; may target minister/group/Council; passage does not automatically require resignation. RS can criticise government but cannot dismiss Council through no-confidence.
  • Motion of Thanks: debate President's address after general election and at first session of each year, not specifically fiscal year. Address sets government programme; LS rejection signals loss of confidence; RS rejection alone cannot remove Council.
  • No-day-yet-named: admitted but discussion day unassigned; chair/House leader/BAC scheduling. Dilatory: postpone debate/delay business; chair can reject abuse; debate restricted to delay issue.
  • Resolution: independent proposal expressing opinion/approval/disapproval/recommendation/request; mover withdraws only with House leave. All resolutions are substantive motions; not all motions are resolutions or require votes.
  • Private member = any MP other than minister, including governing-party MP; private resolutions traditionally alternate Friday afternoons. Government resolutions cover policies, treaty approval or committee recommendations; statutory resolutions required by Constitution/Act, moved by minister or eligible private member.
  • Youth Parliament: Fourth All-India Whips Conference recommendation; Parliamentary Affairs Ministry promotes procedural literacy, discipline/tolerance and democratic values.

12. Ordinary Bills: readings to assent

  • Government/public Bill: minister, departmental drafting with Law Ministry; generally seven-day introduction notice. Private-member Bill: non-minister, member drafts; generally one month notice. Chances of passage differ; rejection of a government Bill is not invariably automatic resignation—confidence context matters.
  • Content types: original, amending, consolidating, expiring-law continuance, repealing, validating, ordinance-replacing, constitutional amendment and Money/Financial Bills.
  • First reading: introduction/title/objectives, publication; prior Gazette publication authorised by Speaker may dispense with leave. Introduction may face permitted objections; “no discussion ever” is too absolute.
  • Second reading: principles/general discussion → optional referral to select/joint/standing committee or circulation for public opinion → clause-wise consideration/amendments/votes. Select = one House; joint = both. Committee referral is not automatic for every Bill.
  • Third reading: accept/reject whole Bill; mainly confined debate, no substantive clause amendments; formal/verbal correction allowed under rules. Simple majority ordinarily passes it; transmit to other House for corresponding stages.
  • Other House: pass unchanged, amend, reject or hold pending. Agreement on identical text needed, or 108 joint sitting where available. Six months is a deadlock trigger, not an automatic maximum detention/expiry period.
  • 111 assent: assent → Act; withhold → no Act; return ordinary Bill once. Repassed by both Houses with/without amendment → assent required. Constitutional amendment duly passed → mandatory assent under 368; cannot return it. Ordinary Bill may require prior recommendation under special provisions (e.g., 3), not merely because it is ordinary.

13. Money Bills versus Financial Bills

  • 110 Money Bill contains ONLY: tax imposition/abolition/remission/alteration/regulation; Union borrowing/guarantees/financial obligations; custody/payment into/withdrawal from CFI or Contingency Fund; CFI appropriation; declaring/increasing charged expenditure; CFI/Public Account receipts/custody/issue or audit of Union/State accounts; incidental matters.
  • Not Money Bill merely for: fines/pecuniary penalties; licence/service fees; local-body taxes for local purposes.
  • Speaker certifies when sent to RS/President; 110(3) calls decision final, but constitutional judicial review is not barred. Rojer Mathew, 2019.
  • Money Bill: LS only, presidential recommendation, government/minister route; RS may recommend, cannot impose amendments/reject; return within 14 days. LS accepts/rejects any recommendation. No return by day 14 → deemed passed in LS form. No joint sitting. President may assent/withhold, cannot return.
  • Financial I, 117(1): 110(1)(a)–(f) subject(s) plus other substantive matters; LS introduction only + recommendation. RS otherwise equal amendment/rejection power; joint sitting possible; President can return. Tax reduction/abolition amendment exempt from recommendation requirement under 117(1).
  • Financial II, 117(3): involves CFI expenditure but is not a Money Bill/117(1) Bill; either House introduction without recommendation, but recommendation for consideration needed before that House passes. RS equal; joint sitting possible; return possible.
  • All Money Bills are financial in broad sense; not all Financial Bills are Money Bills. “Finance Bill” is an annual fiscal Bill's name; classification depends on contents and constitutional provisions, not title alone.

14. Joint sitting: deadlock route

  • 108 triggers: other House rejects; final disagreement on amendments; more than six months from receipt without passage. Exclude prorogation/adjournment over four consecutive days when computing delay.
  • Available for ordinary/Financial I/Financial II, not Money/constitutional amendment Bills. President notifies intent and summons; Houses stop further separate proceedings on Bill after notification.
  • Lapsed Bill cannot be revived by new notification. If notification precedes LS dissolution, joint sitting can proceed despite dissolution.
  • Chair sequence: LS Speaker → LS Deputy → RS Deputy Chairman → member chosen by those present. RS Chairman never presides. Quorum one-tenth combined membership; simple majority present/voting, LS rules apply. Numerical LS advantage is likely, not guaranteed party victory.
  • New amendments limited to resolving disagreements or those necessary because of delay; not unrestricted rewriting.
  • Three historical joint sittings: Dowry Prohibition Bill 1961 (Bill 1960); Banking Service Commission (Repeal) 1978 (Bill 1977); Prevention of Terrorism 2002. Distinguish Bill year from sitting year.

15. Budget: documents and constitutional control

  • 112 Annual Financial Statement (AFS) = estimated Union receipts/expenditure; “budget” not constitutional term. Financial year 1 April–31 March; distinguish revenue expenditure from other expenditure; charged from voted spending.
  • Includes revenue/capital receipts, financing/tax measures, expenditure estimates, actual/revised figures and fiscal policy/new proposals. AFS ≠ entire collection of budget documents.
  • Main documents: Budget Speech, AFS, Demands for Grants, Finance Bill; FRBM macro-economic framework/fiscal-policy strategy/medium-term fiscal-policy statements; Receipts/Expenditure Budgets, Expenditure Profile, memorandum on Finance Bill, Budget at a Glance, output–outcome framework, key features, previous-announcement implementation and document key. Economic Survey usually precedes Budget; separate analytical report.
  • Railway Budget separate 1924, following Acworth Committee 1921: commercial flexibility/business policy, stable general-revenue contribution and retained development funds. Merged with Union Budget from 2017–18; budget presentation advanced to 1 February from February-end practice.
  • 113: President recommends demands; only LS votes grants, may approve/refuse/reduce, not increase demand. RS discusses; charged spending discussable, not voted.
  • 114: CFI withdrawal requires appropriation by law. 265: tax needs authority of law. 117 recommendation safeguards taxation: Parliament can legislate higher taxes with required recommendation; book's blanket “cannot increase a tax” is incorrect.
  • Appropriation amendment cannot vary amount/destination of an approved grant or charged amount. Money Bill 14-day rule applies to qualifying Bills, not every fiscal Bill.

16. Charged expenditure and the budget sequence

  • CFI charged: President's emoluments/office; parliamentary presiding officers' salaries/allowances; SC judges' salaries/allowances/pensions; HC judges' pensions (salary normally State fund); CAG salary/allowances/pension; UPSC members/staff/administration under 322; SC/CAG office administration under relevant provisions; Union debt-service charges; sums satisfying court/arbitral judgments/decrees/awards; other constitutionally/statutorily charged expenditure.
  • Charged ≠ outside budget, discussion, audit or appropriation. Regular MPs' pay is not charged merely because presiding officers' pay is.
  • Presentation: Finance Minister speech in LS, papers laid in both; no same-day budget discussion. General discussion: principles/whole budget, no cut motions or grant votes; Finance Minister replies.
  • Committee scrutiny: recess commonly 3–4 weeks; 24 department-related standing committees, established 1993, expanded 2004, report on ministry demands to both Houses. Reports aid scrutiny; do not replace LS votes.
  • Grant voting: ministry-wise, only voted expenditure in LS. Policy cut → reduce TO ₹1; alternative policy possible. Economy cut → reduce BY specified amount; token cut → reduce BY ₹100, specific Union grievance.
  • Cut-motion limits: one demand/specific issue, clear expression; no defamatory argument, proposed law amendment/repeal, non-Union issue, charged spending, sub judice matter or privilege question. Passage may indicate lost confidence; guillotine puts undiscussed remaining demands to vote on allotted last day.
  • Appropriation Bill: covers voted grants plus charged spending; passage and assent authorise CFI payments. Finance Bill: implements tax/revenue proposals; tax amendments governed by recommendation rules; its constitutional category depends on contents.
  • 75-day trap: chapter cites repealed Provisional Collection of Taxes Act 1931. 2023 Act replaced it; the 75-day rule concerns expiry of specified provisionally collected customs/excise measures unless enacted/otherwise terminated—not a universal constitutional deadline for every Finance Bill. 2023 Act.

17. Grants beyond the regular budget

  • Vote on account, 116: advance expenditure grant pending final budget; usually two months/one-sixth annual estimate, longer election-year cover possible. It is a grant requiring appropriation, not unilateral executive spending authority.
  • Interim Budget ≠ vote on account: interim statement covers receipts and expenditure; vote on account authorises temporary expenditure. Incoming government later presents regular budget. Earlier February presentation helps finish regular appropriation before new financial year; vote on account is still constitutionally available.
  • Supplementary: approved amount insufficient for existing service. Additional: new service not contemplated in year's budget. Excess: already spent beyond grant; voted afterward, with PAC scrutiny before parliamentary regularisation.
  • Vote of credit: unexpected large/indefinite demand not readily itemised; metaphorical “blank cheque”, still parliamentary authority. Exceptional grant: special purpose outside current service of financial year.
  • Token grant: nominal grant for new service funded by reappropriation, not additional net expenditure; differs from token cut opposing a demand. Parliamentary financial procedure still applies.
  • Annuity/rule of lapse: unused annual authorisations lapse at year-end; discourages unauthorised reserves but can cause March rush. Not all money physically disappears or every Public Account balance lapses.

18. Three Union funds

  • CFI, 266(1): revenues, loans raised, repayments of loans received; parliamentary appropriation for withdrawal. “All receipts” is too broad because Public Account/Contingency arrangements differ.
  • Public Account, 266(2): other public money, e.g., provident funds, deposits, remittances; government often acts as banker/trustee. Payments do not require parliamentary appropriation; legal/accounting controls remain.
  • Contingency Fund, 267: imprest established by 1950 Act, at President's disposal; unforeseen expenditure advances pending parliamentary authorisation, then recouped. Held/operated through Finance Ministry on President's behalf; executive advance does not avoid later appropriation.

19. Parliament's functions and limits of control

  • Legislative: Union/residuary and Concurrent subjects; federal conflict rules include 254(2) exception. Chapter counts Union 98/Concurrent 52/State 59 are descriptive counting conventions; learn operative entries, not assume sequential entry numbers equal surviving total.
  • State-List routes: 249 RS resolution; 250 National Emergency; 252 two+ States request; 253 treaty implementation; 356 President's Rule. Different duration/application rules; not all such Union laws are temporary.
  • Delegated legislation: legislature lays policy/framework; executive makes rules within parent Act; parliamentary scrutiny via subordinate-legislation machinery. Ordinance replacement needs a law, not merely an approving resolution.
  • Executive: questions, discussions, motions, assurances/petitions/subordinate-legislation committees; Council collectively responsible to LS, although both scrutinise.
  • Financial: pre-spending budget control; post-spending PAC, Estimates, Public Undertakings Committees and CAG-assisted scrutiny; Estimates also suggests economies, not merely post-mortem audit.
  • Constituent: simple-majority changes outside 368; 368 special majority; specified federal provisions +half States. Basic structure, Kesavananda Bharati 1973, limits amendment. 169: Assembly requests Legislative Council creation/abolition, Parliament legislates; not a State power to introduce 368 amendments.
  • Judicial/quasi-judicial: President impeachment, VP removal, prescribed removal addresses for judges/CAG/CEC and SEC protection; contempt/privilege proceedings.
  • Electoral: elected MPs participate in presidential election with elected eligible MLAs; all MPs elect VP; Houses elect their presiding officers except ex-officio RS Chairman; Parliament regulates elections by laws including RPA 1950/1951 and 1952 Presidential/VP Elections Act.
  • Other: national deliberation, emergency approvals, State boundary/name changes, judiciary jurisdiction/organisation and common HC arrangements within constitutional limits.
  • Weak control factors: complex administration/financial demands; limited time/expertise; executive agenda/majority/whip dominance; large House; retrospective audit; guillotine; extensive delegated laws/ordinances; intermittent political scrutiny; weak Opposition and poor parliamentary conduct.
  • Mains remedies: committee scrutiny/expertise, timely reports and follow-up, more debate/divisions, stronger Opposition participation and transparent fiscal information.

20. RS: equal, weaker and distinctive powers

  • Broadly equal: ordinary Bills; constitutional amendments; Financial II introduction/passage and Financial I passage; presidential election/impeachment; VP election; prescribed removal addresses; emergency approvals; ordinance-replacing legislation; constitutional-body reports and institutional jurisdiction laws. Members of either House may become PM/ministers; accountability still to LS.
  • Weaker: Money Bill introduction/recommendations; no grant votes; cannot dismiss Council by no-confidence or use LS adjournment motion; no LS-only National Emergency disapproval; Speaker certifies Money Bills/chairs joint sittings; LS numerical advantage in joint votes.
  • Distinctive: 249 State-List authorisation; 312 new All-India Service resolution; initiates VP removal. During LS dissolution, RS approval can temporarily preserve 352/356/360 proclamations; reconstituted LS must approve within relevant 30-day constitutional window, not permanently excluded.
  • Value: revises rushed legislation; federal representation; expert nominees; continuity during dissolution. Compared with UK Lords and US Senate, RS is broadly coequal in legislation but limited in finance/executive confidence; not redundant merely because powers differ.

21. Privileges, contempt and constitutional limits

  • 105 privileges: functional safeguards for Houses, committees and members; extend to authorised parliamentary participants such as ministers/AG, not President merely as constituent of Parliament.
  • Collective: authorised publication; exclude strangers/secret sittings; own procedure; punish privilege breach/contempt (reprimand/admonition, imprisonment, member suspension/expulsion); information about members' arrest/detention/release; inquiries, witnesses/records; precinct arrests/legal process subject to chair permission.
  • Individual: speech/vote immunity for parliamentary/committee acts subject to Constitution/House rules; civil-arrest protection during session and 40 days before/after, not criminal arrest/preventive detention; session-related protection concerning court attendance/jury service is functional, not general exemption from courts.
  • 121: judicial conduct in discharge of duties discussed only on removal-address motion. 122: courts do not review mere procedural irregularity; substantive illegality/unconstitutionality remains reviewable.
  • 361A, 44th Amendment: substantially true reports protected unless malicious; secret sittings excluded. Authorised House publications separately protected; press protection is not unlimited.
  • Sita Soren, 2024: bribery for speech/vote has no 105(2)/194(2) immunity; reversed contrary P.V. Narasimha Rao position. Speech immunity does not legalise corruption. Supreme Court e-Committee newsletter.
  • Breach: violation of specific privilege. Contempt: broader obstruction/undermining House/member/officer's functions; every breach is contempt, not every contempt a specific breach (e.g., disobeying lawful House order).
  • Privilege sources: Constitution, statutes, House rules, conventions, judicial interpretation. 105 originally referenced Commons privileges on 26 January 1950; 44th Amendment, operative change 20 June 1979, replaced direct reference with existing Indian privileges; no exhaustive general codification.
  • Chapter historical illustrations: Indira Gandhi expelled/imprisoned by sixth LS, rescinded by seventh; K.K. Tiwari reprimanded by RS 1990. These illustrate sanctions, not ordinary party discipline.
  • India ≠ Dicey's sovereign Parliament: written supreme Constitution, federal distribution, judicial review, enforceable Fundamental Rights and basic-structure limit. UK doctrine: Parliament can legislate/repeal, change constitutional law by ordinary legislation, and primary Acts generally cannot be invalidated simply as unconstitutional. Do not infer UK has no judicial review or no rights laws: executive action is reviewed, and Human Rights Act 1998 protects rights through its statutory framework. UK Parliament.
