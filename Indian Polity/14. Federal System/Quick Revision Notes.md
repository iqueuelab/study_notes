# Federal System

## 1. Federal versus unitary: basis of authority

- **Federal:** Constitution divides powers between national and regional governments; each has constitutionally protected authority in its sphere.
- **Unitary:** regional authority is ultimately derived from national government; decentralisation/devolution can be extensive without making the system federal.
- **Foedus:** Latin for treaty/agreement; explains the term, not a requirement that every modern federation originated in a treaty.
- Federal examples: USA, Switzerland, Australia, Canada, Russia, Brazil, Argentina. Unitary examples in chapter: Britain, France, Japan, China, Italy, Norway, Sweden; some have substantial devolved/regional autonomy.
- Classical federal features: dual government, written/supreme Constitution, constitutional division, protected amendment process, independent judiciary, regional representation in a second chamber. These are a comparative model, **not an inflexible checklist** for every country.
- Unitary systems may also have written/supreme/rigid Constitutions, independent courts and bicameral legislatures. **Bicameral ≠ necessarily federal; written ≠ necessarily federal.**

## 2. Formation and India's choice

- **Integration / coming together:** previously separate units join; USA is the conventional example. **Holding together:** a larger polity distributes constitutional authority; chapter uses Canada to contrast a strong-centre model.
- Treat this as a broad typology: **Canadian Confederation (1867)** united colonies and reorganised authority, not simply the breakup of one pre-existing unitary country.
- USA: Constitution framed **1787**, new federal government began **1789**, after Revolution **1775–83**; originally 13 States, now 50. Canada: originally four provinces, now ten **plus three territories**; provinces and territories are distinct.
- India chose federalism for **large territory and socio-cultural diversity**: efficient government plus unity with regional autonomy.
- **Article 1: Union of States. Ambedkar:** Union not formed by a compact of sovereign States; no State right to secede. Union is indestructible.
- Canadian influence: strong Centre, centralising division and Union terminology. India's design remains distinctive; it is not a replica or a historically literal “disintegration” of independent States.

## 3. Seven federal features of India

- **Dual polity:** Union handles defence, foreign affairs, currency and communications; States handle public order, agriculture, health and local government, subject to constitutional entries/exceptions.
- **Written Constitution:** structures and limits both governments. Original **395 Articles, 22 Parts, 8 Schedules**; now **25 operative Parts, 12 Schedules**. Article totals depend on counting inserted/omitted provisions; the chapter's approximate 470 is not the highest Article number.
- **Division of powers—Seventh Schedule:** Union, State, Concurrent Lists. Original entry totals **97/66/47**; chapter gives **98/59/52** after changes. Entry numbering retains gaps and insertions—do not equate the last serial number with active-entry count.
- **Constitutional supremacy:** both levels' laws/actions are reviewable for constitutional conformity; Union supremacy in specified situations does not mean Parliament is constitutionally unlimited.
- **Federal amendment safeguards—368:** specified federal changes need each House's special majority **plus at least half the States' ratification**. No general requirement of every State's approval.
- **Independent judiciary:** SC safeguards Constitution and adjudicates qualifying federal disputes; security of tenure and service safeguards support independence.
- **Bicameral Parliament:** Lok Sabha represents people; Rajya Sabha represents States and specified UTs, participates in federal amendment and exercises important federal powers. It is not subordinate in every matter.

## 4. Legislative balance: important qualifications

- **Residuary legislative power—248/Union List 97:** Parliament, subject to GST's special framework. USA generally reserves undelegated powers to States/people under its Tenth Amendment.
- **Concurrent conflict—254:** Union law ordinarily prevails; qualifying State law with **presidential assent** may prevail in that State, but Parliament can subsequently override it.
- **GST—246A:** concurrent special power of Union/States; Parliament exclusively legislates on inter-State supplies. Do not squeeze GST into an unchanged three-list rule.
- **249:** Rajya Sabha **two-thirds present/voting**, national-interest resolution enables parliamentary State-List legislation without Emergency. Constitutional duration and continuation rules apply.
- Other State-List routes: **250 Emergency; 252 consenting States; 253 treaty implementation**. Constitutional authorisation, not a blanket Union takeover.

## 5. Fourteen centralising features

- **1. Strong Centre:** important Union subjects, residuary power and specified precedence over State laws; numerical list size alone does not measure power.
- **2. Alterable States—3/4:** Parliament can reorganise area/boundaries/names by ordinary law; President refers proposal for affected legislature's views, but **consent is not mandatory**. Territorial cession to another country is a distinct issue.
- **3. Single constitutional framework:** States ordinarily have no separate Constitution; erstwhile J&K's separate Constitution is historical, not a current general exception.
- **4. Relative flexibility:** most amendments do not require State ratification; some authorised changes occur outside 368. States cannot introduce a 368 amendment Bill.
- **5. Unequal Rajya Sabha representation:** largely population-linked allocation; States have **1–31 seats**, unlike US Senate's **two per State**. UT representation and nominated members are separate.
- **6. Emergency provisions:** **352, 356, 360** strengthen Union powers in different ways; their triggers, consequences and safeguards differ. They do not automatically place every State wholly under Union administration.
- **7. Single citizenship:** Indian citizenship; no separate State citizenship. Residence-based constitutional/statutory exceptions remain possible; all benefits are not necessarily identical across States.
- **8. Integrated judiciary:** common hierarchy applies Union and State law; contrasts with US federal/State court systems. US jurisdiction nevertheless overlaps—“each enforces only its own law” is too absolute.
- **9. All-India Services—312:** IAS, IPS, Indian Forest Service combine Union recruitment/control with State service. Union/State separate services also exist; AIS supports national standards but can raise State-autonomy concerns.
- **10. Integrated audit—148–151:** CAG audits Union and State accounts; constitutional appointment/removal arrangements do not depend on each State's consent. Independence and audit accountability matter; audit does not itself transfer all State financial powers.
- **11. Parliament's State-List access:** 249 and other constitutionally specified routes widen Union competence without necessarily amending the Constitution.
- **12. Governor—155/156:** President appoints; pleasure tenure, with constitutional constraints. Governor has a State constitutional office and specified Union-facing functions, not merely an unrestricted party agent.
- **13. Election machinery—324:** ECI handles parliamentary/State legislative and President/Vice-President elections. **Panchayat/municipal elections use State Election Commissions**, not ECI.
- **14. Reservation of State Bills—200/201:** Governor may reserve; President may assent/withhold. For returned non-Money Bills, reconsideration does **not** create the Article 111 mandatory-assent rule; presidential refusal remains possible.

## 6. Assent: current judicial clarification

- **Supreme Court Presidential Reference, 20 November 2025:** Governor under 200 has three options—assent, reserve, or **withhold and return with comments**; the last option applies only to non-Money Bills.
- Governor has discretion in this choice; Court rejected general judicially prescribed deadlines and **deemed assent** under 142. Similar rejection of prescribed timelines applies to President under 201.
- **Prolonged, unexplained, indefinite Governor inaction** can attract limited mandamus to exercise a constitutional choice within reasonable time; Court does not substitute its own merits decision.
- Use this later clarification when reading earlier assent rulings or simplified “absolute veto” labels. Reservation/assent power is a constitutional process, not a licence for arbitrary indefinite obstruction.

## 7. How scholars characterise India

- **K.C. Wheare:** quasi-federal; perceived central predominance. **K. Santhanam:** State fiscal dependence/central grants and powerful Planning Commission increased centralisation.
- Santhanam's planning criticism is historical: **NITI Aayog replaced Planning Commission in 2015**; it is a policy/advisory body with a State-participation Governing Council, not the same plan-fund allocation institution.
- **Paul Appleby:** extremely federal. **Morris Jones:** bargaining federalism. **Ivor Jennings:** federation with strong centralising tendency.
- **C.H. Alexandrowicz:** sui generis—distinctive. **Granville Austin:** cooperative federalism; strong Union does not reduce States to mere administrative agencies.
- **Ambedkar:** both governments derive authority directly from Constitution; Union cannot change that partition merely by its own ordinary-law preference. Constitutional exceptions let the system respond to circumstances.

## 8. Judicial position and federal practice

- **S.R. Bommai (1994): federalism is basic structure.** Greater Union powers do not make States mere satellites; their independent constitutional existence and allotted autonomy remain protected.
- Bommai subjected **356** to judicial review and imposed safeguards; constitutional exceptions cannot be used as the normal rule of government.
- Federal spirit in practice: **Maharashtra–Karnataka/Belagavi boundary dispute; Karnataka–Tamil Nadu/Cauvery sharing; regional parties; Mizoram/Jharkhand/Telangana State formation; demands for fiscal transfers; resistance to interference; limits on President's Rule**.
- Cooperation, bargaining and conflict can coexist. Disputes show multiple legitimate constitutional interests, not necessarily failure of federalism.

## 9. One-minute recall / Mains framework

- **Federal authority comes from Constitution; administrative decentralisation alone is insufficient.**
- **Seven federal features + fourteen centralising features; federalism remains basic structure.**
- **Union indestructible; States reorganisable; State views required under 3, consent not binding.**
- Mains: unity/diversity rationale → constitutional distribution → centralising safeguards → Bommai/State autonomy → fiscal and institutional cooperation → evaluate actual practice with examples.
