Directive Principles of State Policy

1. Nature, origin and constitutional role

  • Part IV, 36–51: 36 defines State as in Part III; 37 states nature/duty; substantive directives mainly 38–51. Irish Constitution 1937 influence, with Spanish antecedents.
  • Ambedkar—novel features/instructions to legislature and executive; parallels the 1935 Act's Instrument of Instructions. Granville Austin: Rights + DPSPs = conscience of the Constitution.
  • Aim: welfare State, social/economic democracy and Preamble's justice/liberty/equality/fraternity; go beyond a State confined to policing and defence.
  • Not independently enforceable in court, but fundamental in governance; State has a constitutional duty to apply them in lawmaking. Political accountability/public opinion supply the principal sanction.
  • Courts use them to interpret rights and assess statutory purpose/reasonableness; implementing a DPSP does not automatically cure every constitutional violation.
  • B.N. Rau: justiciable/non-justiciable distinction. Reasons for non-justiciability: resource limits, diverse/backward conditions and flexibility over timing/priorities for the new State.

2. Socialistic principles: justice and welfare

  • 38: just social order; reduce income/status/facility/opportunity inequalities among individuals and groups.
  • 39(a): adequate livelihood for men/women equally; (b): material resources distributed for common good; (c): avoid wealth/production concentration to common detriment.
  • 39(d): equal pay for equal work; (e): protect worker health/strength and children from abuse/economic compulsion; (f): healthy development, dignity and childhood/youth protection from exploitation/abandonment.
  • 39A: equal justice/free legal aid. 41: work, education and public assistance in unemployment, old age, sickness/disability and undeserved want, within economic capacity/development.
  • 42: humane working conditions and maternity relief. 43: living wage, decent life, leisure/social/cultural opportunity. 43A: worker participation in management. 47: nutrition, living standards and public health.
  • Wage distinction: minimum—basic statutory protection; fair—between minimum and living in textbook formulation; living—decent life including wider education/health/social needs. Actual wage law, not this mnemonic alone, determines entitlement.

3. Gandhian principles

  • 40: village Panchayats with self-government powers. 43: rural cottage industries on individual/cooperative basis.
  • 43B: voluntary formation, autonomy, democratic control and professional cooperative management.
  • 46: weaker sections, particularly SCs/STs—education/economic interests and protection from injustice/exploitation.
  • 47: prohibit harmful intoxicating drinks/drugs except medicinal purposes. 48: preserve/improve breeds; prohibit slaughter of cows/calves and other milch/draught cattle.
  • Categories are textbook classifications, not constitutional headings; one Article can serve more than one category.

4. Liberal-intellectual principles

  • 44: Uniform Civil Code for citizens. 45: early childhood care/education below six.
  • 48: scientific agriculture/animal husbandry. 48A: environment, forests and wildlife.
  • 49: protect monuments/places/objects of artistic/historic interest declared of national importance.
  • 50: judiciary–executive separation in State public services. 51: international peace/security, just honourable relations, respect for international law/treaties, arbitration.

5. Amendment additions: high-yield sequence

  • 42nd, 1976—four changes: healthy child development 39(f); free legal aid 39A; worker management participation 43A; environment 48A.
  • 44th, 1978: minimise specified inequalities 38(2).
  • 86th, 2002: 45 changed from original under-14 education goal to below-six care/education; 21A makes 6–14 education a right; 51A(k) gives parental/guardian duty. Relevant provisions operational 2010.
  • 97th, 2011: 43B cooperatives, effective 2012. Judicial invalidation of Part IXB for State-only cooperatives does not remove 43B.

6. Rights versus DPSPs: compare without overstatement

  • Rights: enforceable constitutional guarantees, legal remedies, political democracy plus dignity/social aspects; DPSPs: governance goals, no standalone enforcement remedy, social/economic democracy.
  • Rights bind State action and sometimes private conduct; DPSPs guide State policy. Both concern individual and collective welfare.
  • “Rights negative, DPSPs positive” is a rough contrast: rights also impose positive duties, e.g. 21A. “Rights need no legislation” is also too absolute, e.g. statutory implementation of 17/23.
  • Courts cannot strike a law down solely because it fails a non-justiciable DPSP, but can review it under enforceable constitutional standards. DPSP purpose informs review without replacing those standards.
  • Constitutional duty under 37 is more than an optional aspiration; direct litigation remedies depend on rights/statutes and the particular constitutional provision.

7. Conflict and harmony: judgment timeline

  • Champakam Dorairajan 1951: enforceable Rights prevailed in that conflict; 1st Amendment 1951 introduced protective changes. 4th 1955/17th 1964 also supported land-reform/directive implementation.
  • Golaknath 1967: Parliament could not abridge Rights under then-view; later displaced. 24th 1971: affirmed constituent amendment power.
  • 25th 1971: 31C protection for laws implementing 39(b)/(c); originally referred to 14/19/31, now 31 omitted.
  • Kesavananda Bharati 1973: amend Rights subject to basic structure; upheld limited 31C protection but struck the clause excluding review of whether the law genuinely implements those policies.
  • 42nd 1976: attempted protection for laws implementing all DPSPs. Minerva Mills 1980: invalidated that extension; harmony/balance between III and IV is basic structure.
  • Operative principle: realise welfare goals while preserving constitutional rights; no absolute supremacy of all DPSPs over all Rights. Limited 31C shield from 14/19 for genuine 39(b)/(c) laws remains; amendments cannot destroy basic structure.
  • Memory: 1951 Rights priority → 1967 amendment restriction → 1973 basic structure → 1980 balance.

8. Criticisms and their limits

  • No legal force: K.T. Shah—pious superfluities/resource-dependent cheque; Nasiruddin—New Year resolutions; T.T. Krishnamachari—dustbin of sentiments; K.C. Wheare—manifesto/moral homily; Jennings—pious aspirations.
  • Illogical mixture: N. Srinivasan/Jennings criticised arrangement/inconsistent philosophy. Response: integrated social goals can reflect multiple traditions rather than a single school.
  • Conservative/outdated: Jennings associated them with Fabian/Webb influences; this is criticism, not constitutional fact that directives have expired.
  • Potential constitutional conflict: K. Santhanam feared Centre–State, President–PM and Governor–CM disputes. Failure to implement a DPSP is not by itself an automatic dismissal ground or unrestricted presidential veto; operative constitutional powers control.
  • Criticisms raise implementation/enforcement questions; they do not erase Article 37's governance duty.

9. Utility and political sanction

  • Alladi/Ambedkar: responsible government must answer to electorate for ignoring directives. L.M. Singhvi: life-giving social-justice philosophy; M.C. Chagla: welfare-State ideal; Austin: social revolution; B.N. Rau: educative moral precepts.
  • M.C. Setalvad: instructions to all authorities, judicial-review guidance, background to State action and amplification of Preamble.
  • Roles: policy continuity across governments; material conditions for effective Rights; opposition scrutiny; performance yardstick; common constitutional programme transcending party manifestos.
  • The Rights–DPSP relationship is complementary: political participation is weakened when poverty/illiteracy deny effective opportunities.

10. Implementation: economic and labour examples

  • Planning Commission 1950/Five-Year Plans → NITI Aayog 2015; development/inequality goals. These institutional examples are historical policy choices, not bodies mandated by Part IV.
  • Land reforms: remove intermediaries (zamindar/jagirdar/inamdar), tenancy security/fair rent, ceilings, surplus redistribution, cooperative farming.
  • Historical labour laws: Trade Unions 1926; Payment of Wages 1936; Minimum Wages/Factories 1948; Industrial Disputes 1947; Mines 1952; Bonus 1965; Contract Labour 1970; employee compensation 1923; Bonded Labour 1976; child-labour law 1986/2016; maternity 1961, equal remuneration 1976.
  • Update: four Labour Codes—Wages 2019; Industrial Relations, Social Security, Occupational Safety/Health/Working Conditions 2020—made effective 21 November 2025, consolidating 29 laws. Treat older chapter Acts as historical implementation examples, subject to repeal/savings/transition provisions. Official Labour Ministry release
  • Life-insurance nationalisation 1956; 14 banks 1969; privy purses abolished 1971. General-insurance takeover began 1971, but nationalisation Act 1972, effective 1 January 1973—distinguish dates. Insurance regulator history

11. Implementation: legal aid, rural welfare and environment

  • Legal Services Authorities Act 1987: nationwide legal-aid network/Lok Adalats. Ordinary Lok Adalat resolves through compromise, not a contested merits trial; award deemed decree, final/binding, no ordinary appeal; narrow constitutional challenge is distinct.
  • Rural/cottage support: KVIC/boards, NSIC, coir/silk/handloom/handicraft institutions; some listed boards are historical/reorganised, not all presently active.
  • Programme chronology: Community Development 1952; Hill Area Development Programme 1974–75; Drought-Prone Areas 1973; Minimum Needs 1974; IRDP 1978; Jawahar Rozgar 1989; SGSY 1999; SGRY 2001; rural employment guarantee implementation 2006 under 2005 Act. Do not treat predecessor programmes as simultaneous current schemes. The chapter’s 1960 Hill Area date is corrected to the programme’s Fifth-Plan initiation. Planning Commission study
  • Environment: Wildlife Protection 1972; Forest Conservation 1980 (later renamed Van (Sanrakshan Evam Samvardhan) Adhiniyam); Water 1974/Air 1981, pollution-control boards; National Forest Policy 1988.
  • Scientific agriculture: seeds, fertilisers, irrigation and better animal-husbandry methods; public-health infrastructure and disease-control programmes; nutrition/living-standard measures.

12. Implementation: inclusion, local institutions and heritage

  • 73rd Amendment 1992, effective 1993: Panchayats' constitutional status; three levels with constitutional exceptions, not every State/area forced into an identical arrangement.
  • Protective measures/reservations: Protection of Civil Rights Act 1955, renamed 1976; SC/ST Atrocities Act 1989. 65th 1990: combined SC/ST Commission; 89th 2003: separate NCSC/NCST.
  • Commissions: NCW 1992; NCBC statutory 1993 → constitutional 102nd 2018; NCM statutory 1993; NCPCR constituted 2007 under 2005 law.
  • EWS 10% central scheme 2019, enabled by 103rd; separate from SC/ST/OBC schemes. Rights and constitutional conditions still apply.
  • Judiciary–executive separation: criminal-procedure reforms implementing 50; current BNSS replaced CrPC with savings from July 2024. Executive magistrate functions still exist—separation did not abolish every executive adjudicatory responsibility.
  • Heritage: 1951 declaration-of-national-importance Act; distinguish wider Ancient Monuments and Archaeological Sites and Remains Act 1958. India Code
  • State cattle-protection laws, social pensions and public healthcare are examples; eligibility ages vary, so the chapter's 65-year pension illustration is not a universal rule.
  • Panchsheel/non-alignment illustrate international-peace policy. Financial constraints, social inequality, population pressure, administrative capacity and federal coordination affect implementation.

13. Directives outside Part IV

  • 335, Part XVI: SC/ST service claims consistently with efficiency; constitutional proviso applies.
  • 350A, Part XVII: mother-tongue primary-instruction facilities for linguistic-minority children.
  • 351, Part XVII: Union duty to promote/develop Hindi for India's composite culture.
  • Read with whole Constitution; these are not Part IV DPSPs, and their precise legal effect depends on their text/context rather than an automatic identical classification.

14. One-minute recall and mains structure

  • DPSPs non-justiciable, but fundamental in governance. Classification is scholarly.
  • 42nd: child/legal aid/workers/environment; 44th: inequalities; 86th: early childhood; 97th: cooperatives.
  • Rights + DPSPs: means and goals; basic structure protects harmony.
  • Mains: constitutional goal → implementing law/programme → outcomes/remaining barriers → rights safeguards → practical reforms in funding/accountability/federal coordination.
