Parliamentary System

1. Central distinction

  • Parliamentary: executive depends on the legislature's confidence; responsible/cabinet government or Westminster model. India: Union 74/75, States 163/164. Britain, Japan and Canada also use parliamentary government.
  • Presidential: executive has a separately grounded, fixed tenure; it does not depend on legislative confidence. USA is the standard comparison.
  • “Non-responsible” in this classification means no parliamentary collective responsibility, not absence of legal accountability, legislative oversight or impeachment.
  • Avoid treating every country with a President as presidential: India's elected President coexists with parliamentary government. Russia and Sri Lanka have mixed/semi-presidential arrangements, so the chapter's broad examples need qualification.
  • Ivor Jennings: cabinet is the nucleus of parliamentary power. Primus inter pares: Prime Minister as first among equals; Crossman/Mackintosh described growing PM dominance as prime-ministerial government. Actual dominance depends on party, coalition and institutions.

2. India's eight features

  • Dual executive: President = head of State/constitutional executive; PM = head of government, leading the real political executive. Governor/Chief Minister parallels this at State level, with constitutionally specified differences.
  • Majority support: normally majority party leader becomes PM; coalition or other confidence-supported ministry is possible. Largest party alone is not an automatic entitlement when someone else demonstrably commands majority support.
  • Collective responsibility: 75(3) makes Council responsible to Lok Sabha, not Rajya Sabha. Ministers stand together; a successful no-confidence vote requires ministry's departure, subject to transition arrangements.
  • Political homogeneity: common party programme; coalition ministers work through agreed policy/consensus. Homogeneity is a tendency, not a constitutional requirement that every minister share a party.
  • Executive–legislature overlap: ministers usually MPs. A non-MP may be appointed but ceases after six consecutive months without membership: 75(5). “Double membership” means executive plus legislature, not both parliamentary Houses.
  • PM leadership: coordinates ministers, government, parliamentary majority and governing party; PM may belong to either House. Indira Gandhi (1966), Deve Gowda (1996), Manmohan Singh (2004) illustrate Rajya Sabha membership.
  • Lower-House dissolution: President can dissolve Lok Sabha under constitutional arrangements, ordinarily on ministerial advice. Rajya Sabha is permanent; advice/confidence issues in a hung House require constitutional judgment.
  • Secrecy: minister takes office/secrecy oaths administered by President, 75(4), Third Schedule. Cabinet confidentiality supports collective decisions; it does not erase legal disclosure/accountability duties.

3. Advice, responsibility and membership: traps

  • 74: President acts on Council's advice; may require reconsideration once, then must act on reconsidered advice. 42nd Amendment made binding advice explicit; 44th added reconsideration.
  • Collective responsibility is to Lok Sabha; ministers may still answer questions/scrutiny in both Houses. PM need not personally be a Lok Sabha member.
  • A minister may speak/participate in either House under 88, but votes only in the House of membership.
  • The six-month exception permits initial external talent; the textbook claim that selection is absolutely restricted to existing MPs is too strong.
  • Cabinet confidentiality and collective responsibility are different: one protects deliberation; the other requires public unity and lower-House confidence.

4. USA: presidential comparison

  • President is both head of State and head of government; elected through Electoral College, four-year term. Congress is elected separately.
  • Cabinet secretaries are appointed, not collectively dependent on Congress's confidence; many appointments require Senate consent. Cabinet and informal “kitchen cabinet” are distinct, not synonyms.
  • President/secretaries cannot simultaneously sit in Congress. Officials can provide testimony and Congress exercises oversight; “cannot attend sessions” should not be read as a ban on appearing before Congress.
  • President cannot dissolve Congress. Removal is not through a no-confidence motion: House impeachment and Senate conviction, on constitutional grounds of treason, bribery or other high crimes and misdemeanours.
  • Separation with checks/balances, not watertight isolation: veto, legislative funding/oversight, Senate confirmation and judicial review.
  • Stable tenure, policy continuity and appointment of specialists are possible advantages; legislative–executive deadlock, concentrated leadership and narrower representation are possible drawbacks.

5. Parliamentary merits: five

  • Coordination: legislative/executive overlap helps implement the legislative programme and reduces institutional deadlock.
  • Responsibility: questions, debates, committees, adjournment and no-confidence mechanisms scrutinise omissions as well as actions.
  • Checks on despotism: collegiate decision-making and removable government; effectiveness depends on real parliamentary scrutiny.
  • Ready alternative: another majority-supported leader can form government without fresh elections. Jennings described Opposition leader as an alternative PM; appointment still requires ability to command confidence.
  • Wide representation: ministers can represent varied regions, communities and interests; plural executive facilitates accommodation.

6. Parliamentary demerits: five

  • Instability: loss of confidence, coalition breakdown or defections can end a ministry early. Historical examples: Morarji Desai, Charan Singh, V.P. Singh, Chandra Shekhar, Deve Gowda, I.K. Gujral.
  • Policy discontinuity: changed ministries can reverse programmes; chapter contrasts Janata's 1977 changes and Congress's return in 1980. Continuity remains possible through institutions and consensus.
  • Cabinet/PM dominance: large disciplined majority can weaken scrutiny. Laski warned about executive tyranny; Ramsay Muir discussed cabinet domination; chapter cites Indira/Rajiv Gandhi periods.
  • Fusion of powers: executive leads legislation as well as administration. Bagehot's linkage idea captures this; India still preserves judicial independence and constitutional checks.
  • Expertise/time limits: elected ministers may lack subject expertise and balance parliamentary, party and administrative work; specialist advice and non-MP appointment exception partly mitigate this.
  • Montesquieu, The Spirit of Laws (1748): concentration of powers threatens liberty; separation is a safeguard, not a demand that Indian institutions never cooperate.

7. Why India adopted it: four reasons

  • Familiarity: colonial-era representative/responsible institutions supplied experience. K.M. Munshi preferred established parliamentary traditions over an unfamiliar experiment.
  • Responsibility over assured tenure: Ambedkar contrasted executive stability and responsibility; framers prioritised accountable government.
  • Avoid institutional conflict: a new democracy undertaking development needed legislative–executive cooperation.
  • Diverse society: collegiate executive could accommodate regions and interests, supporting participation and unity.
  • K.T. Shah favoured a presidential model in Assembly debates. Swaran Singh Committee (1975) favoured retaining parliamentary government; debate over replacement intensified from the 1970s.

8. India versus Britain: six distinctions

  • Head of State: India elected republican President; Britain hereditary constitutional monarch.
  • Sovereignty: India's Parliament is limited by written Constitution, federalism, rights and judicial review; Britain follows parliamentary sovereignty within its constitutional framework.
  • PM's House: India either House; British PM sits in Commons by modern convention.
  • Ministers: India express six-month non-member exception. Britain generally requires membership of Commons or Lords by strong convention, not an identical Indian six-month constitutional provision; outsiders can enter Parliament through appointment to Lords.
  • Legal responsibility/countersignature: India's Constitution does not impose the British-style ministerial countersignature requirement for every official act of the head of State. Ministers in India remain subject to law—“no legal responsibility” does not mean personal immunity.
  • Shadow cabinet: Britain institutionalised opposition portfolios; India has no comparable established national constitutional/conventional arrangement. The device is not unique to Britain worldwide.

9. One-minute recall / answer frame

  • Confidence, collective responsibility, dual executive, overlap, PM leadership, dissolution.
  • Lok Sabha confidence; either-House PM; six-month non-MP exception; President reconsideration once.
  • Mains: accountability/coordination/representation → instability and majority dominance → India–Britain distinctions → strengthen committees, debates and opposition scrutiny within constitutional limits.
