# Hoysala architecture and rural temple heritage

## Dynasty and architectural vocabulary
- Hoysalas flourished in Karnataka, principally the **11th–14th centuries**, with Belur and Halebidu as important royal centres. **Vishnuvardhana, Ballala II and Someshwara** are important rulers; their dates and individual temple patronage should not be conflated.
- Distinctive features: **soapstone/chloritic schist**, intricate sculptures, narrative friezes, polished lathe-turned pillars, stellate plans and raised **jagati** for circumambulation. **Trikuta** denotes three shrines; not every Hoysala temple has the same plan or a star platform.
- **Sacred Ensembles of the Hoysalas**, inscribed UNESCO **2023**, comprise **Chennakeshava at Belur, Hoysaleswara at Halebidu and Keshava at Somanathapura**. Rural shrines and Jain **basadis** broaden the heritage beyond those three. [UNESCO decision](https://whc.unesco.org/en/decisions/8419).
- Article highlights Lakshmidevi at **Doddagaddavalli (1114 CE)**, Lakshminarasimha at **Nuggehalli (1250 CE)**, other Chennakeshava shrines, Koravangala temples and **Hulikere kalyani**. Similar temple names recur: do not transfer Someshwara-era attribution or a particular platform geometry to Belur's Chennakeshava.
- **Sala slaying a beast** is the dynasty's emblem/origin legend. Shaiva, Vaishnava, Shakta and Jain patronage illustrates religious/artistic diversity; the kalyani links ritual space and water architecture. Source astronomical symbolism and minor-temple patron claims need site-specific evidence.
- **2023 CSE recall:** none of Hoysala, Gahadavala, Kakatiya and Yadava kingdoms was established in early eighth-century AD: **(d)**. Do not use the unrelated IB ACIO sheet's reasoning that “southern Karnataka” is intrinsically wrong; Hoysala heartlands were in Karnataka.
