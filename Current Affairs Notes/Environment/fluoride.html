Fluoride in groundwater and fluorosis

Editable notes

December 2025: Mayurbhanj drinking-water concern

  • Source reports an NGO finding excessive fluoride/fluorosis in villages of Mayurbhanj, Odisha. Geogenic contamination and dependence on untreated groundwater connect geology, drinking-water governance and health.
  • Fluoride F⁻ is the monatomic anion of fluorine; fluoride-bearing fluorspar, cryolite and fluorapatite can release it through weathering/leaching. Hard-rock aquifers, water chemistry and residence time influence concentrations; every deep borewell is not necessarily unsafe.
  • Anthropogenic pathways include aluminium, steel/glass-fibre, phosphate-fertiliser and ceramics industries, via air, soil or water. Test the actual supply rather than infer contamination solely from a district name.
  • Correct BIS IS 10500 limits: 1.0 mg/L acceptable; 1.5 mg/L permissible in the absence of an alternative source. The PDF's “ideal 0.7, maximum 1.0” table misstates the BIS categories. Official drinking-water standards table.
  • Prolonged excessive exposure can cause dental fluorosis during tooth development (mottling/stains/pitting) and skeletal fluorosis (pain, stiffness, ligament/bone changes, severe disability). Risk depends on dose, duration and susceptibility; do not treat an individual diagnosis as automatic at one concentration. The source's broad neurological/IQ claims are not a clinical conclusion for every exposed child.
  • Mitigation: tested safe surface water, rainwater harvesting, carefully controlled blending, Nalgonda defluoridation, activated alumina or RO with maintenance/residual disposal; surveillance and public awareness. Nutrition can support health but cannot replace a safe drinking-water supply. The source's 230+ districts is an undated reported count, not an updated national estimate.
  • Practice: cryolite + fluorapatite (a) contain fluoride; gypsum is calcium sulphate. Nalgonda removes fluoride (b), distinct from arsenic removal, softening or seawater desalination.
