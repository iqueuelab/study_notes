Leopards: conflict, rescue and illegal wildlife trade

Editable notes

November 2025: Maharashtra rescue-centre transfer proposal

  • Maharashtra proposed transferring 50 leopards already held for years in rescue centres to Vantara, Jamnagar, Gujarat, seeking Central Zoo Authority (CZA) approval to ease overcrowding and free space for new rescues. A proposal is not a completed transfer or a plan to capture 50 fresh wild animals.
  • Source conflict hotspots: Junnar, Shirur, Ambegaon and Khed (Pune); sugarcane fields provide cover, nearby livestock/dogs attract predators and fragmented corridors increase encounters. The clipping reports three deaths since 12 October including a 2 November attack; its explanatory “November 2024” label is inconsistent with this 2025 issue and should not date the incident series.
  • CZA is statutory under the Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972; recognised zoos need applicable permissions for acquisition/transfer of scheduled animals. Capture, transfer and captive breeding have different statutory requirements; “all actions require Central Government approval” overstates a single approval rule. Schedule I protection does not prohibit every authorised rescue intervention.
  • Assess transport stress, veterinary/quarantine capacity, lifetime welfare, records and independent oversight; a private facility's capacity claim is not proof of good outcomes. Transfer of captive rescues cannot by itself solve habitat loss, livestock vulnerability or conflict at source. Sterilisation/birth-control and selective removal reported in the source remain proposals subject to law/science.

Population estimates and trafficking

  • Panthera pardus, Vulnerable, CITES Appendix I, Indian Schedule I. Status of Leopards in India 2022, released 29 February 2024, estimated 13,874 (12,616–15,132) in the surveyed landscape; not a 2024 census of every habitat. Coverage: forested habitats of 18 tiger States, with limits for non-forested/high-altitude areas. MP 3,907, Maharashtra 1,985, Karnataka 1,879, Tamil Nadu 1,070 lead; reported growth 1.08% annually relates to comparable sampled areas since 2018. MoEFCC report release.
  • Threats: habitat fragmentation, prey loss, retaliatory killing and illegal skin/bone/claw/tooth trade. Substitution for tiger parts is a trafficking risk; stronger tiger enforcement alone does not prove causation for every leopard killing. Leopards outside tiger reserves also need monitoring and corridors.
  • WCCB, State forest agencies, customs/police and international CITES/INTERPOL cooperation address trafficking; camera traps, DNA forensics, online-trade investigation, prompt compensation and livestock protection complement demand reduction. CITES regulates international trade; Appendix I is not a blanket ban on all domestic transfers or an exception only for education/science.
  • Source's ~12,000 traded during 2020–23 lacks a specified CITES dataset/unit and may mix live animals, parts and records; ~75% historic range loss also needs its study/baseline. Treat neither as a verified current census. A national Project Leopard is advocated here, not established by this article; distinguish existing State initiatives and Project Snow Leopard.
