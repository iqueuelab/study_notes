# Green India Mission and ecological restoration

## Revised mission and November 2025 restoration debate
- **National Mission for a Green India (GIM)**, under **MoEFCC/NAPCC**, addresses forest quality, ecosystem services, carbon and forest-based livelihoods; implementation activities began **2015–16**. The **revised mission document was released 17 June 2025**, emphasising landscape restoration, soil/moisture conservation, local ecological conditions and convergence. [MoEFCC annual report, retrospective confirmation of release](https://moef.gov.in/uploads/pdf-uploads/Nitin_Rev_4.pdf).
- Keep distinct: GIM's original **5 million ha additional cover + 5 million ha improved quality**; India's broader **26 million ha land-restoration commitment by 2030**; and NDC additional carbon sink **2.5–3 billion tonnes CO₂ equivalent by 2030**. The PDF's 25-million-ha GIM target and 2.5–3.39-billion-tonne wording should not replace these different measures. [Government mission description](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressNoteDetails.aspx?ModuleId=3&NoteId=153994&lang=2&reg=48).
- **ISFR 2023's 25.17% is combined forest and tree cover**, not forest cover alone. More mapped green area does not automatically mean higher biodiversity or carbon uptake. Source reports a study finding a 12% decline in photosynthetic efficiency associated with warming/drying; retain as a source-reported study claim pending its precise period/method verification, not a universal decline for every Indian forest.
- Priorities: **Aravallis, Western Ghats, Himalayan catchments, mangroves/coasts**; use native/site-appropriate species, maintain hydrology and habitat diversity, and integrate agroforestry/watershed work. Monocultures can damage biodiversity/water balance; open natural grasslands should not be treated automatically as degraded planting sites.
- **Community participation, ecological design and finance** are the editorial's three gaps. Recognise **FRA 2006/community rights**, Gram Sabha participation and forest-dependent livelihoods; promote JFM/participatory monitoring and NTFP-based income. Source examples include Tamil Nadu mangroves, Himachal biochar, UP plantation/carbon-market initiatives, Odisha JFM and Chhattisgarh livelihood-linked planting; these examples do not independently establish every reported outcome.
- **CAMPA**, under the **Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act, 2016**, channels compensation associated with forest diversion; it is not GIM's sole funding source. Track fund releases/utilisation separately from ecological outcomes. Source ₹95,000-crore fund and Delhi 23% utilisation during 2019–24 are dated reported figures, not permanent balances. Public dashboards should report survival, native-species mix, rights compliance, water and carbon outcomes.
- Reject the source's **11.22 million ha/₹575 crore GIM achievement** as a verified mission-only total: official August 2024 reporting instead gave **₹909.82 crore released to 17 States and one UT for 155,130 ha**. [Parliament reply](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=2041462&lang=34&reg=37). Funding periods and programme scope must match.
- Finance through convergence, carefully designed carbon markets/payment for ecosystem services, ecological training and independent review can support restoration. The unyearred practice's **A** key depends on the questionable 25-million-ha statement; revise the factual premise rather than memorising its key.
