Vultures and toxic veterinary drugs

Editable notes

Nimesulide: correct regulatory scope

  • NSAID, pain/inflammation medicine. Veterinary exposure can damage vulture kidneys; visceral gout indicates uric-acid deposition associated with renal failure.
  • Medicated livestock → contaminated carcass → scavenging vulture → kidney injury → gout/death; highlights indirect ecological effects of pharmaceuticals.
  • S.O. 5633(E), 30 December 2024, section 26A, Drugs and Cosmetics Act, 1940: nimesulide/formulations for animal use prohibition. 20 February 2025 CDSCO circular sought enforcement. CDSCO record, enforcement circular.
  • January source's blanket assertion that this makes all human administration banned/unsuitable is unsupported; human-use safety restrictions are a separate issue. No medical-use recommendation follows from this note.
  • Source lists earlier veterinary use in cattle/pigs/horses and human pain/fever indications; these describe usage, not endorsed prescribing.
  • Conservation: enforce veterinary-drug restrictions, monitor carcass exposure, safe alternatives under professional guidance and vulture-population recovery; scavengers provide ecosystem sanitation services.

Long-billed vulture and habitat

  • Indian/long-billed vulture: Gyps indicus, Critically Endangered; cliff-nesting across much of central and peninsular India.
  • Ramadevarabetta near Ramanagara, Karnataka, established in 2012, is an important vulture sanctuary; “first” should not become “India's only sanctuary forever”.
  • Protect nesting cliffs, safe food and habitat connectivity; address quarrying, disturbance, electrocution and collision risks.
  • Veterinary diclofenac harms vultures through renal failure. The 2006 veterinary prohibition differs from 2015 restrictions on large multidose human formulations; all human medicinal use was not banned.
  • Vulture Safe Zones, captive breeding and monitored reintroduction complement drug regulation. Scavenging reduces carcass accumulation and associated disease risks; it does not sterilise every pathogen.

September 2025: conservation network and breeding

  • We Foundation India, collaborating with Gauhati University Zoology Department, launched a vulture conservation portal in Assam ahead of International Vulture Awareness Day (first Saturday of September). Source calls it India's first; purpose is networking researchers, conservationists and citizens, with local-language information beginning in Assamese.
  • Citizen observations, awareness and coordination complement safe carcass supply, drug-law enforcement and protected nesting habitat. The issue also illustrates successful Indian long-billed vulture breeding at Ramadevarabetta, Karnataka; existing Gyps indicus/habitat and diclofenac notes apply.
  • Embedded species table: white-rumped, slender-billed, Indian/long-billed and red-headed Critically Endangered; Egyptian Endangered; Indian griffon Least Concern; Himalayan griffon, cinereous and bearded Near Threatened in the source's status snapshot. Do not generalise the source's 97–99% decline to every species or all periods; its separate chart covers later 2003-onward declines.
  • 2012 PYQ: (b), veterinary drug exposure, not invasive nesting-site species, simple food scarcity or an unspecified epidemic. The source's “only sanctuary” language is broader than its documented 2012 first-sanctuary history.

December 2025: planned Assam release

  • BNHS planned to release six captive-bred vultures, three males and three females, aged around 2–3 years, in January 2026 in Assam. Species: slender-billed, Gyps tenuirostris, and white-rumped, Gyps bengalensis, both Critically Endangered. This is a plan reported in December, not a completed release.
  • Bred at Rani Vulture Conservation Breeding Centre, Kamrup; source reports over 800 bred across BNHS centres. Kamrup and Biswanath were chosen within natural range and near existing flocks; Biswanath is near Kaziranga. These are source-era programme figures.
  • Soft release: roughly three months in acclimatisation enclosures → release → BNHS/Forest Department monitoring. Exposure to other scavengers and local conditions helps captive-bred birds adapt; safe carcasses, community cooperation and drug-law enforcement remain essential.
  • Existing notes cover diclofenac-mediated kidney failure, the 2006 veterinary prohibition, separate 2015 multidose human-formulation restriction, safe zones, captive breeding and species statuses. Do not copy the PDF's misplaced Himalayan griffon under an “Endangered” heading: existing corrected snapshot is Near Threatened. Nor treat “~20,000 vultures” as a secure all-species census or apply one decline percentage to every species.
  • The Assam portal section repeats the earlier We Foundation India/Gauhati University launch and Assamese-language network; no new launch is established by republication. Additional cultural recall: vultures figure in Parsi funerary practice at Towers of Silence; scavenging reduces carcass-management costs and some disease risks, not every pathogen.
  • Practice keys: veterinary diclofenac (b); released species Critically Endangered (d); portal statement set 1 and 2 only (a) because the Rajasthan-2024 claim is false. The 250-word/15-mark vulture Mains prompt has no exam year, so remains practice/review material rather than a PYQ bank entry.

January 2026: proposed Tamil Nadu Vulture Safe Zones

  • Tamil Nadu Forest Department told Madras High Court it was identifying Vulture Safe Zones, with the first proposed for the Moyar River Valley, Nilgiri Biosphere Reserve, following a PIL. Identification/committee work is not a completed legal declaration of every zone.
  • Field monitoring committee chaired by Field Director, Mudumalai Tiger Reserve includes forest officers from Nilgiris, Gudalur, Coimbatore and Erode, Anamalai/Sathyamangalam reserve officers and the Advanced Institute for Wildlife Conservation, Vandalur. Task: map nesting/roosting and habitat-use sites, identify carcass hotspots and recommend suitable zones under the State's 2025–30 vulture vision document.
  • A VSZ combines safe carcass food, toxic veterinary-drug control, habitat protection, monitoring and veterinary/livestock-owner outreach; merely naming a zone cannot make all carcasses drug-free. Existing diclofenac/nimesulide distinctions, safe alternatives and ecological sanitation functions remain covered; not every NSAID has the same vulture toxicity.
  • Pages 3–8 repeat December's BNHS Assam release plan and September's portal launch, not a newly confirmed release or portal inauguration. The six captive-bred slender-billed/white-rumped vultures, three-month soft release, Rani/Kamrup/Biswanath locations and We Foundation/Gauhati University portal retain their earlier dates and qualifications.
  • Reprinted Himalayan griffon “Endangered” grouping, generic ~20,000 population and universal lifespan/immunity statements are unreliable species-wide generalisations. Practice questions remain practice; no missing Mains year is invented. Articles 48A/51A(g) support conservation duties without making every reported committee plan an already operative protected-area notification.

February 2026: Himalayan griffon in Melghat

  • Himalayan griffon, Gyps himalayensis, is an Old World vulture (Accipitridae) associated with the Himalaya, Tibetan Plateau and Central Asian uplands; it also disperses seasonally to lower elevations and beyond its breeding range. Near Threatened, not the source appendix's misplaced Endangered label. Convention on Migratory Species.
  • Source reports a sighting in Melghat, Amravati, Maharashtra, in the Satpura landscape, associated with BNHS rewilding/captive-bred vulture activity. This is an observation of visiting birds, not proof a new self-sustaining Himalayan griffon breeding population has been established in Maharashtra. “Biodiversity hotspot” here is descriptive, not designation as one of the formally recognised global hotspots.
  • Large, diurnal carrion-feeder, roughly 2.5–3 m wingspan, commonly cliff-nesting; nesting pairs may reuse sites. Old World vultures and New World Cathartidae are different families. The latter do not all possess identical olfactory abilities, so the table's strong-versus-weak smell contrast is a broad simplification.
  • Pages 4–8 reproduce the already-covered six-vulture January release plan in Assam and earlier We Foundation/Gauhati University portal, not confirmation of a newly completed release or new February portal launch. Retain the existing species, Rani/Kamrup/Biswanath, soft-release and drug-safety qualifications. Practice questions remain outside the CSE bank.
