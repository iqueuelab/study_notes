# Rhisotope: nuclear markers against wildlife trafficking

## Marker → border detection → deterrence
- **University of the Witwatersrand (South Africa)** with **IAEA** and partners: insert low-level radioisotopes into rhino horns so existing customs radiation detectors can identify trafficking; project moved to operational deployment in **August 2025**.
- Radioisotopes are unstable nuclei emitting radiation during decay; natural (C-14) and artificial (Co-60) examples illustrate the class, not disclosure of the exact horn isotope. Other uses include medical imaging/radiotherapy, industrial inspection/sterilisation, tracers and agriculture.
- Pilot **20 rhinos** in **Waterberg Biosphere, Limpopo**; veterinary/blood-cell tests found no observed harm at tested doses. Detection tests used **3D-printed horns** with comparable shielding, including a **40-foot container**. Distinguish tested safety/detection from proven long-term reductions in poaching.
- PDF claims horns become “toxic to humans/worthless” and calls procedure non-invasive; do not use these as established conclusions. It involves inserting markers, with dose controlled for safety; the verified mechanism is **traceability/deterrence**, not poisoning consumers. [Wits1 August 2025 operational report](https://www.wits.ac.za/future/stories/2025/rhisotope-project-goes-live.html).
- Poaching for traditional-medicine/status markets persists; source global population~27,000 and historical500,000 are approximate dated estimates. It lists103 early 2025 cases without a clear period/geography; not a verified whole-year South African total.
- **Waterberg**: Bushveld landscape, water/biodiversity and San cultural heritage; **Nylsvley** is a Ramsar wetland in Limpopo, but proximity/region alone does not prove inclusion within the biosphere's boundary. Nuclear markers supplement surveillance, habitat protection and community action; scaling to elephants/pangolins remains a possibility.
