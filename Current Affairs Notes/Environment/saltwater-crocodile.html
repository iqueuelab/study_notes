Saltwater crocodiles and India’s crocodilian conservation

Editable notes

Sundarban survey and habitat

  • Embedded survey clipping reports estimated Sundarban Biosphere Reserve saltwater crocodiles: 220–242 in 2025, compared with 204–234 in 2024. 213 direct sightings in 2025: 125 adults, 65 juveniles and 23 hatchlings, versus 114 in 2024: 71 adults, 41 juveniles and two hatchlings. Estimated ranges and direct sightings are different measures; this is a regional survey, not an all-India census. The report, “Population Assessment and Habitat Ecology Study of Saltwater Crocodiles in Sundarbans 2025”, used systematic surveys, GPS mapping and habitat characterisation; sighting frequency was around one per 5.5 km.
  • Crocodylus porosus, estuarine/saltwater crocodile: largest living reptile; inhabits mangroves, brackish estuaries and coastal wetlands and can cross open sea using currents. Indian anchors: Sundarbans, Bhitarkanika, Andaman & Nicobar; wider range extends through South/Southeast Asia to northern Australia and western Pacific islands.
  • Three native Indian crocodilians: saltwater crocodile, mugger and gharial. Threats include wetland/mangrove destruction, fragmentation, fishing entanglement, poaching and conflict. Protection and healthy habitat remain necessary even for an IUCN Least Concern species.
  • Indian population: WPA Schedule I; CITES Appendix I. CITES has Appendix II exceptions for certain other national populations; “all international movement banned everywhere” is inaccurate. CITES Appendices.
  • 2017 wildlife-law PYQ: D, gharial, Indian wild ass and wild buffalo all protected, subject to statutory exceptions. Section 11 addresses specified dangerous or irrecoverably injured/diseased animals with statutory safeguards; self-defence is not a licence for unlawful prior conduct.
  • 2017 gharial habitat PYQ: B, Chambal River; fish-eating, slender-snouted gharials inhabit freshwater rivers. Chambal is a major population, not India's only viable population. 2013 fauna PYQ: C (all three), historical exam answer; do not transplant that generic “endangered” label into a current IUCN list. Source practice B (2 and 3) despite its explanation misnumbering statements.
