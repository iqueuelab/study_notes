# AI, data centres and electricity demand

## May 2025: environmental footprint
- AI training and inference require computing, electricity and cooling; emissions depend on model/task, hardware efficiency, utilisation and electricity mix. A viral image-generation trend illustrates demand, not a measured universal footprint.
- The PDF's **“10% of world electricity by 2030”** is not the IEA base case: **Energy and AI (April 2025)** projected data centres at about **945 TWh, just under 3% globally in 2030**. This includes non-AI workloads; global shares can hide concentrated local-grid stresses. [IEA demand analysis](https://www.iea.org/reports/energy-and-ai/energy-demand-from-ai).
- The “five cars over their lifetime” comparison came from particular model-training assumptions, not a universal metric for every AI model or user request. “GPUs melting” is rhetorical, not proof of literal equipment failure.
- Disclose energy use, power sources, carbon intensity and mitigation; distinguish training/inference, facility/computing energy and operational/lifecycle emissions. Efficient models/hardware, cooling, demand scheduling and cleaner grids can reduce impacts.
## SMRs: possible firm low-carbon supply
- **Small modular reactors**, generally up to **300 MWe per module**, offer a possible continuous non-weather-dependent complement to renewables; see common SMR topic for India's mission/development status.
- Modular manufacture and passive safety can help selected designs; faster construction, cheaper power and safe siting near every data centre are **potential benefits**, not guaranteed outcomes. Nuclear has low lifecycle emissions, not literally zero environmental impact.
- Cost, financing, licensing, emergency planning, fuel supply, spent fuel/waste, water/cooling and public acceptance constrain deployment. The source's **₹10.3 → ₹5/kWh** is a projection lacking clear design/date assumptions, not an achieved tariff.
- Combine reliable clean power, renewables/storage/grid investment and efficiency; public–private research is proposed, not evidence SMRs already power Indian AI facilities or that existing capacity guarantees all future demand.

## July policy connection
- Source links AI expansion in **Bengaluru/Hyderabad** with local water and power stress; incorporate cooling-water demand, location/grid constraints and environmental disclosure into AI planning. Avoid interpreting every data centre's total demand as AI-only consumption or claiming no Indian environmental-policy discussion exists.

## October 2025: data-centre power density
- Source compares enterprise racks **15–20 kW** with AI racks **80–150 kW**: indicative power-density ranges, not proof all AI centres use exactly five to seven times the electricity. Facility utilisation, cooling, efficiency and workload affect annual demand.
- India **1.4 GW** data-centre capacity and potential fivefold growth by 2030 are source-era estimate/projection, not delivered generation or a measured AI-only load. Announced Visakhapatnam/Jamnagar hubs are not proof all capacity is operational.
- Retain the IEA baseline already explained above rather than the PDF's inconsistent 460 TWh/2024 and 1,000–1,300 TWh range without matched scenarios. Data centres are significant growing users, not established as the world's largest electricity-consuming sector. Natural gas backup emits carbon; fusion is not a commercially available near-term solution.

## January 2026: siting data centres within local resource limits
- Data centres support cloud/AI, payments, commerce and government services, but concentrate **electricity, cooling-water, backup-diesel, heat and construction burdens**. The editorial's “data dumping ground” is a warning about externalised costs, not a legal category or proof all Indian projects lack public benefits.
- Source forecasts **77% capacity growth by 2028 /8–9 GW by 2030**; these are projections with different horizons and unspecified baselines, **not achieved generation capacity or AI-only demand**. Compare local substation/grid upgrades and water availability with facility loads rather than extrapolate national averages to every locality.
- Proposed safeguards: assess cumulative power/water impacts before siting; avoid ecologically fragile/water-stressed locations where impacts cannot be mitigated; require efficient cooling and appropriate **non-potable/recycled water**; disclose water source/cooling and energy/emissions; publish audits/incidents and enforce post-clearance conditions. These are recommendations, not evidence of an enacted nationwide categorical siting ban.
- Condition incentives on measurable public benefit and environmental performance; ensure grid upgrades are funded fairly, consultation is meaningful and confidentiality does not obscure impacts. Air/liquid/evaporative cooling involve different energy–water tradeoffs; “liquid cooling” alone does not establish zero water use. Chile/US/European community opposition provides the source's social-acceptance examples, not one identical legal outcome everywhere.
