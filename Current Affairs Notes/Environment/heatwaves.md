# Heatwaves, inequality and heat action plans

## Heat risk and inequality
- WMO identified **2024 as the warmest year on record**, global mean temperature **1.55 ± 0.13°C above 1850–1900**. One calendar year's exceedance is not identical to breaching the Paris Agreement's long-term warming threshold. [WMO 2024 report](https://wmo.int/publication-series/state-of-global-climate/state-of-global-climate-2024).
- Heat exposure reflects temperature, humidity, solar radiation, wind, work intensity and night-time cooling. High humidity limits evaporative cooling; heat-related illness can become life-threatening. Body temperature around 37°C is not a universal outdoor danger threshold.
- Outdoor/agricultural/construction workers face lost working capacity and wages; crops/livestock and food security are affected. Older people, migrants, informal workers and poorly housed households may lack cooling, water and access to services.
- Gendered exposure can arise from caregiving, unpaid work, access to resources and local social norms; vulnerability is context-specific. Avoid treating all women as having the same clothing or work pattern.
## Action plans
- **Ahmedabad's 2013 heat-health action plan** was a pioneering South Asian city initiative, combining early warning, public communication and health preparedness. [Original implementation study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4024996/).
| Immediate preparedness | Structural adaptation |
|---|---|
| Local alerts; communication in accessible languages | Cool/reflective roofs, shade, ventilation and urban trees |
| Drinking-water access and shaded rest/cooling spaces | Heat-sensitive housing and public-space design |
| Adjust outdoor work hours and rest breaks | Wage-loss protection and inclusive access to cooling |
| Health-worker training and surveillance | Funded local coordination across health, labour and planning |
- Plans should integrate humidity, hot nights and neighbourhood vulnerability; assign responsibilities, finance implementation and track outcomes. Link health preparedness with NPCCHH and disaster-management institutions.
- Air conditioning protects users but can increase electricity demand, emissions and outdoor waste heat; passive cooling, efficient appliances and cleaner electricity reduce this adaptation trade-off.
- The source's **20 days earlier**, workforce/GDP/work-hours figures and **23 states/140 cities** counts lack a consistent cited denominator/time series here; do not memorise them as verified national totals. Prioritise exposure, feasible protection and accountable local implementation.
## May 2025: informal workers and heat governance
- Source reports Delhi temperatures above **41°C in April**; construction workers, vendors, gig workers and other informal workers face exposure and lost earnings while lacking reliable employer protections. A single reading is not by itself a complete attribution study or official heatwave declaration.
- **Heat Action Plans** often lack secure funding, occupation-specific protocols, wage/health protection and coordination between labour, urban, environment and health agencies. “No guidelines/protection anywhere” is too absolute; the issue argues that practical coverage and enforcement remain inadequate.
- Editorial cites **4.5% GDP loss** attributed to RBI 2024 without a clear scenario/time horizon; treat as projection requiring verification, not measured 2024 loss. It advocates worker participation and bottom-up assessment.
## Worker-centred recommendations
- Revise national guidance to explicitly cover informal/outdoor occupations; involve unions/collectives; establish safe drinking-water, gender-sensitive shade/rest/cooling at markets, transport hubs, labour chowks and construction sites; adjust working hours and rest schedules.
- Finance through city budgets/CSR/community contributions with accountable maintenance; examine insurance and wage-loss protection. Embed cool roofs, shaded walkways, passive ventilation, trees/blue infrastructure and workspace retrofits in master plans, building by-laws and infrastructure standards.
- Proposed **inter-ministerial Climate and Work Task Force** (Labour, Urban Affairs, Environment, Health, NDMA) and dedicated **city/district Heat Officers** are recommendations, not offices proved established by this article.
- Comparators: California/Oregon heat-safety rules; France working-hour/cooling measures; Qatar outdoor-work restrictions; Australian heat-risk controls vary by jurisdiction, so avoid a blanket national ban. Indian examples include Ahmedabad work/rest adaptations and Odisha peak-hour restrictions; check local scope and dates before operational application.
- Monitor illness, exposure and income losses alongside implementation; heat adaptation must protect livelihoods as well as announce alerts.

## 15 May: Andhra Pradesh and Telangana
- Source reports May 12 temperatures above **41 °C in 17 AP districts** and April readings above 40 °C across Telangana’s 33 districts, while labelling the section 2024 ; this is an internally ambiguous year context. Do not silently assign all readings to 2025 or infer an official heatwave from temperature alone.
- Both States lie **south of**, rather than on/especially close to, the Tropic of Cancer. Strong pre-monsoon insolation, dry soil, sparse clouds and limited evapotranspiration contribute to heat; Telangana’s inland plateau differs from humid coastal AP.
- Rock/soil properties, vegetation, irrigation and built surfaces influence local energy balance; black soil alone is not a universal explanation. Blanket claims that both States have little irrigation or no large water bodies are misleading.
- Dry heat and humid heat pose different risks; coastal humidity limits sweat evaporation, while inland dryness/low soil moisture can intensify daytime heating. Monsoon onset varies year-to-year: “after June 10 ” is not an invariant rule.
- Priorities: local forecasts and hourly/accessible warnings where feasible, district heat-risk maps, funded action plans, shade/water/rest and health surveillance; water-efficient agriculture, micro-irrigation, rainwater harvesting and appropriate urban greening. Expansion of summer cropping must respect water scarcity, rather than assume irrigation always provides a sustainable heat solution.
