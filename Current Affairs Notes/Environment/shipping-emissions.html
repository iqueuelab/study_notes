Environment

IMO shipping emissions and Net-Zero Framework

Editable notes

April 2025 decision: legal status matters

  • At MEPC 83, 7–11 April 2025, the International Maritime Organization (IMO; London headquarters) approved draft MARPOL Annex VI amendments for its Net-Zero Framework. India's favourable vote was among 63 supporting countries in the supplied account. The US did not participate in the vote; absence must not be relabelled abstention.
  • Approval of a draft is distinct from formal adoption, acceptance, entry into force and enforcement. The headline “global carbon tax” compresses a fuel standard and a linked pricing system.

How the draft works

ElementMeaning
ScopeLarge ocean-going ships over 5,000 gross tonnage, covering most international-shipping emissions
Fuel standardProgressively lower annual GHG fuel intensity: emissions per unit of energy
AccountingWell-to-wake: fuel production/supply plus use aboard ship
ComplianceBase and direct-compliance thresholds; surplus-unit transfers/banking and remedial units for deficits
EconomicsTwo-tier emissions pricing; zero/near-zero emission performance can earn rewards

Cleaner fuel/technology → lower lifecycle fuel intensity → smaller deficit or surplus → lower compliance cost. This is not one universal flat tax on every ship's total emissions. IMO April announcement.

Climate and equity

  • Shipping produces roughly 3% of global GHG emissions. Cross-border voyages complicate attribution; IMO supplies the dedicated sectoral regime. The Paris Agreement does not set this specific international-shipping fuel/pricing mechanism; that does not exempt maritime activity from climate policy or remove domestic shipping from national climate action.
  • IMO's 2023 strategy seeks net zero by/around 2050, with total annual GHG reductions of at least 20%, striving for 30%, by 2030, against 2008. These are strategy ambitions, not a guaranteed result of the draft.
  • Proposed Net-Zero Fund functions include clean-ship rewards, innovation, infrastructure, capacity building and mitigating impacts on vulnerable states. Revenue forecasts such as the PDF's “up to $40 billion by 2030” are projections, not collected revenue.
  • Source criticism: island/developing states sought greater climate-equity support and questioned negotiating transparency/ambition; fossil-fuel-exporting opponents resisted the mechanism. The PDF's modelled 10% emissions reduction is an attributed projection, not an observed outcome.
  • India: cleaner ports/fuels and fleet renewal must be balanced against freight costs, export competitiveness, fuel availability and the needs of poorer trading states. IMO climate strategy and impact work.

Later development — 17 October 2025

Adoption talks were adjourned for one year, superseding April's planned October adoption. The PDF's 2028 implementation expectation therefore remains a former target, not an operative deadline. The framework was not made binding by the April approval. IMO October statement.

May 13 additions: maritime strategy and draft status

  • 2023 IMO strategy: at least 40% carbon-intensity reduction by 2030 versus 2008; total annual GHG reductions by 2040: at least 70%, striving for 80%. The PDF confuses the 2040 total-emission checkpoint with an intensity target. EEDI, SEEMP and mandatory fuel-use reporting complement EEXI/CII measures.
  • Draft amendment adoption requires the applicable MARPOL parties' process, not a simple vote of every IMO member. Tacit acceptance has objection thresholds: at least one-third of parties or parties representing at least 50% of merchant-fleet gross tonnage; these are alternatives, not a combined threshold. IMO MEPC83 summary.
  • Source describes Indian bridging/Singapore hybrid proposals linking underperformance charges with zero/near-zero-fuel rewards; ICS/EU favoured levy variants, China favoured flexible trading/technology approaches. Negotiating positions are source accounts, not final binding national obligations.
  • Source's 19 gCO2e/MJ until 2034, 14 from 2035 refers to proposed zero/near-zero-fuel qualification, not automatic certification of every Indian hydrogen shipment. Lifecycle production/transport/use emissions must be assessed.
  • India could supply lower-emission marine fuels and bunkering infrastructure, supported by National Green Hydrogen Mission; opportunity depends on verified emissions, competitive cost, infrastructure and contracts.
  • The source's 236 Indian ships >5,000 GT /135 international, projected trade-cost increases 5–8% by 2030 /33–35% by 2050, and old 50–250% emissions growth scenario are dated scope/model estimates, not achieved effects. Its 90% global-trade share is boundary-dependent; UNCTAD commonly uses over 80% by volume.
  • CBDR-RC informs climate-equity debate, while IMO's ship rules also seek consistent application. SIDS seek finance/ambition; developing fleets face technology/cost gaps. Avoid assuming national interest establishes all oil-exporting states' identical position.
  • The US announced Paris withdrawal in January 2025; it had not completed the one-year withdrawal process by April/May. Aviation comparison should distinguish ICAO's aspirational long-term goal from enforceable aviation measures such as CORSIA, rather than calling the whole sector unregulated.

October 2025: adoption session adjourned

  • The issue reports a 57–49 vote, with 21 abstentions/non-votes in its account, to postpone the adoption process for one year; it reports US diplomatic pressure and strong criticism from climate-vulnerable island states. India's October vote is not established by this source.
  • IMO confirms the 14–17 October 2025 extraordinary MEPC session was adjourned for twelve months, with implementation-guideline work continuing. Existing notes already record this outcome. The PDF's April 2024/October 2024 dates are errors: draft approval was April 2025; neither its planned 2027 start nor adoption was an operative requirement after adjournment. IMO session record.
  • Delay increases uncertainty for investment in low-emission fuels and ships and intensifies debate over freight costs, sovereign policy space and support for vulnerable trading states. The repeated 2040 target must remain a total-GHG reduction checkpoint, not a carbon-intensity target.
