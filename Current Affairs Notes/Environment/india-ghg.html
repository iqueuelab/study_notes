Environment

India’s greenhouse-gas inventory and climate commitments

Editable notes

Inventory: use a stated year and denominator

  • India's BUR-4, submitted 30 December 2024, reports 2020 emissions. Sector shares: energy 75.66%; agriculture 13.72%; IPPU 8.06%; waste 2.56%. Correct source's loose 74/14/8/4 “latest” split. Official inventory summary.
  • Fossil combustion →CO₂; livestock/rice/waste →CH₄; fertiliser/industrial processes →N₂O; refrigeration and specialised processes →fluorinated gases.
  • India's large total emissions coexist with lower per-capita emissions; “third largest /7%” depends on year, gases, territorial accounting and inclusion of land use.
  • Source gas shares 75/15/7/<3 are not a verified inventory table; compare gases using CO₂-equivalent and a stated warming-potential methodology.

Commitments and programmes

  • Updated 2022 NDC: 45% reduction in GDP emission intensity by 2030 from 2005; about 50% cumulative installed electricity capacity from non-fossil sources; additional 2.5–3 billion tonnes CO₂-equivalent forest/tree sink by 2030.
  • Net-zero 2070, announced at COP26; 500 GW non-fossil capacity by 2030 is a separate policy ambition. Neither means 50% of actual generation or an absolute 45% emission cut.
  • Solar mission 2010, renewable deployment, PAT efficiency, clean transport, green hydrogen 2023 /5 MMT annually by 2030, afforestation and waste/bioenergy improvements.
  • Source assigns the whole 500 GW target to the original Solar Mission and claims “40% renewable electricity”: inaccurate scope/denominators. Non-fossil includes nuclear; programmes/timetables differ.

Equity and implementation

  • Coal dependence, growing demand, technology/finance access, agricultural methane and difficult industrial processes; protect energy access and a just transition while reducing intensity.
  • Mitigation reduces emissions; adaptation funds reduce vulnerability, not necessarily carbon output. Forest sinks need ecological integrity and durable protection.
  • GHG Protocol: emissions accounting/management standards, not a UN treaty or World Bank REDD+ programme. INDCs: countries' intended climate-action pledges, not refugee or AIIB commitments.

September 2025: preparing the next NDC

  • Ahead of COP30, Belém, November 2025, the clipping discusses a possible stronger Indian energy/emissions-efficiency target. This is a prospective NDC revision, not proof a new target was formally submitted or adopted. Successive NDCs are communicated every five years and should progress in ambition; equity, development needs, finance and technology remain central.
  • Existing 2022 targets distinguish emissions intensity, non-fossil installed capacity and additional forest/tree carbon sinks. The source's 50% non-fossil capacity by June 2023 date is wrong: the milestone was June 2025, already explained in renewable-energy notes. Its 33% intensity reduction through 2019 is an earlier assessment, not a September 2025 measurement; BUR-4 reports 36% through 2020. Government climate summary.
  • Carbon-market discussion anticipates 2026 operation; do not infer completed trading from the plan. CCTS uses carbon-credit certificates and has compliance/offset mechanisms; its transition from PAT concerns nine identified energy-intensive sectors, not an unqualified 13-sector mandatory market. Scheme/sector notification and actual issuance/trading are different stages. BEE framework.
  • Source's ~3°C warming is an assessment/scenario claim, not a guaranteed outcome. EU 66–72% and Australia's 62–70% 2035 figures are source-period discussions/announcements with different baseline years, not India's targets. Do not treat “US out” as an already effective September 2025 exit merely from a withdrawal announcement.
  • Climate-finance adequacy remains disputed, but “the $100-billion promise has never been met” is inaccurate: OECD reports $115.9 billion in 2022, first exceeding it two years late. This does not establish adequate grants, adaptation finance or fairness. OECD assessment.
  • 2016 PYQ (b): INDCs concern countries' climate-action plans. Existing JCM notes distinguish India's August 2025 agreement with Japan from credit issuance and explain Article 6 accounting.

2024 emissions: separate report boundaries

  • The issue combines figures from different datasets. UNEP Emissions Gap Report 2025 puts global 2024 GHG emissions at 57.7 Gt CO₂e, up 2.3%, not the sheet's 2.7%. That is a global total, not India's emissions. UNEP November 2025 assessment.
  • EDGAR 2025 reports 53.2 Gt CO₂e excluding LULUCF, up 1.3%; in that accounting India had the largest absolute annual increase, 164.8 Mt CO₂e, while Indonesia had the largest percentage rise among the six largest emitters. The sheet's “India highest emissions” headline confuses largest increment with largest total: China and the US remained above India. JRC EDGAR 2025.
  • Do not join the sheet's 69% fossil CO₂ /16% methane, ~3 t Indian/~6.4 t global per capita, 3.7% Indian/0.04% global per-capita growth and +1,500 Mt into a single verified inventory. Boundaries, population series, GWP conventions and LULUCF treatment must match; these figures are source-era claims requiring their original table.
  • Energy demand, industry, transport, agriculture and land-use change explain different emission components. High national totals and lower per-capita output are compatible; low per-capita emissions do not make rising absolute emissions irrelevant.
  • Existing 2022 NDC/intensity/capacity/forest-sink commitments remain distinct from the 500 GW ambition and net-zero 2070. Non-fossil installed capacity is not actual generation, and intensity reductions are not absolute emission reductions. Efficiency, clean power/storage, industrial transition, transport, methane control and durable sinks all matter.

January 2026: capacity, intensity and ecological outcomes

  • Editorial asks whether headline progress delivers durable mitigation. 36% emissions-intensity reduction through 2020 from 2005 and non-fossil installed capacity passing 50% in June 2025 repeat existing milestones; the applicable 2022 NDC targets are 45% intensity reduction and about 50% non-fossil capacity by 2030. The older 33–35%/40% pair belongs to the original NDC. 175 GW renewable capacity by 2022 was a separate domestic target, not a new 2030 Paris pledge.
  • Falling emissions/GDP can coexist with rising total emissions (relative rather than absolute decoupling). Installed MW/GW, actual electricity MWh/TWh, non-fossil including nuclear, and renewable generation shares are distinct. Growing steel, cement and transport demand matters alongside power-sector change.
  • Source ~2,959 Mt CO₂e in 2020 is historical gross inventory context, not January emissions; power figures ~111 GW solar /51 GW wind, coal >70% generation and renewables ~22% need matching dates/definitions. Its 336 GWh storage need /500 MWh installed September 2025 may compare battery-only with broader storage categories: do not treat this as total Indian storage including pumped hydro.
  • Carbon-sink accounting and ecosystem health differ: plantations/tree cover cannot substitute automatically for biodiverse natural forests. The issue's ~2.29 Gt added sink still falls below the 2.5–3 Gt range; “already met” overstates even its own figures. Verify assessment period/method before comparing inventories; reported CAMPA ₹95,000 crore /Delhi 23% need fund/utilisation periods.
  • Priorities: batteries/pumped storage, grid flexibility, energy efficiency/PAT/UJALA, industrial decarbonisation, a just coal transition, native-habitat regeneration and transparent ecological/fiscal outcomes. Finance and technology require coordination and accountable implementation.
