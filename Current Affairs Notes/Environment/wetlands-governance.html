Wetlands: governance, Ramsar sites and traditional knowledge

Editable notes

Wetlands as public goods: 3 February 2026

  • Wetlands support water storage, flood moderation, biodiversity, fisheries/livelihoods, climate resilience and cultural practices. Treat them as living ecosystems and shared resilience infrastructure rather than vacant development land or ornamental ponds.
  • World Wetlands Day: 2 February; 2026 theme Wetlands and traditional knowledge: Celebrating cultural heritage. Examples in the issue: Tamil Nadu's cascading kulam tanks, Wayanad's kenis and Srikakulam fishing practices; traditional knowledge can complement hydrology and ecological science. Convention campaign.
  • Main pressures: encroachment/obsolete land records; dams, road embankments, channel blockage, sand mining and groundwater extraction; untreated sewage/industrial effluent/agricultural runoff; coastal development, cyclones and sea-level rise; weak staffing, enforcement and interdepartmental coordination.
  • Response: public maps and participatory boundary notification; restore feeder channels and catchments; treat wastewater before entry; regulate extraction/development; protect mangrove/floodplain buffers; train wetland authorities in hydrology, GIS, ecology, law and community engagement. Constructed treatment wetlands can supplement engineered treatment; natural wetlands should not become sewage sinks.
  • Wetlands Rules 2017 under the Environment (Protection) Act 1986, NPCA conservation support, coastal regulation and the Ramsar framework have different scopes. State/UT wetland authorities need capacity and measurable ecological outcomes. Rule coverage has specific exclusions/overlapping legal regimes; Ramsar's broad wetland definition does not mean every river channel or irrigation tank falls under the 2017 Rules. MoEFCC implementation guidance.
  • The editorial's 40% lost/50% degraded figures lack a specified reproducible national baseline here; do not present them as official census results. Monitor area, water quality, hydroperiod, biodiversity and livelihood outcomes using satellite time series and community evidence. The supplied 250-word/15-mark question is practice, not a UPSC PYQ.

Patna Bird Sanctuary and Chhari Dhand

  • The Ramsar Secretariat announced 30 January 2026 that these two sites brought India's total to 98. This is the January/early-February snapshot, not an undated present total. Secretariat announcement.

Convention and register recall

  • Convention adopted 1971, Ramsar (Iran); in force 1975, India party 1982. Ramsar sites are designated wetlands; the Montreux Record (1990) separately records listed sites with actual/likely ecological-character change from human pressures. Keoladeo and Loktak remain India's standard Montreux examples; Chilika was removed in 2002.
  • India's Sundarban Wetland is the largest Indian Ramsar site; Vembannur, Tamil Nadu (~19.75 ha) is a very small site. The source inconsistently gives Tamil Nadu 20 and 14: the 20 figure is the issue-era count; 14 is stale. Pantanal's description as a vast tropical wetland must not be turned into a reliable ranking of the world's largest single Ramsar site.
  • Wetlands include marshes, peatlands, mangroves, lagoons, floodplains, saline/freshwater and seasonal/permanent systems. Their ecological definition and the narrower coverage of particular Indian rules are different. The embedded 2018 OAS Montreux question is State-PSC, excluded from CSE banks.
