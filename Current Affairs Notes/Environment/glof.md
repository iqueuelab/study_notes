# Glacial lake outburst floods: Himalayan risk and preparedness

## Mechanism and exposure
- Glacier retreat/melt creates lakes: **supraglacial** lakes sit on the glacier; **moraine-dammed** lakes are impounded by debris, often ice-cored. Sudden release after dam failure/overtopping is a **GLOF**. Avalanches, landslides, earthquakes, heavy inflow and weak moraine can trigger cascading floods; not every lake is equally dangerous.
- **4 October 2023, South Lhonak, Sikkim:** outburst in the Teesta basin destroyed **Teesta III/Chungthang dam**, damaging bridges, homes, roads and downstream hydropower. Source counts **14 bridges, 1,320 houses and 35+ deaths** are dated estimates, not a final reconciled toll.
- Warming/retreat can enlarge lakes; avalanches and dam geometry govern individual hazard. Infrastructure/settlements in flood paths increase exposure. **Kedarnath 2013 was a multicausal flood/debris disaster**, not proof every Himalayan flood is a GLOF; volcanic jökulhlaups elsewhere are not a dominant Indian Himalayan trigger.
- Source's **28,000+ lakes**, Sikkim **733/10 highly vulnerable**, Uttarakhand **486/13 vulnerable**, and J&K superlative mix inventory years, boundaries and screening methods; treat as source-specific figures pending matched primary inventories, not a current national ranking.
## Preparedness
- Satellite mapping, field bathymetry/moraine assessment, weather/lake/river sensors and **SAR interferometry** for slope motion; prioritise hazards and update downstream flood models.
- Early warning must connect detection to redundant communications, community evacuation drills and safe shelters. Dams, bridges and highways need sediment/debris, seismic and cascading-risk assessment; evaluate cumulative catchment/hydropower impacts and regulate construction near flow paths.
- Protect lives, livelihoods, connectivity and water security with science-led catchment planning and local participation; afforestation or one sensor alone cannot remove GLOF risk.

## Mechanism
- Glacier melt/retreat creates lakes; **supraglacial lakes** sit on ice, while **moraine-dammed lakes** are held by loose rock/debris, sometimes ice-cored. Failure releases a sudden downstream flood (**GLOF**); retreat alone does not guarantee an outburst.
- Ice/rock avalanche → displacement wave/overtopping → moraine erosion/breach → debris-rich downstream flood. Earthquakes, slope failure, rapid inflow and weak dams compound risk; glacier surges can temporarily block drainage. Construction/deforestation can increase exposure or slope instability. Volcanic melting is a global trigger, not a typical Indian Himalayan explanation.
## South Lhonak and exposure
- **4 October 2023, South Lhonak, Sikkim:** outburst devastated the Teesta valley and **1,200 MW Teesta III/Chungthang dam**; roads, bridges, homes, hydropower and livelihoods were damaged. Source reports **14 bridges,1,320 homes,35+ deaths**: dated reported estimates, not a final reconciled toll. Kedarnath 2013 involved intense rain and Chorabari lake flooding; the disasters are not identical mechanisms.
- Issue cites **28,000+ lakes** for the Indian Himalayan region; embedded clipping distinguishes the wider Himalayan region and **over 7,500 in India**. Inventory boundary, minimum size and survey date matter—do not use 28,000 as a verified India-only total. Its Sikkim **733/10 high-risk**, Uttarakhand **486/13 vulnerable** and J&K ranking are differently sourced snapshots, not a common current risk census.
## Reduce risk
- Satellite/drone mapping, field bathymetry and moraine/slope assessment; **SAR interferometry** detects slope movement. Weather/lake/river sensors, resilient warning communication and evacuation drills require local maintenance and community participation.
- Risk-based prioritisation, lake-lowering or other engineering where scientifically justified, land-use restrictions and sediment/runout modelling; assess cascading effects on hydropower, roads and bridges.
- **NDMA National GLOF Risk Mitigation Programme**, coordination with States/scientific agencies, catchment-scale risk assessment and updated design standards support preparedness. Avoid presenting continuous monitoring or every planned mitigation survey as already completed.
