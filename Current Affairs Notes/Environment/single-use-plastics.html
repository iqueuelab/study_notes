Environment

Single-use plastics, microbeads and circularity

Editable notes

Problem and policy

  • SUP products are intended for one/few uses before disposal: bags/films, cutlery, straws and other packaging; medical disposables also require safe segregated handling. Persistence, fragmentation into microplastics, blocked drains and wildlife exposure make brief use costly environmentally.
  • India's 2021 rule amendment prohibited identified low-utility/high-litter SUP items from 1 July 2022. Packaging outside that list is governed through plastic-waste rules and 2022 EPR guidelines; this is not a ban on all plastics. Multilayer packaging poses recycling challenges, but not every multilayer structure has identical recyclability. MoEFCC account.
  • Barriers: cheap incumbent materials, costly/inaccessible substitutes, livelihood dependence, poor segregation/collection and uneven enforcement. Responses: transparent compliance data and inspections, reuse/refill systems, support for alternatives and reliable collection/recycling. A ban on bags of every thickness is a proposal in the source, not the prevailing national rule.
  • International examples include Bangladesh's 2002 thin-bag ban and EU restrictions taking effect in 2021. UNEA resolution 5/14, 2 March 2022, launched negotiations for a legally binding plastics instrument; it was not itself a completed treaty signed by 124 countries. UNEP resolution.

Microbeads and PYQ

  • Manufactured tiny plastic particles, commonly PE/PP/polystyrene, used as scrubbers in some personal-care/cleaning products; often <5 mm. Escape through wastewater depends on treatment, rather than all plants filtering none.
  • Ingestion can expose aquatic organisms and food webs to persistent plastics and associated contaminants. 2019 Prelims: (a), harmful to marine ecosystems.
