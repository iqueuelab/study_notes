# PFAS: persistence and bioaccumulation

## 2024 PYQ revision
- **Per- and polyfluoroalkyl substances (PFAS)** are a large group of synthetic chemicals used in many products/processes, valued for resistance to water/oil/heat. Carbon–fluorine bonds make many highly persistent; “forever chemicals” is shorthand for slow environmental breakdown, not literal infinite lifetime.
- PFAS can contaminate drinking water, food and food packaging; continued exposure can cause accumulation of certain PFAS in people/animals. Persistence, mobility, bioaccumulation and toxicity vary across compounds; do not attribute identical risk to every member. **Phosphoric acid is not PFAS**—the source places an unrelated chemistry PYQ after its section. [EPA explanation](https://www.epa.gov/pfas/pfas-explained).
- **PYQ 2024 answer (d) 1, 2 and 3:** widespread occurrence, not easily degraded, and persistent exposure can lead to animal bioaccumulation. Consider exposure controls, source substitution, monitoring and suitable water/waste treatment.

## Exposure and regulation
- Additional source examples: non-stick cookware, stain-resistant textiles/carpets, food packaging and firefighting foams; industrial use includes aerospace, automotive, construction and electronics. Water, food and airborne particles can transmit exposure.
- PFOS and PFOA are listed under the **Stockholm Convention**. Different PFAS have different persistence, accumulation and toxicity profiles; avoid treating every compound as equally toxic.
- The issue's “nearly 20 bacteria break down PFAS” claim is unsubstantiated here. **Binding/bioaccumulation is not chemical degradation**; do not infer a proven bacterial cure or safe disposal method. The 2024 PYQ's three statements about occurrence, persistence and bioaccumulation are all correct.
- Health associations for studied PFAS include reproductive/developmental effects, hormonal/immune disruption, raised cholesterol and some cancers; associations depend on compound and exposure, rather than every exposure causing disease. Pollution pathways can include industrial waste, firefighting foam and maternal transfer via placenta/breast milk; prevention focuses on sources and exposure monitoring.

## January 2026: replacement chemicals in blood
- An embedded research clipping concerns **156 US serum samples collected during 2003–2021**, including autoimmune-disease patients, healthy siblings and unrelated controls. It reports an **86% decline in older PFAS** alongside emerging replacements: **9Cl-PF3ONS detected in 106/156 people (67.9%)**, and **Cl-PFNPA** identified in human serum. These are sample-specific findings, not prevalence in all Americans or Indians. [Primary study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12826319/).
- The study found **no discernible association with autoimmune diagnosis** in this dataset; detection alone does not prove causation or safety. Replacement chemistry can sustain exposure even as legacy compounds decline: monitor substitutes, assess their hazards and avoid regrettable substitution.
- Existing persistence/exposure notes and **2024 PYQ (d), all three** apply. The issue's practice assertion that **all PFAS are completely banned worldwide under Stockholm** is false: listed substances and applicable restrictions/exemptions differ; PFAS are not one chemically or toxicologically identical substance.
