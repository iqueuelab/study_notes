Environment

Phytoplankton, iron deposition and ocean nutrient cycles

Editable notes

Two different ocean studies

  • North Pacific transition zone, PNAS 2 June 2025: anthropogenic atmospheric iron estimated at 39 ±9% of mixed-layer dissolved-iron sources under studied spring conditions. East Asian emissions/westerly transport can stimulate blooms and advance nitrate/phosphate drawdown; not 39% of all ocean iron everywhere.
  • Four 2016–19 expeditions, trace-metal-clean sampling, iron isotopes, gene-expression/metatranscriptomics and satellite chlorophyll observations connect deposition to biological response. HNLC means high nutrient, low chlorophyll, often reflecting iron limitation. Chlorophyll is a biomass proxy, not direct proof of permanent carbon sequestration. Original PNAS study.
  • Separate late 2019–early 2020 southeast-Madagascar bloom linked to dust from drought-stricken southern African drylands, including Namib/Kalahari/Etosha source regions; atmospheric transport/deposition supplied nutrients. Do not transfer the North Pacific anthropogenic 39% estimate to this Indian Ocean event; source's ~2,000 km² needs confirmation. Original Madagascar study.
  • Warming-related stratification and nutrient supply interact; iron addition does not alone demonstrate that it directly causes all stratification. Changes in dust are region-specific, not proof storms must always become more frequent everywhere.

Ecology and risks

  • Phytoplankton include photosynthetic diatoms, cyanobacteria and many dinoflagellates/algae; primary producers supporting phytoplankton → zooplankton → fish → predators. Copepods and foraminifera are consumers in the exam distinction, although biological symbioses can occur.
  • Marine photosynthesis contributes roughly half global annual oxygen production, not necessarily more than half the accumulated atmospheric oxygen stock. Much oxygen is consumed again by respiration/decay. Cyanobacteria are bacteria, not taxonomically true algae.
  • Blooms may enhance food supply and carbon uptake; some species produce toxins, while decomposition can consume oxygen/hypoxia. Not every bloom harmful; durable sequestration depends on export/storage, not photosynthesis alone. Natural dust fertilisation is not automatically safe intentional geoengineering.
  • 2012 PYQ: (a), statements 1/2—destroying phytoplankton damages carbon sink and food chains, not drastically seawater density. Density chiefly varies with temperature/salinity/pressure. 2021 primary producers: (b), cyanobacteria and diatoms.
