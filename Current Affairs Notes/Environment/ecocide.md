# Ecocide and environmental criminal accountability

## Proposed international crime

- **Ecocide** denotes exceptionally grave environmental destruction. A 2021 independent expert-panel proposal covers unlawful or recklessly disproportionate acts knowingly risking severe damage that is widespread or long-lasting; this is a **proposed definition**, not an enacted universal offence.
- Campaigners seek its addition as a **fifth Rome Statute crime**. The ICC presently covers **genocide, crimes against humanity, war crimes and aggression**; ecocide has not become a separate ICC crime. [ICC Rome Statute](https://www.icc-cpi.int/publications/core-legal-texts/rome-statute-international-criminal-court)
- The ICC prosecutes **natural persons**, subject to jurisdiction and admissibility rules—not states or companies as entities. Domestic law may separately provide corporate liability.
- **Belgium adopted a revised criminal code in 2024**, including ecocide. Adoption and commencement differ; its new code entered into force in **September 2026**, a later development. Belgium's legislation did not itself amend the Rome Statute. Avoid the claim that it was the first European country ever to criminalise environmental destruction. [Belgian Justice: code reform](https://www.justice.belgium.be/fr/themes/securite_et_criminalite/reforme_du_code_penal)

## India: existing protection, distinct-offence debate

| Framework | Purpose |
|---|---|
| Environment (Protection) Act, 1986 | Umbrella environmental protection |
| Wild Life (Protection) Act, 1972, amended in 2022 | Species/habitat protection and regulated wildlife trade |
| Forest conservation legislation, 1980, amended/renamed in 2023 | Regulation of diversion of covered forest land |
| Compensatory Afforestation Fund Act, 2016 | Management of compensatory-afforestation funds |

The supplied April 2025 discussion proposes a distinct ecocide offence; existing environmental violations can already attract legal consequences. A new offence must specify severity, geographical/duration thresholds, culpability and evidentiary standards.

**Case for:** ecological justice, deterrence, polluter accountability and stronger protection against major ecosystem damage. **Challenges:** causation across actors, enforcement capacity, fair development decisions and avoiding vague criminalisation. Clear thresholds, independent ecological evidence and accountable enforcement matter as much as a new label.
