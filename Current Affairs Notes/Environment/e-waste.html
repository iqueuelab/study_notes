Environment

E-waste and extended producer responsibility

Editable notes

Core facts

  • E-waste is discarded electrical/electronic equipment. Hazardous constituents include lead, mercury, cadmium, chromium and beryllium.
  • EPR shifts end-of-life responsibility towards producers; collection, authorised recycling and certificate systems help implement obligations.
  • The source reports 2024 amendments enabling EPR-certificate trading; verify the applicable category and target rather than assuming a uniform 25% recycling obligation.

January 2025 source figures

  • Generation: 1.751 million tonnes in 2023-24, reported increase 72.54%; recycled share 43%, untreated share 57%. The comparison year and statistical definitions need verification.
  • Source also cites 65 cities generating 60% and 10 states generating 70%; baseline years are unspecified.

Risks and response

  • Unsafe informal dismantling/burning contaminates air, soil and water and exposes workers. Landfilling can leach toxins; uncontrolled incineration releases hazardous emissions.
  • Priorities: take-back points, authorised recyclers, worker training and formalisation, repair/reuse/refurbishment, better recovery technology, public awareness and enforcement.
  • Basel Convention: hazardous-waste transboundary movement; Minamata Convention: mercury. Domestic links include hazardous-waste regulation and circular-economy policy.

PYQ trap

  • UPSC Prelims 2013: beryllium, cadmium, chromium, mercury and lead are the relevant listed substances; answer (b). The full question is in the common Environment PYQ file.
  • PYQ recall: EPR was an important feature of the e-Waste (Management and Handling) Rules, 2011; distinguish the historical rule asked in 2019 from later replacement rules.

May 2025: certificate pricing and reliable measurement

  • E-Waste (Management) Rules, 2022 took effect 1 April 2023; producer obligations are implemented through registered actors, recycling targets and EPR certificates on CPCB's portal. Certificate compliance must reflect actual recycling/recovery, not fictitious transactions. CPCB portal and rules.
  • 2024 amendments provide certificate exchange-price bands linked to environmental compensation: floor 30%, ceiling 100% of applicable compensation. This is not a single rupee price for every device, nor a floor-price provision that should be attributed solely to the original 2022 text. Follow applicable CPCB methodology/date; later August 2025 guidelines are not proof of the exact May price table.
  • Predictable remuneration can support collection, transport and verified recovery investments. Producers' cost/pass-through concerns require assessment against environmental/health externalities; pricing without audits/traceability can still produce paper compliance.
  • Source compares 708,445 tonnes (2017–18) with 1,778,400 (2023–24), claims 151.03% growth and 169,283 tonnes/year increase. These are source figures with unresolved classification comparability and arithmetic: the endpoint increase is about 151.0%, average over six year-to-year intervals about 178,326 tonnes/year, not the printed annual figure.
  • Reject unsupported universal claims of informal workers' lifespan below 27 years. The source's 95% informal share, <10% formal versus 70%+ EU, ₹80,000 crore / US$10bn / US$20bn / US$20bn loss estimates lack harmonised years/definitions and cannot be added or treated as established national totals.
  • Unsafe acid extraction/burning exposes workers, including women/children, to toxic substances and contaminates soil/water/air. Recover gold/copper and other materials through authorised technology; encourage durable/modular products, repair/refurbishment, state-level material tracking and recovery-based incentives.
  • Integrate informal collectors through training, safe jobs and fair payment rather than abrupt livelihood exclusion; strengthen recycler verification, take-back access, public awareness and health protections.

June 2025: silver recovery using fatty acids

  • Research by Helsinki/Jyväskylä scientists explores oleic, linoleic and linolenic acids plus aqueous hydrogen peroxide for dissolving/recovering silver under mild conditions. This is a controlled chemical process, not simply adding cooking oil to scrap. Original research.
  • Silver's electrical conductivity supports electronics, photovoltaic contacts and vehicle components; photovoltaics' semiconductor absorbs light, while silver contacts carry current.
  • Urban mining recovers secondary materials from discarded products; it differs from extracting virgin urban mineral deposits. Safer solvents may reduce burdens of cyanide/strong-acid routes, but scale, selectivity, reagent recovery, cost and worker protection need assessment.
  • June source's per-device silver quantities and recovery percentage are indicative estimates with unspecified product/year coverage; they are not a universal inventory or proof that all unrecovered silver is permanently lost.
