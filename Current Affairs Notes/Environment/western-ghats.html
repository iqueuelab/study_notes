Environment

Western Ghats: ecology, indigenous knowledge and cultural memory

Editable notes

Geography and ecology

  • Sahyadri / Western Ghats: Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu; important endemic biodiversity, river headwaters and slope ecosystems.
  • Lion-tailed macaque, Nilgiri tahr and Malabar civet exemplify conservation concerns. Source 7,402 species is an unspecified inventory, not a complete current total.
  • UNESCO serial World Heritage property inscribed 2012 comprises selected sites, not every square kilometre of the range.

Communities and literary memory

  • Kurumba, Toda, Kattunayakan, Irula, Malasar: diverse communities; customary livelihoods include forest produce, pastoralism and crafts. Avoid romanticising all communities as identical or unchanging.

Governance

  • Plantations, mining, roads, dams and unmanaged tourism can combine with intense rain to increase fragmentation, landslide/flood risk and displacement.
  • FRA 2006, meaningful Gram Sabha involvement, land-use alternatives, cumulative impact assessment, native habitat restoration and community-benefiting tourism link ecological resilience with cultural rights.

Geography and committee recall

  • Sahyadri/Western Ghats traverse Gujarat, Maharashtra, Goa, Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu, broadly from the Tapi region towards Kanyakumari; do not extend the main range to Satpura indiscriminately.
  • Western Ghats–Sri Lanka is one global biodiversity hotspot. UNESCO 2012 listing comprises 39 serial sites, not blanket designation of the entire range. Wayanad is a wildlife sanctuary, not a combined “Wayanad–Mudumalai National Park”.
  • Gadgil/WGEEP (2011) favoured graded ecological sensitivity across the Ghats with community participation; Kasturirangan (2013) distinguished natural/cultural landscapes and identified roughly 37% as ecologically sensitive. Recommendations, draft ESA notifications and final operative restrictions are different.
  • Eastward basin anchors: Godavari, Krishna, Kaveri; Tunga/Bhadra are tributaries. Westward: Periyar, Bharathapuzha, Netravati, Sharavathi, Mandovi.
  • Passes: Thal/Kasara, Bhor, Palakkad, Amba, Amboli. Shola–grassland mosaics support endemic biodiversity and catchments.
  • Mining, forest conversion, plantations and habitat fragmentation affect hydrology/conflict; governance must account for indigenous livelihoods, consultation and site-specific risk. Geological age is not the age of individual trees.

October 2025: IUCN World Heritage Outlook 4

  • Source reports significant concern for Western Ghats, Manas and Sundarbans; Khangchendzonga good; Great Himalayan, Kaziranga, Keoladeo and Nanda Devi/Valley of Flowers good with some concerns. Conservation outlook is an assessment, not UNESCO delisting or a species Red List category.
  • 2025 report assesses 271 natural/mixed properties. The 228 sites are the comparable cohort first assessed in 2014: their positive outlook fell from 63% in 2014/2017 and 62% in 2020 to 57% in 2025. Among all 271 sites, about 61% had a positive outlook. Do not apply 57% to all sites or call 228 the 2025 total. IUCN report.
  • Ghats pressures: dams/hydropower and river alteration, roads/habitat fragmentation, plantation conversion, tourism waste, invasive wattle/acacia/eucalyptus and warming effects on montane species. Proposed Sillahalla pumped storage illustrates cumulative ecological appraisal; a project proposal is not proof all works are built.
  • Sundarbans: sea-level/salinity changes, cyclones, pollution and resource extraction can damage mangroves, fisheries and coastal protection. Site-specific management, native habitat restoration, tourism/waste controls, community participation and cumulative impact review support the Kunming–Montreal biodiversity framework aims.
  • The report began in 2014, with editions 2017, 2020 and 2025; it was not a perfectly uninterrupted three-year schedule. Existing notes explain the Ghats' six-state geography and selected serial-property scope.
