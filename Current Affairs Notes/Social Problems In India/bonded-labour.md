# Bonded labour, forced labour and rehabilitation

## Labour Day 2025: debt and coercion
- **Bonded labour** ties work/service to debt or customary obligation with constrained freedom; debt can be inherited or manipulated. **Forced labour** is the wider category of work extracted involuntarily under menace of penalty; it need not involve debt. Debt bondage is a form of forced labour, not a mutually exclusive category.
- **Article 23** prohibits trafficking, begar and similar forced labour; **Article 21** protects dignity. DPSPs **39, 42 and 43** support protection from exploitation, humane work and living wages. The **Bonded Labour System (Abolition) Act, 1976** abolishes the system and extinguishes bonded debts—not a duty to work until the debt is “repaid”. [Government legal framework](https://www.pib.gov.in/newsite/PrintRelease.aspx?lang=2&reg=48&relid=124739).
- The article recounts Mukesh Adivasi's trafficking from Madhya Pradesh to Karnataka sugarcane work after a **₹500 advance**, violence and 14–16-hour days; K. Thenmozhi's child brick-kiln labour after a **₹2,000 advance**, lost schooling and confinement. These are reported cases, not national prevalence estimates.
- Distress expenses, food insecurity, loss of earnings and social obligations interact with caste marginalisation, illiteracy, restricted credit, labour-market domination, migration/climate displacement and weak enforcement. Women, children, Dalits, Adivasis and migrants can face layered vulnerabilities.
- The issue recalls the government's **2016 ambition to rehabilitate 1.84 crore people by 2030**, against **12,760 reported rescued/rehabilitated in 2016–21**. Its target arithmetic is approximate, and these figures must not be read as a verified current prevalence count. The revamped rehabilitation scheme took effect **17 May 2016**. [PIB](https://www.pib.gov.in/PressReleasePage.aspx?PRID=1514985&lang=2&reg=48).
- The issue's **47-crore workforce/39-crore unorganised workers** figures are older source-cited estimates without a stated survey year. Informal employment and unorganised-sector employment differ; do not describe every informal worker as bonded. The ILO/IHD Employment Report 2024 supports concern about poor job quality, not that equivalence.
- District/subdivision vigilance committees, worker identification, rescue, prosecution, timely compensation and sustainable rehabilitation must work together. Pair legal literacy, collective bargaining, portable migrant benefits, affordable credit, minimum wages and safe work with monitoring of brick kilns, agriculture, construction and textiles.
- Labour Codes enacted in **2019–20** had not generally commenced as of **1 May 2025**; the editorial's criticisms of retrenchment/collective bargaining provisions are policy assessment, not evidence that all proposed changes were already operative.
