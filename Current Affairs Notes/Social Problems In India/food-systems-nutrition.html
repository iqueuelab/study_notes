Social Problems In India

Food systems: nutrition, access and sustainability

Editable notes

Triple burden and indicator caution

  • Food security includes nutritious affordable diets, not cereal output alone: undernutrition, micronutrient deficiencies and overweight/obesity can coexist. Climate stress, biodiversity loss, low incomes and poor storage compound inequity/NCD risks.
  • NFHS-5 (2019–21), under 5: stunting 35.5% (low height-for-age), wasting 19.3% (low weight-for-height), underweight 32.1% (low weight-for-age). Source lists adult overweight/obesity women 24%, men 22.9%, and anaemia 57% women 15–49. These are distinct indicators/cohorts, not proportions all suffering the same condition. Official NFHS nutrition figures.
  • Source's 194 million undernourished is not an NFHS national calorie-undernourishment count; it needs a separate FAO estimate/year. GHI 2024: 105/127 is a composite rank, not a direct census of hunger or every nutritional condition.
  • Article cites 55.6% unable to afford healthy diet in 2022 at PPP US$3.36/day; dollar-PPP diet cost is not a nominal exchange-rate rupee price, and affordability estimates depend on methodology/reference year. Global US$12 trillion/year food-system costs are an estimated hidden-cost concept, not India's fiscal spending or an audited invoice.

Transformation priorities

  • Nutrition-sensitive agriculture: diverse/biofortified crops, nutrition goals in farm policy, better storage/processing and locally suitable foods; supplementation/fortification complements dietary diversity rather than replaces it.
  • Nutrition-Sensitive Community Planning: connect food with WASH, soil/water conservation and healthcare. Source's Nutri-Pathshala model combines nutrition education, biofortified grains and local sourcing to support small farmers; programme examples are not proof every implementation succeeded.
  • Diversify PDS and PM-POSHAN (mid-day meals) appropriately; strengthen safety nets and healthy-diet behaviour campaigns. Private sector: understandable labels, responsibly fortified foods, suitable plant alternatives and digital/QR education; innovation needs nutrition/equity safeguards.
  • Climate-smart, gender-inclusive farming and rural opportunities; nutrition literacy via interactive tools (source example MyPlate Blast Off) and radio for remote areas.
  • Source's TERI–FOLU Himalayan model: region-specific micronutrient-rich crops, local food networks and decentralised processing; assess measured outcomes rather than asserting universal success. Integrate nutrition/health expertise with planning; links SDG 2, 3, 12. FAO dietary guidance.

Gender justice and nutrition: May 2025

  • NFHS-5, 2019–21: anaemia affects 57% of women 15–49, versus about 25% of men 15–49; roughly 18.7% of women have low BMI. Women's anaemia rose from 53% in NFHS-4 (2015–16). These are different indicators; anaemia is not identical to calorie undernutrition and can have multiple causes. Official health statistics.
  • Unequal intra-household food allocation, women eating last, unpaid care, weak income control, poverty and low bargaining power can limit adequate diets despite food availability. Employment alone does not ensure safe work, fair wages or control over earnings.
  • POSHAN Abhiyaan (2018) sought phased reductions in stunting/undernutrition and anaemia, not literal elimination of every form of malnutrition by 2022. Saksham Anganwadi/POSHAN 2.0 integrates relevant services; before/after survey comparisons alone cannot isolate programme causality. Official objectives.
  • Source 69% fund utilisation in 2022–23, 49% lacking control over own income and 53% self-employed earnings gap lack complete denominator/accounting/method details. Do not present them as universal statistics for every Indian woman; distinguish access to personal money from sole/joint decisions over cash earnings.
  • PLFS female LFPR, usual status, age 15+: 23.3% in 2017–18 →32.8% in 2021–22. Rounded source 23→33 is consistent, but LFPR includes those seeking/available for work and is not the employment rate; unpaid family work/informal employment requires job-quality analysis. Official PLFS series.
  • Proposals: link nutrition with NRLM, MGNREGS, SHGs, skills, credit/financial literacy and maternal healthcare; make Anganwadis accessible hubs for nutrition, literacy and livelihood referrals without overburdening workers.
  • Measure dietary/health outcomes alongside income control, job security/pay equity and service use; coordinate health, WCD and rural-livelihood departments. Jan Andolan awareness complements actual food/health access; neither awareness nor larger allocation alone guarantees consumption/outcomes.
  • Mains: nutrition problem → gendered resource/power structures → programme gaps → accountable services plus social/economic empowerment. The source's 250-word question is practice, not a PYQ.
