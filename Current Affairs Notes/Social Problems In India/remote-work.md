# Remote work: flexibility, care and workplace organisation

## Global Survey of Working Arrangements, 2024–25
- Survey covered **16,422 full-time college-educated workers in 40 countries**, November 2024–February 2025; it is not representative of every worker or manual occupation. Average actual WFH **1.27 days/week**, compared with **1.6 in 2022 and 1.33 in 2023**; researchers describe broad stabilisation since 2023 rather than continuing global collapse. [Stanford research summary](https://siepr.stanford.edu/publications/essay/working-home-2025-five-key-facts).
- Source reports desired WFH about **2.6 days/week**; women with children desired **2.66**, versus **2.53** without children. Desire and actual availability differ; family/care needs shape preferences, while fathers and childless men also value flexibility. Do not infer all mothers prefer home working for one reason.
- North American/European adoption generally exceeds Asia; office-presence norms, role suitability, broadband, crowded housing and management practices can influence access. The PDF's regional averages are illustrative, not uniform rates for every named country.
## Mains: make flexibility inclusive
- Benefits: less commuting, autonomy, wider talent access and potential work–care compatibility. Risks: unequal unpaid-care burdens, isolation/digital fatigue, poor ergonomics, blurred hours and collaboration/supervision problems; outcomes depend on job and design.
- Use planned hybrid schedules, output-based assessment, good collaboration tools, ergonomic support, breaks and boundaries; improve broadband and care infrastructure. Hybrid working is a policy option, not automatically best for every occupation. Share domestic responsibilities so flexibility does not entrench gender inequality.
