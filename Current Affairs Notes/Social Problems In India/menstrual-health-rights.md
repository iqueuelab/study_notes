# Menstrual health: dignity, equality and school education

## Menstrual health, dignity and education
- **Dr Jaya Thakur v. Government of India, 30 January 2026**, Justices **J.B. Pardiwala and R. Mahadevan**: menstrual health falls within **Article 21**; lack of facilities undermines dignity/privacy and equal school participation (**Article 14**), and obstructs education (**Article 21A/RTE Act**).
- Period poverty includes unaffordable products and inadequate water, sanitation, privacy and disposal. Facilities and stigma-free teaching are enabling conditions for substantive equality; the State's duty goes beyond isolated pad-distribution projects.
- Schools must provide functional gender-separated toilets with water, accessible/private facilities, soap/handwashing, free sanitary napkins, menstrual-hygiene corners and safe sanitary-waste disposal. The judgment also requires curricula/teacher sensitisation, inspections with anonymous student feedback, child-rights-commission oversight and compliance reporting; **three months** was the compliance period, not proof implementation was already complete.
- Government schools' non-compliance attracts State accountability; private-school recognition consequences require the **RTE procedure**, rather than automatic closure without process. [Judgment, conclusions/directions paras 172–179](https://indiankanoon.org/doc/101264378/).
- The issue's **NFHS-5: 77.3% of women aged 15–24 using hygienic menstrual methods** is a survey indicator, not a direct count of all women without product access; the complement is **22.7%**, rather than exactly 25%. Address affordability, rural/urban inequalities, disability access, taboo and sustained maintenance together.

## 13 March 2026: menstrual-leave petition
- The Supreme Court declined to impose a nationwide menstrual-leave policy through the petition and left scope for government consultation/policy consideration. **CJI Surya Kant and Justice Joymalya Bagchi**, not the source's stale Chandrachud/Pardiwala pairing, discussed risks of hiring exclusion and career stereotyping from a compulsory mandate.
- Oral concern about unintended discrimination is not a ban on menstrual leave or a finding that all accommodation harms women. The policy debate balances health/dignity, substantive equality, retention and evidence about employer behaviour; the earlier school-hygiene judgment and **Article 21** protections remain distinct from a uniform workplace-leave entitlement.
- **Statutory mandate versus voluntary employer/institution policy** differ in coverage/enforceability. Odisha public-service, Kerala educational and Karnataka workplace arrangements have different beneficiaries/conditions; do not label all State rules merely voluntary or apply a student's annual attendance concession to all employees. Source's blanket “up to 60 days annually” is not a nationwide entitlement.
- **CEDAW**: UN General Assembly adopted **1979**; India ratified **1993**, with declarations/reservation. It promotes elimination of discrimination, not one internationally uniform number of paid menstrual-leave days. Japan, South Korea, Indonesia, Taiwan and Zambia have differing legal approaches; UK employer policies are not a general UK statutory period-leave right. [UN treaty record](https://treaties.un.org/Pages/ViewDetails.aspx?src=TREATY&mtdsg_no=IV-8&chapter=4).
- Practical options: privacy, flexible work/leave, adequate toilets/products, access to treatment and anti-discrimination safeguards. Involuntary disclosure, inaccessible informal-sector leave and loss of wages require attention alongside formal entitlement.
