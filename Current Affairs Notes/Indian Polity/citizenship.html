Indian Polity

Citizenship, visas and deportation

Editable notes

Constitutional and statutory distinction

  • Part II, Articles 5-11 addresses citizenship at commencement and Parliament's power to legislate; it does not itself prescribe all present-day acquisition procedures.
  • Citizenship Act, 1955 governs birth, descent, registration, naturalisation and incorporation of territory; loss through renunciation, termination or deprivation.
  • Citizenship is a Union subject; CAA 2019 provides a special route for specified Hindu, Sikh, Buddhist, Jain, Parsi and Christian migrants from Afghanistan, Bangladesh and Pakistan entering by 31 December 2014, subject to statutory conditions/exemptions. It is not automatic citizenship for every such entrant.

MHA annual report 2023-24: source figures

  • FRRO deportations 2, 331: Nigeria 1, 470; Bangladesh 411; Uganda 78; reasons include visa violations/overstay.
  • 1, 112 long-term visas to Pakistani minority-community members; LTV is not citizenship.
  • Citizenship certificates 1, 699: registration 1, 278 + naturalisation 421; this source table excludes CAA figures.
  • Foreign visitors 98 lakh: Bangladesh 21.1 lakh, US 17.4 lakh, UK 9.9 lakh.
  • Source lists Amendment Act years 1986, 1992, 2003, 2005, 2015, 2019; avoid turning this source list into an assertion that no other changes exist.

NRI, PIO and OCI: diaspora distinction

TermCitizenship / meaning
NRIIndian citizen residing abroad; residence definitions depend on the law/context
PIOPerson of Indian origin; historical/demographic description can continue
OCI cardholderEligible foreign national, registered under Citizenship Act, 1955 section 7A; not an Indian citizen
  • PIO card scheme merged into OCI in 2015; the demographic category 'person of Indian origin' was not abolished.
  • Eligibility connects to qualifying Indian citizenship/territory and descendants, subject to statutory conditions; Pakistan/Bangladesh citizenship/ancestry exclusions apply as prescribed. Do not infer eligibility from Indian ethnicity alone.
  • OCI is not dual citizenship. No Indian voting rights, election eligibility or ordinary entitlement to constitutional/public posts; visa/residence privileges and permitted activities remain subject to rules. MHA explanation, OCI FAQ.

US birthright citizenship: January 2025 clipping

  • 20 January executive order sought to restrict recognition for specified US-born children of non-citizen parents; State litigation immediately followed, invoking 14th Amendment protection.
  • An executive announcement is not proof constitutional birthright citizenship was finally abolished. Birth citizenship ≠ H-1B employment permission ≠ green-card status; Indian citizenship rules must be assessed separately.
  • EO targeted specified children whose mother was unlawfully present or lawfully temporary and father neither US citizen nor lawful permanent resident, prospectively after a 30-day period; parental tests are more specific than “all foreign parents”. 20 January order.

US deportations: February 2025

  • 5 February 2025: US military flight brought 104 Indians to Amritsar, correcting the PDF's preliminary “around 200”. Nationality verification and acceptance of own citizens differ from endorsing detention/transport conditions. Contemporaneous report.
  • Source 18, 000 refers to a reported removal/enforcement population; not a census of all undocumented Indians in the US. Legal students/workers are not automatically undocumented migrants.
  • Balance sovereign immigration enforcement with due process, dignity, humane treatment, consular assistance and protection against trafficking; reintegration needs counselling, skills/jobs and action against fraudulent agents.
  • Trump, not Biden, was US President for this February 2025 sequence; Modi's impending visit was a diplomatic context, not proof of a settled deportation agreement.

Irregular migration and rights

  • Irregular entry/overstay, refugee protection, smuggling and trafficking are different categories; trafficking centres on exploitation and need not involve crossing a border.
  • Article 21 protects persons, including non-citizens; immigration enforcement must respect applicable law and due process. CAA exceptions have specified conditions, not a general exemption for all migrants.
  • Drivers include work, persecution, disasters and family networks. Recruitment regulation/eMigrate, safe-migration training, consular support and border management need coordination.
  • Migration agreements do not eliminate domestic unemployment or guarantee every applicant an overseas job; source agent counts are dated.

Immigration and Foreigners Bill: March 2025

  • Introduced on 11 March 2025; introduction is distinct from passage, assent and commencement.
  • Proposed consolidation of Passport (Entry into India) Act 1920; Registration of Foreigners Act 1939; Foreigners Act 1946; Immigration (Carriers' Liability) Act 2000. It did not repeal the Citizenship Act 1955 or Passports Act 1967. Official account.
  • Covers entry, exit, stay, travel documents, carrier information and reporting by institutions accommodating foreigners. Registration depends on visa/category conditions, not a universal rule for every visitor.
  • Proposed undocumented-entry penalty: up to five years and ₹5 lakh; forged documents: two to seven years and ₹1–10 lakh. These are Bill provisions in this dated update.
  • Articles 14 and 21 protect persons; Article 19 rights are for citizens. National security and public health controls must coexist with due process, proportionality and constitutional judicial review.
  • Assess compliance costs, privacy, clear procedures and business/student mobility alongside security objectives.

May 2025: detention and the rule of law

  • Assam's final 2019 NRC excluded roughly 19 lakh people; the PDF wrongly equates exclusion with citizenship being stripped. Non-inclusion alone does not declare someone a foreigner; tribunal/legal process is distinct. MHA clarification.
  • Assam citizenship verification involved the 24 March 1971 midnight cutoff and proof/ancestry questions; floods, missing records and spelling differences can impede fair adjudication. Avoid treating every excluded person as detained or stateless.
  • Source cites Foreigners Act 1946 detention/deportation powers and NSA 1980 preventive detention. These are different legal grounds, not interchangeable authority to hold all non-citizens indefinitely. Assent to the Immigration and Foreigners Act 2025 did not alone commence its replacement framework.
  • Article 21 protects persons. Article 22's arrest/preventive-detention provisions have exceptions and safeguards; all clauses do not apply identically to every detention category. Courts can review legality, purpose, fairness and proportionality.

Editorial critique and comparative judgment

  • Source invokes Rajubala Das v Union of India, identified with a 2020 proceeding; do not treat that year as a verified final judgment or assume all requested relief was granted.
  • NZYQ (Australia, 2023): continued immigration detention lacked constitutional justification where no real prospect of removal becoming practicable in the reasonably foreseeable future existed. This concerns Australia's constitutional separation of powers, not an automatically binding Indian rule. High Court summary.
  • Source's 26 deported (2017–2023)/over 1.5 lakh declared foreigners is an editorial dataset claim with unclear matching cohorts; it does not establish the status of every detainee. Feasibility of receiving-country acceptance and nationality verification matters.
  • Policy argument: detention needs a lawful purpose, individual assessment, periodic review, fair hearings/legal aid and humane conditions; evaluate alternatives when removal is unrealistic. The editorial's blanket unlawfulness claim should not replace case-specific legal findings.

8 May 2025 hearing: Rohingya and UNHCR cards

  • Embedded clipping reports Supreme Court oral observations that persons determined to be foreigners could be dealt with under applicable law, including deportation following prescribed procedure. Petitioners invoked non-refoulement, feared persecution in Myanmar and alleged removal of persons holding UNHCR cards; these are litigating claims, not facts conclusively adjudicated by the clipping.
  • Articles 14 and 21 protect persons; citizen-specific Article 19(1)(e) residence/settlement rights differ. A UNHCR card does not itself confer Indian citizenship or an unconditional statutory right to remain.
  • The Court scheduled fuller hearing for July. Distinguish oral/interim observations, existing executive powers under the then-applicable Foreigners Act, and a final determination of the pending refugee-protection claims; India's non-membership of the Refugee Convention does not answer every constitutional/procedural question.
