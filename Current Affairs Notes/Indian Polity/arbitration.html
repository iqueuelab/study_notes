Indian Polity

Arbitration capacity and professional arbitrators

Editable notes

April 2025 editorial: human capacity

  • India's commercial-dispute growth, court backlog and arbitration-hub ambition require skilled tribunals alongside legislative reform. Arbitration depends on party agreement and specialist decision-making; judicial experience alone need not provide arbitral case-management skills.
  • Editorial concerns: overreliance on retired judges, courtroom-style lengthy procedures, cost escalation, insufficient specialised training and poorly reasoned awards; weak global empanelment can constrain institutional credibility. These are critiques, not proof that every retired judge or Indian award is deficient.
  • Core skills: procedure tailored to the dispute, efficient hearings, technical comprehension, cross-cultural communication, tribunal management and clear award drafting. A dissatisfied party cannot automatically have an award set aside: statutory review grounds differ from a merits appeal.

Proposed reforms and relevance

  • Training/accreditation (e.g. CIArb standards), professional specialisation and experienced finance/engineering/technology practitioners alongside lawyers; transparent appointment by relevant expertise, not seniority alone; stronger party autonomy and institutional rather than reflexively ad hoc arbitration.
  • Encourage global forums/empanelment in ICC, LCIA, SIAC and strengthen MCIA and Delhi International Arbitration Centre; support specialised training and research through institutional/public/private funding.
  • Potential gains: quicker credible dispute resolution, less court burden and investor confidence; accreditation/mandatory certification are editorial proposals, not newly enacted universal requirements. The source's Ease of Doing Business ranking language is obsolete: World Bank discontinued that ranking, so reforms cannot promise an improvement in a currently published ranking.

October 2025: wider alternative dispute resolution

  • ADR includes arbitration (binding award), conciliation/mediation (facilitated settlement), negotiation and Lok Adalats. A mediator does not impose a judgment; enforceability depends on the applicable settlement framework. Ordinary Lok Adalats seek compromise, while Permanent Lok Adalats have distinct statutory powers.
  • Article 39A, Section 89 CPC, Arbitration and Conciliation Act 1996 and Legal Services Authorities Act 1987 are key anchors. Article 51(c) concerns respect for international law/treaty obligations; Article 51(d) specifically encourages settlement of international disputes by arbitration.
  • Reject the source's universal 180-day ADR/arbitration cap: section 29A ordinarily provides twelve months from completion of pleadings for domestic awards, with statutory extensions; section 29B is the agreed fast-track six-month route. The Arbitration Council of India is the correct statutory name, not “Indian Arbitration Council”. Do not infer a functioning body or fresh 2021 establishment merely from statutory provision.
  • Mediation Act 2023 enactment does not mean every provision immediately commenced; do not present blanket mandatory civil pre-litigation mediation or a universal two-session exit rule as operative. Commercial Courts Act pre-institution mediation has its own scope and urgent-interim-relief exception.
  • Source NJDG/India Justice Report figures (about 4.57–4.58 crore total, 81,768 SC, 62.9 lakh HC; vacancies 33% HC/21% district) mix snapshots and need aligned dates/denominators. Use as source-era context, not live totals. Its first Lok Adalat “Gujarat 1999” claim is incorrect; see corrected historical record below.
  • Benefits: access, cost/time savings and preserved relationships. Limits: awareness, trained neutrals, rural reach, digital exclusion and enforcement. Expand professional training, legal aid, ODR/e-Lok Adalat and outcome monitoring without promising every dispute is suitable for consensual settlement.
  • Official Arbitration Act, sections 29A–29B. Gujarat held the first Lok Adalat at Una, Junagadh, on 14 March 1982, according to the judiciary-hosted NALSA report; replace the source 1999 date. NALSA report.
