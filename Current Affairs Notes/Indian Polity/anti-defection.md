# Anti-defection law and timely adjudication

## Tenth Schedule
- **52nd Amendment, 1985** inserted the Tenth Schedule to curb political defections.
- Party member: voluntarily giving up membership, or voting/abstaining against an authorised direction without prior permission, unless condoned within **15 days**. Giving up membership can be inferred from conduct; formal resignation is unnecessary.
- Independent elected member: disqualified on joining a party. Nominated member: joining a party **after six months from taking the seat** attracts disqualification.
- **Merger exemption requires at least two-thirds of the legislature party**, subject to the Schedule's merger conditions. It is not permission for any two-thirds group to defect arbitrarily.
- **91st Amendment, 2003 removed the one-third split exemption**; it did not replace a one-third merger threshold with two-thirds. It also limits ministry size and restricts minister/remunerative political appointments of disqualified defectors.
## Decision and reform
- Speaker/Chairman decides; *Kihoto Hollohan* (1992) permits judicial review. The Schedule contains no express decision deadline.
- *Keisham Meghachandra* (2020): ordinarily decide within three months, absent exceptional circumstances; an independent tribunal was suggested, not automatically established.
- Stability and mandate protection versus legislators' deliberative freedom; partisan delay can change majorities. Consider transparent deadlines, fair hearings, narrower whips on government-survival matters and internal party democracy.
