Indian Polity

Governor: constitutional powers and State relations

Editable notes

Constitutional position

  • Article 153: Governor for each State; the same person may serve multiple States. Article 155: appointed by the President. Constitutional head of the State and a link with the Union; the office must uphold constitutional government rather than act as a party representative.
  • Article 156: ordinarily five years, subject to the President’s pleasure; continues until successor assumes office. Pleasure is not a licence for arbitrary removal.
  • Articles 157–158: Indian citizen, at least 35 years; cannot remain a legislator or hold another office of profit; entitled to official residence and prescribed emoluments.
  • Article 161: clemency for offences against laws within State executive power. Article 163: Council of Ministers aids/advises, except constitutionally recognised discretion. Article 164: appoints Chief Minister and other ministers on CM’s advice.
  • Article 200: assent, withholding/return for reconsideration where permitted, or reservation for President; mandatory reservation applies where a Bill would derogate from High Court powers so as to endanger its constitutional position. A Money Bill cannot be returned under the first proviso.

Rejecting a pocket veto, it prescribed timelines; the source’s generic statement that Bills can be withheld indefinitely misses this day’s central development.

Later legal clarification: 20 November 2025

  • In its Article 143 Presidential Reference opinion, the Constitution Bench rejected courts imposing universal timelines or substituting constitutional assent through “deemed assent”. Prolonged unexplained inaction can still invite limited judicial intervention requiring action. Keep this later clarification separate from the April news. Supreme Court opinion.

Friction and reform

  • Disputes: inviting a party to form government, timing floor tests, delaying Bills, partisan public comments and perceived dependence on the Union for appointment/removal.
  • Constitution-review and commission debates stress impartial appointments, consultation with the CM, secure tenure and restraint. Punchhi Commission (2010) proposed a State-legislature impeachment mechanism; it is a recommendation, not an enacted constitutional removal process. National Constitution Review Commission’s final report was 2002, despite the source’s 2001 reference.
  • Mains link: parliamentary responsibility, federalism and separation of powers require constitutional offices to facilitate elected government while protecting legitimate constitutional safeguards.

Constitutional governance: 19 April editorial

  • The April Tamil Nadu ruling illustrates elected legislature → responsible ministry → constitutional head, with judicial review addressing unconstitutional delay. Articles 74 and 163 provide ministerial aid and advice; constitutionally recognised discretion has limits.
  • Article 201 governs Presidential consideration of reserved State Bills; Article 142 enables complete justice; Article 145(3) requires at least five judges for a substantial constitutional interpretation question. The editorial's larger-bench concern is a constitutional debate, not a finding that the April judgment was void.
  • Reform proposals: clearer assent procedures, impartial gubernatorial conduct and timely reasoned decisions. Balance federal autonomy and democratic accountability with separation of powers.
  • The April ruling's one-/three-month periods were maximum limits for different situations, not a universal minimum-one-month waiting period. Its subsequent legal position is recorded separately in this topic. 8 April judgment.

24 April: judicial activism and constitutional democracy

  • The editorial rejects the label “judicial despotism”; this is an argument, not a judicial finding. Activism uses interpretation/remedies to protect rights or address governance gaps; overreach takes over policy choices beyond constitutional authority.
  • Articles 13, 32 and 226 underpin rights protection/judicial review; judicial review is part of the basic structure. Article 142 empowers the Supreme Court to do complete justice in pending causes, subject to constitutional limits; it is not a general power to legislate.
  • The Tamil Nadu assent judgment illustrates the tension between preventing constitutional paralysis and respecting institutional boundaries; its April directions and later clarification are recorded above.
  • Examples of independence cited: electoral-bonds invalidation, NJAC invalidation and reversal of President’s Rule in Arunachal Pradesh. Judicial approval in other cases and criticism of bail/civil-liberties handling do not establish a single uniform institutional stance.
  • Correction: the source groups CAA with measures upheld by the Supreme Court; it supplies no final CAA merits judgment establishing that proposition. Do not confuse pending challenges or interim decisions with final constitutional validation.
  • Constitutional democracy protects rights, minorities and federalism alongside electoral mandates. Fair criticism, reasoned judgments, judicial restraint and visible impartiality support public trust; unelected judges’ role requires constitutional justification and accountability.

4 May 2025: West Bengal report

  • The clipping reports Governor C. V. Ananda Bose’s recommendations after April Murshidabad communal violence: inquiry under the Commissions of Inquiry Act 1952, stronger Union intervention where State machinery fails, and BSF outposts in vulnerable areas. These are proposals, not proof they were enacted.
  • He mentioned Article 356 without presenting a formal recommendation for President’s Rule. A Governor’s report does not itself impose President’s Rule: constitutional conditions, Presidential action, parliamentary approval and judicial review apply. Ordinary law-and-order difficulty alone does not automatically establish constitutional breakdown.
  • Source reports his 18–19 April visits and public demands including a permanent BSF presence; distinguish citizens’ requests from operational deployment decisions.

Presidential Reference: May 2025 snapshot

  • 13 May 2025: President Droupadi Murmu referred 14 questions under Article 143(1) concerning Governor/President assent under Articles 200–201, discretion, judicial timelines/review, Article 142 remedies, bench composition and Centre–State jurisdiction. The April ruling and separately recorded 20 November 2025 opinion must remain distinct chronological stages.
  • Article 143(1): President may refer a question of law/fact which has arisen or is likely to arise and is sufficiently publicly important; Supreme Court may report an advisory opinion after hearing. Article 143(2) concerns disputes of the kind excluded by the proviso to Article 131, including specified pre-Constitution agreements; its text uses shall for the Court's report. Do not transfer the discretionary subsection-(1) wording indiscriminately to subsection (2).
  • Advisory reference is not an ordinary appeal/review petition or automatic stay of an existing judgment. Opinions carry substantial constitutional authority; “non-binding” should not be read as permission to disregard authoritative constitutional interpretation.
  • Source's questions 12–14 concern Article 145(3) Constitution Bench requirements, limits of Article 142, and Article 131 original jurisdiction. Not every case mentioning the Constitution automatically requires a larger Bench; the test concerns substantial constitutional interpretation.
  • The April three-month Presidential period was a judicial direction, not text newly inserted into Article 201. Judicial review of inaction, directions to constitutional institutions and Article 361 personal immunity need separate analysis; “mandamus against the President personally” overstates the position.
  • Examples: Berubari reference (1960) on territorial cession; Keshav Singh privilege reference (1964), correcting PDF 1965; Special Courts Bill (1978). Court declined to answer the Ayodhya historical reference (1993 reference, 1994 opinion) and returned the 1982 J&K resettlement reference in 2001 without an opinion. The source's simple “sub judice” explanations are not exhaustive accounts of these decisions.
  • Mains: prevent legislative paralysis while respecting ministerial responsibility, separation of powers, federalism and proper judicial procedure; potential reforms/larger-Bench proceedings were possibilities at the issue date, not completed outcomes. Primary Court record containing the reference and later analysis.

Advisory jurisdiction: additional reference examples

  • Reference proceeds on ministerial advice; Article 145(3) requires at least five judges for Article 143 reference hearings. The (1)/(2) wording and May 2025 reference are explained above.
  • Additional examples: Delhi Laws 1951 (delegated legislation); Kerala Education Bill 1958 (rights/education); Presidential election 1974; Third Judges 1998 (appointments).
  • Cauvery reference/opinion 1991, not a blanket 1992 permission to appeal prior judgments. A reference is not an ordinary appellate/review route.
  • Canada permits statutory references; US federal courts ordinarily reject advisory opinions because of case-or-controversy requirements. Advisory jurisdiction is not unique worldwide to India. Court’s reference analysis.

Comparative reference jurisdiction: practice clarification

  • Canada’s federal reference power comes from section 53 of the Supreme Court Act, exercised by the Governor in Council; provincial references use their applicable provincial frameworks. Do not label it a directly equivalent constitutional Article or say every provincial government sends references directly to the Supreme Court. Canadian Act.
  • The May 25 practice sheet’s absolute “no precedential value” claim for Indian advisory opinions is misleading: Article 143 opinions have substantial constitutional authority, as the earlier discussion explains.

August assent debate: constitutional restraint

  • Embedded clippings discuss whether courts should remain passive while Governors block Bills; the issue reprints the April Tamil Nadu judgment, not a fresh August enactment of assent deadlines. Existing notes distinguish April directions, the pending Presidential Reference and the later separately dated opinion. Constitutional text contains no numerical deadline, but this does not grant an unlimited pocket veto.
  • Reform proposals—CM consultation, neutral appointees, protection against arbitrary removal, clearer discretion and State-legislature impeachment—remain proposals. NCRWC final report: 2002, not the source's 2001 shorthand. Practice 2 and 4 only (C): reservation and multiple-State appointments are permitted; five years is subject to pleasure, and aid/advice has constitutional exceptions.
  • 2015 custodian PYQ: Supreme Court (d); High Courts also exercise constitutional judicial review under Article 226. Do not infer that constitutional guardianship excludes every other institution's duty to uphold the Constitution.
  • The SSC CHSL 2018 pleasure question is outside the CSE bank: AG and Governors hold office at Presidential pleasure; CAG removal follows the constitutional judge-removal standard. Its further note wrongly equates all Election Commissioners with the CEC's protection: Article 324(5) protects CEC removal like a Supreme Court judge, while other Election Commissioners cannot be removed except on the CEC's recommendation.

September 2025: judicial review of constitutional inaction

  • During the pending Article 143 reference, CJI B. R. Gavai stressed that constitutional authorities cannot evade their duties; Justice Surya Kant distinguished directing a decision from commanding assent. The Union argued a universal deadline ignores differences between Bills; these are hearing submissions, not a final new ruling.
  • The issue misdates the Tamil Nadu judgment: 8 April 2025, not 2024. Existing notes give its different one-/three-month directions; the issue's blanket three-month summary is incomplete. Keep the later November opinion separate. April judgment.
