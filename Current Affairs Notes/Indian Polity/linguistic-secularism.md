# Linguistic diversity and constitutional accommodation

## Constitutional framework
- “Linguistic secularism” is the editorial's argument for equal respect and pluralism, not a separately defined constitutional office or an absolute ban on official-language policies.
- India has **no constitutionally designated national language**. **Article 343** specifies Hindi in Devanagari as Union official language; English continues under law. **Article 345** enables States' official-language choices; **Eighth Schedule lists 22 scheduled languages**, not a declaration that all 22 are Union official languages.
- **Article 29(1)** protects any section of citizens with a distinct language/script/culture; **29(2)** bars specified admission discrimination in State-maintained/aided institutions. These clauses are not simply a general minority-only language provision. **Articles 25–28** concern religious freedom; religious and linguistic protections are related pluralist commitments with different texts.
## Census and public policy
- **Census 2011 language tabulation:** **121 languages**, including **22 scheduled and 99 non-scheduled**, and **270 mother-tongue groupings with 10,000+ speakers**; not all mother tongues spoken in India. About **96.71%** reported scheduled languages as mother tongue. [Census language paper](https://www.censusindia.gov.in/nada/index.php/catalog/42458/study-description).
- Hindi imposition disputes and hostility toward non-Marathi speakers illustrate the tension between identity preservation and chauvinism. Protect local languages through teaching, materials and cultural support alongside mobility, multilingual access and non-discrimination.
- Parties, schools and media should discourage intimidation and preserve federal choice. Source's unitary “Western secularism” history is oversimplified; Western constitutional models differ. Its 250-word/15-mark answer-writing prompt is coaching practice, not an authenticated PYQ.

## November 2025: conserving languages in the digital economy
- The editorial links language vitality to **education, work, publishing, translation, tourism, local administration and digital access**. Language shift towards dominant tongues can weaken oral knowledge and intergenerational transmission; digital exclusion can compound economic marginalisation.
- Support low-resource languages through digitised manuscripts/literature, validated speech/text corpora, OCR/ASR and translation tools, youth research/startups and cross-language publishing. **BHASHINI** illustrates Indian-language translation infrastructure; performance needs testing and community validation, not an assumption every language pair is equally accurate. LLM data scarcity can reinforce language inequalities.
- Classical recognition alone does not guarantee daily use. Following **October 2024** additions **Marathi, Pali, Prakrit, Assamese and Bengali**, India had **11 classical languages**, alongside earlier Tamil, Sanskrit, Kannada, Telugu, Malayalam and Odia. Census counts and scheduled/classical status are different categories; retain the corrected Census tabulation above rather than treating 19,500 raw mother-tongue returns as a count of separately classified languages.
- **Article 350A** addresses primary-stage mother-tongue instruction facilities for children of linguistic minority groups; **350B** creates the Special Officer for Linguistic Minorities. They do not both simply mandate mother-tongue education. Article 29(1)'s scope remains any section of citizens, not minorities alone.
- Balance language preservation with multilingual mobility and creative freedom; assess AI training/copyright, translation quality and literary livelihoods without treating all AI use or short digital expression as necessarily destroying literature. The source's 250-word/15-mark bilingual mains prompt is unyearred coaching practice, not an authenticated PYQ.
