# Euthanasia, advance directives and dignity at the end of life

## November 2025: medical assessment ordered
- Supreme Court asked **Noida District Hospital** for a primary medical-board assessment concerning **31-year-old Harish Rana**, following his father's plea; source describes longstanding quadriplegia and a vegetative state. A board/report direction is **not permission already granted to withdraw treatment**. [26 November court order](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2025/60980/60980_2025_7_14_66304_Order_26-Nov-2025.pdf).
- **Active euthanasia** involves a direct life-ending act (e.g., lethal injection); **withholding/withdrawing life-sustaining treatment** is often termed passive euthanasia. Refusal of burdensome treatment and proportionate palliative care require distinct clinical/consent analysis; neither disability nor unconsciousness alone creates automatic eligibility.
- **Common Cause v. Union of India (2018)** recognised end-of-life dignity under **Article 21** and advance medical directives with safeguards; **2023 modifications** simplified implementation. A living will records an informed adult's voluntary future treatment choices when decision-making capacity may later be lost; execution and medical-board implementation are distinct stages. No general right to demand active euthanasia follows.
- As of this issue, direct intentional killing is not legalised through these rulings. The PDF's reference solely to **IPC** is outdated for new offences after **BNS commencement in 2024**; apply the relevant law and facts. A case-specific medical/legal assessment is essential.
- Consent categories: **voluntary** request, **non-voluntary** inability to consent, **involuntary** against wishes. **Quadriplegia/tetraplegia** means paralysis affecting four limbs, commonly from cervical spinal-cord injury; it is **not synonymous with a vegetative state or inevitable loss of cognition**. Support/rehabilitation and palliative care remain vital.
- Global comparisons must distinguish clinician-administered euthanasia from **physician-assisted dying**, where a patient self-administers; the source wrongly groups some US States with countries permitting active euthanasia. Balance autonomy and relief of suffering with consent, prognosis, coercion protection, disability rights and access to care.
