# Reorganisation of Indian States

## Origins and constitutional framework
- Independence integrated former provinces/princely states; Constitution **1950** classified **Part A** former governors' provinces, **Part B** princely-state unions/States headed by Rajpramukhs, **Part C** centrally administered chief-commissioner/former princely territories, **Part D** Andaman and Nicobar Islands. Examples: A—Assam/Bihar/UP/West Bengal; B—Hyderabad/J&K/Mysore; C—Delhi/Himachal Pradesh/Manipur.
- **Dhar Commission 1948** prioritised administrative/economic viability; **JVP Committee 1948–49** comprised **Jawaharlal Nehru, Vallabhbhai Patel, Pattabhi Sitaramayya**, initially opposed immediate linguistic reorganisation.
- **States Reorganisation Commission, 1953**: Fazl Ali, K.M. Panikkar and H.N. Kunzru; **1955** recommendations balanced language, unity/security, administration and economic viability—**16 States/3 centrally administered territories**, differing from the final settlement.
- **States Reorganisation Act 1956 + Seventh Amendment 1956**, effective reorganisation **1 November 1956**, produced **14 States/6 UTs**, replacing the A/B/C/D scheme. Andhra Pradesh combined existing Andhra State with Hyderabad's Telugu-speaking areas; Kerala, enlarged Bombay/Madhya Pradesh/Mysore illustrate territorial regrouping. [Act text](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/1680/1/a1956-37.pdf).
- Under **Articles 3–4**, Parliament may reorganise States through the prescribed process; affected State legislature's views are sought, not a constitutional veto. Linguistic identity is one consideration alongside viability, inclusion and federal consultation.
## Formation timeline and graphic corrections
| Year | Development |
|---|---|
| 1948–49 | Graphic notes integration of Manipur/Tripura; assess distinct accession/merger processes rather than a single identical event |
| 1953 | **Andhra State**, not Andhra Pradesh, separated from Madras following the Telugu linguistic movement; Andhra Pradesh followed in 1956 |
| 1956 | Comprehensive reorganisation; island territory then called **Laccadive, Minicoy and Amindivi Islands**, later Lakshadweep |
| 1957 | Naga Hills–Tuensang Area administrative formation; graphic incorrectly labels it a UT |
| 1960 | Bombay split into **Maharashtra/Gujarat**, following Samyukta Maharashtra/Mahagujarat movements |
| 1961 | Goa's liberation and incorporation; does not mean every European settlement's legal transfer completed in 1961 |
| 1962 | French territories' de jure transfer; distinguish earlier **1954 de facto transfer**, rather than first incorporation only in 1962 |
| 1963 | **Nagaland** statehood |
| 1966 | Punjab reorganisation: **Haryana**, **Chandigarh UT**, transfer of hill areas to Himachal Pradesh; Himachal statehood did **not** occur in 1966 |
| 1971 | **Himachal Pradesh** statehood |
| 1972 | **Manipur/Tripura** statehood; **Meghalaya**, formerly an autonomous State within Assam, became a State; Mizoram/Arunachal Pradesh became UTs |
| 1975 | **Sikkim** became State; associate-State status was introduced by **35th Amendment 1974**, superseded by **36th Amendment 1975** |
| 1987 | **Mizoram/Arunachal Pradesh** statehood; **Goa** statehood, Daman and Diu remained UT |
| 1991 | **69th Amendment** introduced Delhi NCT/Article 239AA framework; not full State status |
| 2000 | **Chhattisgarh from MP; Uttaranchal from UP; Jharkhand from Bihar**; Uttaranchal renamed Uttarakhand later |
| 2014 | **Telangana** separated from Andhra Pradesh |
| 2019 | J&K State reorganised into **Jammu and Kashmir UT/Ladakh UT** |
- Reasons vary: linguistic/cultural identity, tribal representation, development disparities, administrative access and security. Meghalaya was not simply a former UT; Himachal was not newly created by the 1966 Punjab law.
## Statehood demands: evaluate proposals
- Source examples: **Purvanchal, Bundelkhand, Awadh Pradesh; Vidarbha; Maru Pradesh; Delhi; Gorkhaland; Bodoland; Saurashtra; Rayalaseema**. These are demands/proposals, not approved new States.
- Assess identity and representation alongside fiscal viability, equitable resource sharing, minority safeguards, boundaries, infrastructure and consultation. Smaller States do not automatically ensure better governance.
