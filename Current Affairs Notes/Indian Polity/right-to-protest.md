# Right to peaceful protest and limits on strikes

## Constitutional basis
- Peaceful protest draws principally on **Article 19(1)(a)** (speech/expression), **19(1)(b)** (peaceful assembly without arms) and **19(1)(c)** (associations/unions). Article 21 supports dignity and personal liberty; the specific assembly guarantee is in Article 19.
| Right | Restriction clause and grounds |
| --- | --- |
| Speech/expression | **19(2):** sovereignty/integrity, State security, friendly foreign relations, public order, decency/morality, contempt of court, defamation, incitement to an offence |
| Peaceful assembly | **19(3):** sovereignty/integrity and public order |
| Association | **19(4):** sovereignty/integrity, public order and morality |
- Restrictions must be imposed by law and be reasonable; the broader speech grounds cannot all be transferred to the assembly clause. [Constitution, Legislative Department](https://www.legislative.gov.in/documents).
## Courts and practical balance
- **Ramlila Maidan Incident (2012):** peaceful assembly/protest is constitutionally protected; arbitrary suppression violates that protection.
- **Amit Sahni v Commissioner of Police, 7 October 2020** addressed the Shaheen Bagh protests that began in 2019: public roads cannot be occupied indefinitely. Distinguish the protest's starting year from the judgment year.
- **Strike ≠ peaceful assembly:** there is no general fundamental right to strike. Labour legislation may regulate permitted industrial action, notice, dispute procedures and sector-specific restrictions; government servants do not possess a fundamental right to strike.
- Mains balance: facilitate peaceful dissent through proportionate policing, suitable locations and dialogue, while preserving public access, safety and others' rights.
- [Supreme Court, Amit Sahni judgment](https://api.sci.gov.in/supremecourt/2020/2418/2418_2020_36_1501_24294_Judgement_07-Oct-2020.pdf).
