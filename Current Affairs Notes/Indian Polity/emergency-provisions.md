# Emergency provisions and the legacy of 1975

## 1975 Emergency: anniversary and framework
- **25 June 2025 marked 50 years** since the 1975 proclamation; source text “2024/49th” is stale. **Part XVIII, Articles 352–360** covers national emergency, constitutional breakdown in States and financial emergency. National proclamations: **1962 and 1971 external aggression; 1975 internal disturbance**, the last ending in **1977**.
## National Emergency — Article 352
- Grounds now: **war, external aggression, armed rebellion**; may cover all or part of India. President requires the **Union Cabinet's written decision**. The 44th Amendment replaced “internal disturbance” with “armed rebellion”.
- Both Houses must approve within **one month**. Approval/renewal requires **majority of total membership plus at least two-thirds present and voting**; approved continuation is **six months**, renewable. If Lok Sabha is dissolved, the special survival window is **30 days after its first sitting**, provided Rajya Sabha approved in time.
- President may revoke without parliamentary approval; must revoke if Lok Sabha disapproves. **One-tenth of Lok Sabha members** may trigger a special sitting for disapproval, to be held within **14 days**.
- **Article 358:** Article 19 restrictions relax only during emergency based on **war/external aggression**, subject to constitutional conditions. **Article 359:** a presidential order can suspend court enforcement of specified rights, **excluding Articles 20 and 21**. A proclamation does **not automatically suspend every fundamental right**. [Constitution, Part XVIII](https://www.indiacode.nic.in/bitstream/123456789/19150/1/constitution_of_india.pdf).
## President's Rule — Article 356
- Constitutional machinery breakdown; **S.R. Bommai (1994)**: judicial review, relevant material, floor-test principle for legislative majority and no irreversible assembly dissolution before parliamentary approval. **Rameshwar Prasad (2006)** reinforces limits on arbitrary dissolution.
- Approval normally within **two months**; continuance **six months at a time**, ordinarily up to **three years**, with additional constitutional conditions beyond one year. Distinguish this from the national-emergency one-month/special-majority regime.
- Source examples require correction: Uttarakhand's floor-test crisis was **2016, not 2020**; UP's **June–July 1991** President's Rule followed dismissal after Rajiv Gandhi's assassination, while **December 1992** followed the Babri Masjid demolition—separate episodes. Punjab **1987–1992** concerned militancy; Maharashtra **November 2019** concerned government formation.
- **Sarkaria:** last resort, reasoned Governor report, alternatives first and assembly initially suspended. **Punchhi:** proposed localised intervention and short duration; recommendations **are not existing permission for ordinary district-wise President's Rule**.
## Financial Emergency — Article 360
- Threat to India's/part's financial stability or credit; both Houses approve within **two months**. Once approved, no six-month renewal rule; revocable by President. It can permit financial directions, salary reductions including judges and reservation of State financial bills. **Never proclaimed as of June 2025**.
## Amendments and safeguards
- **38th Amendment Act, 1975:** attempted immunity for emergency satisfaction; **44th Amendment Act, 1978** removed that shield and strengthened safeguards. **42nd Amendment Act, 1976:** allowed territorial limitation of a national proclamation and increased centralising powers; its provisions need clause-specific treatment.
- **39th Amendment Act, 1975** inserted Article 329A concerning high-office election disputes; **Article 329A(4)** shielding the PM's election was struck down in **Indira Nehru Gandhi v Raj Narain (1975)**. Do not describe every provision/the whole amendment as judicially invalidated.
- The 42nd Amendment's six-year legislative terms were reversed by the 44th; restrictions on judicial review/basic-structure questions require provision-specific analysis. Emergency review and civil liberties show why effective crisis powers need parliamentary, judicial and federal checks.
