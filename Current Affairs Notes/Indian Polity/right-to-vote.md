# Right to vote and universal adult suffrage

## Constitutional foundation and legislation
- **Article 326** provides adult suffrage for Lok Sabha/Assembly elections: citizens **18+**, subject to lawful disqualifications; **Article 325** prohibits exclusion from a general electoral roll solely on religion, race, caste or sex. Representation of the People Act 1950 regulates registration; **1951 Act, section 62** governs voting subject to its conditions/disqualifications.
- Distinguish **entitlement to vote**, traditionally characterised as statutory in *N.P.Ponnuswami (1952), Jyoti Basu(1982), Kuldip Nayar(2006)*, from **freedom of voting/expression**, protected by Article 19(1)(a). Constitutional origin does not mean every detail is a fundamental right or immune from statutory conditions.
- *PUCL(2003)* and *Raj Bala(2015)* discuss constitutional foundations. In *Anoop Baranwal(2023)*, Justice **Ajay Rastogi wrote a separate concurring opinion**, arguing for fundamental-right protection in direct elections by reading Article 326 with Articles 15,17,19,21. The PDF wrongly calls it a dissent and reduces it to a mere request for future consideration; do not attribute his separate reasoning to the whole Bench. [Reported judgment, especially pp 199–205](https://patnahighcourt.gov.in/ILR/viewpdf.aspx?ID=NjYx-dRYRy6tBACQ%3D&T=EN).
## Universal adult suffrage
- Inclusive political voice irrespective of property, education, caste, gender or religion; India adopted adult franchise from the Republic's beginning rather than a property-based gradual extension. The original age was 21, reduced to 18 by the 61st Amendment; do not project 18 backwards to 1950.
- **New Zealand 1893** is the landmark national women's-franchise example. Voting and eligibility to stand are distinct rights.
- Democratic benefits: participation, accountability, empowerment of marginalised groups and a more representative party system. Obstacles include migrant/disability access, unequal civic awareness, misinformation, vote buying and exclusionary registration practices.
- Bihar electoral-roll debate illustrates why classification and due process matter. Statutory rights remain constrained by constitutional equality/non-arbitrariness; they are not freely removable without review. Printed 250 word/15 mark question is coaching practice.
