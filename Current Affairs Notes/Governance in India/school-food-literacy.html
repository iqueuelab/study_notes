Governance in India

Food literacy and nutrition education in schools

Editable notes

April 2025 editorial argument

  • Move from food provision alone towards food literacy: knowing how to choose, prepare and understand nutritious food. Connect health, learning, equity, culture, biodiversity and sustainability.
  • First 1,000 days (conception to age two) remain crucial; the editorial highlights the next 4,000 days, childhood/adolescence, for cognition, emotional health, habits and long-term disease prevention. Schools shape peer norms; children can carry learning to households.
  • Risks: breakfast skipping, low fruit/vegetable intake, sugary/processed foods, marketing and delivery-app environments; lack of diversity can coexist with obesity and micronutrient deficiency. Monocultures, affordability and loss of traditional food knowledge also shape choices. The assertion that “70% of preventable adult diseases” originate in childhood diets is not substantiated in the issue.

Global context and dietary-diversity correction

  • UN General Assembly extended the 2016–2025 Nutrition Decade to 2030 on 25 March 2025; FAO/WHO co-lead. The Nutrition for Growth summit provides a commitment platform. UN decision.
  • The PDF conflates indicators: MDD-W = women aged 15–49 consuming at least 5 of 10 food groups in the previous 24 hours; young-child MDD = ages 6–23 months, at least 5 of 8 groups. Neither is automatically a validated “5 of 10” test for all schoolchildren. FAO MDD-W, WHO child MDD.

Proposed school response

  • Age-appropriate learning from preschool through middle school: local/seasonal foods, label reading, simple cooking, kitchen gardens, food systems, food waste and climate/biodiversity; address emotional relationships with food as well as nutrient knowledge.
  • Editorial recommends a weekly lesson, teacher training through NCERT, SCERTs and DIETs, experiential materials and family/community participation; these are recommendations, not a newly notified national mandate.
  • Link NEP 2020 holistic/experiential learning, Ayushman Bharat School Health and Wellness Programme, and PM-POSHAN meals/gardens. Food supply and education are complementary; a meal entitlement alone does not prove food literacy.
  • Barriers: curriculum load, teacher capability, kitchens/garden space, funding and fragmented delivery. Develop an integrated framework, support teachers and measure suitable dietary/learning outcomes without inappropriate indicator transfer or stigma.
  • Better nutrition → stronger learning → healthier human capital, with potentially lower future health costs. Agency, care for others and cultural continuity are ethical aims; benefits still depend on implementation and equitable access.
