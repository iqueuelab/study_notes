Governance in India

Right to Information: transparency, privacy and implementation

Editable notes

Statutory framework: revision points

  • The right to know is derived from Article 19(1)(a); State of UP v Raj Narain was decided in 1975, not 1976. RTI Act 2005 supplies the request/disclosure machinery.
  • Section 2(h): constitutional/statutory/notified public authorities; bodies owned, controlled or substantially financed by government, including substantially financed NGOs. Not every private organisation is directly covered.
  • Section 4: record management/proactive disclosure; 6: request procedure; 7: response timelines; 8: exemptions/public-interest balance; 19: first and second appeals; 20: penalties; 23: statutory court bar.
  • Ordinary request normally 30 days, life/liberty 48 hours. Information Commission may impose ₹250 per day, maximum ₹25,000, on the PIO under section 20 after the statutory process; penalty is not automatically deducted whenever a reply arrives late.
  • Applicant need not explain why information is wanted. RTI covers existing information/records rather than requiring officials to create a new analysis or answer hypothetical questions.
  • Exemptions include protected national-security/foreign-relations material, commercial confidence/IP where competitive interests would be harmed, confidential sources, investigation impediments and specified Cabinet records. These are qualified statutory tests—not a blanket exemption for every document touching those subjects.
  • Section 8(2) permits disclosure where public interest outweighs protected harm, notwithstanding the Official Secrets Act/exemptions; section 10 allows severing exempt portions.
  • Appeal within the department → CIC/SIC second appeal; commissions also have complaint functions. PIOs/public authorities give information in the first instance, not only commissions.
  • Section 23 does not eliminate constitutional judicial review: Supreme Court Article 32 / High Court Article 226, not Article 225. DoPT RTI Act.

Disclosure–privacy balance

  • CBSE v.Aditya Bandopadhyay2011: recognised access to evaluated answer-books while discussing practical limits; not a judgment simply eliminating transparency or every student's access.
  • Girish Ramchandra Deshpande2012: personal/service information and demonstrated larger public interest; disclosure debate needs facts and applicable law, not an assumption no public official has privacy.
  • Source-era parliamentary-information proviso matters, but does not justify skipping all other statutory/contextual analysis.

Amendments: specify status

  • 2019 amendment changed service-condition control for Information Commissioners; institutional-independence concerns differ from content exemptions.
  • DPDP Act2023 section44(3) legislated replacement of8(1)(j)'s personal-information wording; not operative in February 2025 merely because enacted. Distinguish that historical discussion from later commencement/notifications.
  • Reforms proposed in the editorial—reverse amendments, strengthen penalties/new protection laws—are proposals, not existing provisions.

Improve access

  • Timely transparent appointments, diverse expertise, digitised records and accessible tracking; publish reasons, enforce existing non-compliance penalties and protect applicants.
  • Proactive disclosure reduces repetitive requests; exemptions must be lawful/proportionate and appellate oversight timely. Compare case-disposal figures cautiously; different institutions have unlike case complexity.
