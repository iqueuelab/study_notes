Governance in India

Digital content: free speech, obscenity and OTT regulation

Editable notes

Rights and statutory thresholds

  • Article 19(1)(a) expression; 19(2) permits only constitutionally valid reasonable restrictions. Public dislike/vulgarity ≠automatic criminal obscenity.
  • Apoorva Arora, 19 March 2024: distinguish profanity from legally obscene material and assess content/context under the law; it does not automatically decide every later creator's liability. Supreme Court judgment.
  • Modern community-standards/context analysis differs from the old Hicklin isolated-passage test; do not present them as one identical current test.
  • IT Act 69A blocking: sovereignty/integrity, defence, security, friendly foreign relations, public order and preventing incitement to related cognizable offences. “Decency/morality” is not a freestanding listed 69A ground; 67/67A/67B address different prohibited electronic content.

OTT framework and February advisory

  • IT Rules 2021: publisher grievance officer →industry self-regulatory body →government oversight. Distinguish OTT publishers, digital news and social-media intermediaries' obligations.
  • Age ratings U /U-A 7+ /U-A 13+ /U-A 16+ /A; parental locks for U-A 13+ and higher, reliable age verification for A. Advisory compliance is not the creation of a new CBFC pre-certification system.
  • Relevant laws include BNS, POCSO, Indecent Representation of Women Act 1986 and IT Act; each has a defined scope. Television Cable Act 1995 and cinema Cinematograph Act 1952 use different frameworks.
  • Broadcasting-bill proposals and committee comments were not a February 2025 enacted universal “digital censor board”; ongoing legal challenges/implementation must be distinguished from draft policy.

Assess regulation

  • Protect children and address lawful harms through transparent rules, notice/reasons, appeals and independent scrutiny; disproportionate threats can chill journalism/art.
  • Source's techno-patrimonialism/DBT-electoral-loyalty link is a political interpretation, not established proof every transfer purchases loyalty.
  • International examples have different scopes: MPA ratings are not universal US statutory OTT censorship; EU on-demand obligations are not identical to every broadcasting rule.
  • Improve grievance handling, media literacy and child safeguards while preserving lawful dissent and consistent, proportionate enforcement.

X petition and Sahyog: March 2025

  • IT Act section 69A concerns blocking public access with specified grounds and rules; section 79 concerns conditional intermediary immunity/safe harbour.
  • Shreya Singhal (2015) upheld 69A with safeguards and read section 79 actual knowledge narrowly around court orders or appropriate government notification, linked to Article 19(2).
  • X alleged section 79 notices/Sahyog bypassed 69A safeguards; government characterised them as liability notices and coordination. These were litigating positions, not a final March 2025 judgment.
  • A portal does not create unlimited powers for every police officer. Assess statutory authority, reasons, notice/hearing, review, transparency and remedy against unlawful removal.
  • Prompt lawful action on illegal content and protection from arbitrary censorship require clear procedures; blocking, takedown and immunity loss are different legal effects.

April 2025 petition on OTT obscenity

  • April 29 reporting describes Supreme Court notice to the Union on a plea concerning sexually explicit/obscene content and children's access. Notice and expressed concern are procedural developments, not a final censorship order or finding that all challenged material is unlawful.
  • Government said existing measures were in place and further action was being considered; court indicated executive/legislative responsibility while keeping the matter pending.
  • OTT delivers content through the internet, bypassing conventional broadcast distribution; inexpensive data, smartphones and pandemic viewing accelerated adoption.
  • Balance Article 19(1)(a) with proportionate 19(2) restrictions for decency/morality; criminal obscenity, adult-rated expression and mere vulgarity have different thresholds.
  • Existing three-tier complaints, age ratings, parental locks and adult-age checks should be strengthened through clear reasons, appeals, effective child safeguards and digital literacy. Independent public accountability is a policy recommendation, not an authority created by this petition.
  • The source's platform/user/industry totals are undated estimates; its claim that IT Rules are the only applicable legal instrument is incorrect given IT Act, POCSO and other applicable statutes.
  • Practice answer: constitutional test → existing regulation → child access/enforcement gaps → proportionate safeguards and remedies.

11 May 2025: safe harbour and intermediary liability

  • IT Act section 79 provides conditional protection for third-party information, not blanket immunity for a platform's own unlawful conduct. Statutory conditions include a limited transmission role, not initiating transmission/selecting its receiver/selecting or modifying information, and due diligence. Loss of safe harbour removes that protection; liability still depends on the relevant offence/cause of action and evidence.
  • The 2004 Baazee.com episode involved user-listed illegal material and an executive's arrest; an arrest is not a finding of guilt, nor a model for every case under the later amended law. US CDA section 230 (1996) is a different jurisdiction's framework, not an exact copy of India's provisions.
  • Shreya Singhal's court/appropriate-government knowledge standard and section 69A safeguards remain relevant. Under the 2021 framework discussed here, specified court/authorised-government removal notices had a 36-hour outer limit; this is not a universal deadline for all complaints. Intimate/non-consensual sexual content complaints, grievance acknowledgment/disposal and assistance requests have different rules.
  • Significant social media intermediaries, rather than every website/platform, face the additional resident compliance officer, nodal contact officer, resident grievance officer and monthly-report duties. MeitY FAQ on scope and timelines.
  • The disputed 2023 fact-check amendment addressed allegedly fake/false/misleading information concerning Central Government business, not all “fake news”. The Bombay High Court struck the challenged provision down in September 2024; the issue reports a government appeal. Do not present that clause as operative merely because an appeal is mentioned, or describe the whole IT Rules as struck down.
  • The reported Digital India Act remained a proposed overhaul with no public draft identified in this issue, not an enacted replacement for section 79. X's challenge and complaints about delayed takedowns were ongoing disputes, not final legal outcomes at this date.
  • Balance prompt lawful responses to fraud, deepfakes and prohibited content with clear grounds, proportionate obligations, transparency, user notice/reasons where applicable, appeal and independent oversight. The editorial's court-only takedown proposal is a reform recommendation: existing law also recognised appropriate government notifications. Liability, blocking, platform moderation and safe-harbour loss are distinct mechanisms.
