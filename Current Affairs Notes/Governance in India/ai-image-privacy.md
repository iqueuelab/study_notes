# AI image generation: privacy, consent and safeguards

## Photos, AI and consent
- Uploading a portrait shares identifiable personal data; facial images can enable impersonation, profiling or deepfakes. A stylised output does not automatically anonymise the original input.
- Risks include unclear retention/training practices, third-party sharing, unauthorised reuse and security breaches. These are risk pathways, not evidence that a named service has suffered a breach or violated GDPR.
- Processing an image to fulfil a request, retaining it, and reusing it for training are different purposes. Check the applicable service, account settings and terms; do not assume every upload is used for training or that deletion guarantees immediate removal from every model.
## Governance and practical safeguards
- Privacy principles: clear notice and lawful basis; purpose limitation; data minimisation; retention limits; access security; meaningful user control and grievance redress.
- India's **Digital Personal Data Protection Act, 2023** provides the statutory framework for digital personal data. Enactment and commencement/implementation are separate; do not treat all provisions as operational on 2 April 2025 merely because the Act was passed.
- Protect children's and bystanders' privacy; avoid unnecessary identity documents or sensitive backgrounds. Consent to a photograph is not automatically consent to every AI reuse.
- Transparent policies, usable controls, privacy by design, audits and public digital-literacy campaigns can preserve creative benefits while reducing misuse. AI ethics proposals are not themselves binding law.
- **Mains balance:** creativity and accessibility ↔ privacy, autonomy, cybersecurity and accountability. Assess actual processing and safeguards rather than assuming innovation is inherently unlawful.
- Statutory reference: [DPDP Act, 2023, official Gazette](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2024/02/Digital-Personal-Data-Protection-Act-2023.pdf).

## November 2025: personality rights and generative AI
- Source discusses **Abhishek Bachchan and Aishwarya Rai Bachchan's Delhi High Court litigation** concerning fabricated/explicit videos and unauthorised use of identity on Google/YouTube, including demands for compensation and safeguards against AI training. Allegations and requested remedies are not final judicial findings or a general ban on all AI training.
- Personality/publicity interests concern **name, image, likeness, voice, signature and identifiable persona**, linking privacy, dignity/autonomy and commercial endorsement value. Deepfake advertisements, cloned voices and impersonating chatbots create fraud, reputational harm, non-consensual sexual imagery and loss of trust.
- Indian protection is distributed across constitutional privacy, passing off/IP and other applicable law and judicial injunctions; source recalls **Amitabh Bachchan (2022), Anil Kapoor (2023), Arijit Singh (2024)**. Case-specific injunctions do not confer ownership of every imitation, mannerism or critical artistic use. Article 21 privacy must be balanced with Article 19 expression and lawful restrictions; Article 300A is not a standalone codified personality-rights statute.
- Comparative recall: **Haelan Laboratories v. Topps (US, 1953)** and Tennessee's **ELVIS Act 2024** illustrate publicity/voice protection; China has recognised actionable synthetic-voice misuse. Avoid sweeping claims that every AI output loses free-speech protection or that all jurisdictions use one privacy/property model.
- **EU AI Act:** deepfake disclosure is an **Article 50 transparency** issue; a deepfake is **not automatically an Annex III high-risk system**. GDPR has several lawful bases, not mandatory consent for every personal-data processing. Enactment and phased application differ. [European Commission Article 50 explanation](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/faqs/transparency-obligations-under-article-50-ai-act).
- Reform proposals: clear rights/remedies, provenance/labelling, proportionate platform duties and prompt redress, cross-border enforcement and protection of vulnerable users. Distinguish proposals from operative law; source's vague “2024 Intermediary Guidelines” should not replace the actual applicable IT Rules/advisories or DPDP commencement chronology already recorded.
- Ethical issues include deceased-person replicas, consent/control, exploitative companion/therapy bots and innovation. Not every posthumous claim is resolved by the assertion that all relevant IP/privacy/property interests necessarily end together at death.

## December 2025: NCII and the ‘fishbowl society’
- The editorial uses **fishbowl society** for pervasive visibility/data tracking that erodes privacy and practical obscurity. **Non-consensual intimate imagery (NCII)** includes real or morphed intimate material disseminated without consent; AI abuse can impair dignity, bodily autonomy, employment and mental health without any consensual original photograph.
- **11 November 2025 MeitY SOP** provides victim/platform reporting and response guidance under existing **IT Rules 2021, Rule 3(2)(b)**, including removal/disabling access within **24 hours** after a qualifying complaint. It follows Madras High Court directions and is **not a standalone new criminal statute**. [Official SOP](https://www.meity.gov.in/static/uploads/2025/11/a2c9500ef5f8b62a43bfc68747de592d.pdf).
- Correct the PDF's ‘no accountability/no binding duties’ absolutes: an SOP can elaborate duties under existing rules, even if it does not itself create new offences. The official guidance refers to **an individual/authorised representative**; the initiating case's focus on women does not prove every existing takedown protection excludes transgender or male victims. Distinguish a critique of inclusive implementation from the legal scope of each provision.
- **Puttaswamy 2017** recognises privacy as a fundamental right; IT/criminal law already supplies remedies for specified acts. DPDP enactment/commencement remain separate, and absence of a bespoke AI statute does not create universal immunity. The source's IPC shorthand is dated after replacement by BNS for relevant post-commencement offences.
- Underreporting, stigma, police capacity and absence of a clean standalone NCII/deepfake statistical category hinder assessment; do not assert NCRB has no related categories or treat an unspecified NGO ‘thousands daily’ claim as a verified national total. Priorities: accessible reporting, evidence preservation, rapid redress, victim-sensitive support, investigator training, platform safeguards and clearly scoped developer duties.
