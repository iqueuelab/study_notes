Governance in India

Disaster risk financing and preventive resilience

Editable notes

October 2025: financing prevention alongside response

  • Fifteenth Finance Commission's 2021–26 framework expands disaster finance beyond relief: national/state risk-management funds comprise 80% response funds and 20% mitigation funds. State risk-management allocation ₹1,60,153 crore plus national ₹68,463 crore totals ₹2,28,616 crore (rounded ₹2.28 lakh crore).
  • Within the state framework, shares of the whole fund: response/relief 40%, recovery/reconstruction 30%, preparedness/capacity 10%, mitigation 20%. Thus 30% is preventive/preparedness and 70% post-disaster; 80% response-fund allocation includes preparedness, not only emergency relief. MHA annual report, fund framework.
  • MHA coordinates central disaster policy; NDMA sets policy/guidelines; NIDM trains/researches; NDRF provides specialist response. The Prime Minister's 2016 ten-point agenda and Sendai Framework 2015–30 support mainstreaming risk into development/public finance.
  • Match measures to hazards: wetlands/water bodies and green space for urban floods; vegetation/bioengineering for slope stability; rejuvenate Brahmaputra beels; manage forest fuel/fire lines. GLOF remote sensing/weather stations are monitoring technology, not themselves nature-based restoration.
  • Source reports ₹5,000-crore reconstruction across Uttarakhand, Himachal Pradesh, Sikkim, Assam and Kerala, ₹5,000 crore for fire services and roughly ₹10,000-crore mitigation projects; these are dated source figures, not all components of one project or automatic state entitlements. Its 2.5-lakh volunteer figure combines Aapda Mitra/Yuva Aapda Mitra discussion and should not imply every planned volunteer already trained.
  • CAP multilingual/multimedia warnings, last-mile communications, drills, school safety, Panchayat training, geospatial labs and university networks join finance with local capability. The historical National Cyclone Risk Mitigation Project covered eight coastal states; shelters, embankments, evacuation and early warning collectively reduce deaths, rather than a uniform guaranteed seven-day forecast for every hazard. CDRI and regional cooperation share resilience expertise.

November editorial: fiscal federalism and disaster relief

  • Argument: delayed/discretionary assistance and gaps between assessed needs and releases can strain State finances and cooperative federalism. Wayanad, July 2024 is the case study; the source's ~₹2,200-crore loss estimate versus ~₹260-crore assistance compares different accounting concepts, not necessarily all public support received. Its roughly 11% comparison is a narrow editorial calculation, not a complete compensation rate.
  • Distinguish allocation, approval, cash release and expenditure, and separate grants, regular SDRF balances and capital-assistance loans. Later official accounting identifies ₹260.56 crore approved central recovery/reconstruction share, with ₹78.17 crore first instalment released 17 October 2025; it is incorrect to call the whole approval an already paid tranche. Source ~₹529-crore loan / ₹780-crore balance are separate reported financing/balance figures, not interchangeable relief grants. Official accounting.
  • Immediate relief under notified SDRF norms is distinct from the wider recovery/reconstruction and mitigation windows already described above. The blanket claim that the framework has no reconstruction provision is incorrect. Relief norms such as ₹4 lakh ex gratia for death are assistance ceilings, not a valuation of life or full indemnification; the source’s ₹1.2-lakh house-assistance example is category/terrain-dependent, not one universal reimbursement or full rebuilding cost.
  • Additional central assistance follows assessment/approval procedures, not automatic full reimbursement of every State memorandum. Improve transparent criteria and timelines; an accounting balance may include commitments, but that needs evidence rather than assuming every balance is already spent. The Fifteenth Finance Commission methodology already incorporates past expenditure, exposure and a Disaster Risk Index—the claim that hazards are wholly ignored is false. Finance Commission methodology.

January 2026: disaster losses as fiscal risk

  • OECD-based source chart estimates India's average annual recorded disaster damage at about 0.4% of GDP, 1990–2024. This is a historical average ratio, not an identical loss every year, not a forecast, and not a licence to multiply today's GDP to recreate the historical rupee series. Recorded direct damage can omit wider welfare, health and indirect output losses.
  • Source describes Emerging Asia at roughly 100 disasters / 80 million people affected annually; chart country composition/time windows vary. India faces substantial flood/storm losses; China leads the chart's absolute disaster count. Frequency, intensity, reported damage and risk-index ranking are distinct measures; greater exposure/assets and reporting changes also raise measured losses.
  • Its second-highest Asian World Risk Index position after the Philippines is a source-chart snapshot without a clearly specified index edition, not a timeless current rank. Risk combines exposure with vulnerability, including susceptibility, coping and adaptation capacity; the PDF's additive slogan is not the index's formal formula. Lower historical geophysical damage share does not make Indian seismic risk negligible.
  • Fiscal response: prevention/adaptation and resilient infrastructure, contingency budgets, insurance/parametric cover and catastrophe bonds, alongside recovery finance. Match financing layers to hazard frequency/severity, affordability and basis risk; Sendai resilience goals link development planning with fiscal protection. Existing Indian fund architecture remains covered above.
