Governance in India

Custodial violence, data and police accountability

Editable notes

Ajith Kumar case and accountability

  • June 2025: security guard Ajith Kumar died in police custody in Sivaganga, Tamil Nadu; Madurai Bench's response renewed scrutiny of police violence. Sathankulam 2020 and Ambasamudram 2023 are earlier cases, not current incidents.
  • Custodial death includes police custody and judicial custody; a death in custody is not automatically established murder/torture. Independent investigation and medical evidence determine cause/liability.
  • Source 2016–17 to 2021–22: 11,656 reported custodial deaths nationally;UP 2,630;Tamil Nadu 490; combines custody categories and is not all deaths caused by police assault. Embedded map also lists Maharashtra 755, West Bengal 955.
  • Separate 2017–2022 police-custody death cases: 123 police arrested,79 chargesheeted, zero convicted in the source dataset. Broader police human-rights chart gives 75 arrested,41 chargesheeted,3 convicted; the issue's prose says 74 cases whereas chart shows 78—do not merge these into one consistent national total.
  • Preventive detenues,31 December 2022: TN 2,129, SC share 38.5% versus 20.01% population. Chart comparators: J&K 546(1.3%SC/7.38%population), Gujarat 334(9.6%/6.74%), Haryana 326(1.3%/20.17%), UP 172(7.6%/20.7%). These are detention-composition snapshots, not direct measures of the caste share of torture victims or causal proof about every arrest.
  • Detenue≠convict; preventive detention differs from pretrial remand. Protect Articles 21/22 safeguards, prompt independent inquiry, legal aid, medical documentation, court/NHRC oversight, witness protection and police accountability; conviction counts lag incidents and need case-year/outcome interpretation.

Safeguards and reform

  • Physical assault, sexual violence, denial of medical care, threats and coercive interrogation violate dignity and life/liberty. Articles 14, 21 and 20(3) support equality, liberty and protection against compelled self-incrimination; Article 20(1) concerns retrospective criminal punishment.
  • BNS Section 120 addresses hurt/grievous hurt to extort confession or compel restoration of property; it is not limited to police. BNSS Section 35 governs specified arrest powers/procedures; BSA Section 22 excludes confessions caused by inducement, threat, coercion or promise under its conditions. BNS text.
  • D.K. Basu (1997): arrest memo, informing relatives, medical examination and access to legal safeguards. Nambi Narayanan (2018) illustrates compensation for wrongful arrest. The sheet's Ram Sagar Yadav (1985) sentence should not be memorised as an automatic universal reversal of the criminal burden of proof; custodial evidence requires careful judicial evaluation.
  • India signed UNCAT in 1997 but had not ratified it in the source period. Ratification and a dedicated anti-torture framework, independent complaints/investigation, medical documentation, witness/victim protection, legal aid and separation of investigation from routine law-and-order duties are reform proposals. Do not equate preventive detention with proof a person was about to commit an offence, or every custodial death with proven torture.

Evidence-led interrogation

  • July 31 editorial contrasts coercive 'Dirty Harry' policing with professional investigation. Source's 687 deaths,2018–19 to 2022–23, is a reported police-custody series; do not merge it with all police+judicial custody deaths or assume every death was proven torture.
  • Torture undermines memory and encourages false confessions. Better methods: PEACE—Preparation and Planning, Engage and Explain, Account, Closure, Evaluation; rapport, open questions, recorded interviews, corroboration and forensic evidence. HIG research and wrongful-confession histories support examining reliability rather than romanticising coercion.
  • Source cites Global Torture Index 2025 high-risk classification, an external assessment, not a court finding in every case. Law Commission's 273rd report (2017) recommended a dedicated anti-torture law; existing July 11 notes cover UNCAT status, D.K. Basu and legal safeguards.
  • Invest in forensic capacity/training, independent complaints and investigation, witness protection, medical access and accountable supervision. The daily mains prompt is publisher practice, not a UPSC PYQ.

Judicial language and caste-sensitive accountability

  • Editorial criticises a Chhattisgarh High Court judgment describing police conduct toward a Dalit victim as an attempt to teach a lesson. This is the editorial's assessment of a specific case; punishment requires lawful process, not police retaliation or judicially excused violence.
  • The article disputes non-application of the SC/ST (Prevention of Atrocities) Act and stresses structural caste power. A victim's caste alone does not automatically prove every statutory offence; courts must apply the relevant provision and evidence without ignoring circumstantial or structural discrimination.
  • Articles 14, 21 and 22, D.K. Basu safeguards, independent investigation, medical documentation, CCTV/recorded arrests, witness protection, compensation and rehabilitation support accountability. Article 17 is relevant where untouchability practices are involved; Article 19 freedoms do not replace the specific arrest safeguards of Article 22.
  • Avoid legitimising torture through correction/deterrence language; judicial reasons influence policing norms. Source's generic 'hundreds annually' lacks a period/custody category; retain the existing notes' distinctions between police/judicial custody deaths and proven torture.
  • Daily Mains practice (250 words, 15 marks) asks about the judiciary's role: balance condemnation of custodial violence, effective safeguards and evidence-based liability. It is publisher practice, not a past CSE question.

November 2025: CCTV compliance scrutiny

  • Supreme Court Bench of Vikram Nath and Sandeep Mehta criticised missing compliance with Paramvir Singh Saini v. Baljit Singh (2 December 2020). Source reports only 11 States/UTs submitted reports, none from the Centre, and a 19 December compliance deadline with possible personal accountability of agency directors/Home Secretaries. Filing a report is not proof of complete compliance.
  • Directions cover police stations and offices of central investigative agencies conducting interrogation, including CBI, ED, NIA, NCB, DRI and SFIO: night vision, audio/video, interrogation rooms, lockups, entrances/corridors and other specified areas; State/district oversight and maintenance matter as much as installation.
  • The 2020 direction aims for 18-month storage; where technology is unavailable, procure the maximum possible storage, not below one year, and upgrade. The source's unconditional “at least 18 months in every installation” omits this qualification. Supreme Court judgment.
  • Source cites 11 custodial deaths in eight months in Rajasthan as a dated illustration, not a proven torture finding in every death. CCTV deters abuse and preserves evidence, protecting detainees and officers from false allegations; access, tamper prevention, working audio and independent investigation remain necessary.
  • Article 21 dignity, 22 arrest safeguards, 32/226 remedies; Article 141 makes law declared by SC binding on courts, while Article 144 requires civil/judicial authorities to act in aid of SC. Do not paraphrase Article 141 alone as the full rule governing executive compliance. Existing UNCAT/D.K. Basu and custody-category cautions remain relevant.
