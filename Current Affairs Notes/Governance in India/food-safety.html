Food safety: contamination, toxins and cold chains

Editable notes

Spoiled coconut water: infection versus poisoning

  • The report recycled in April 2025 is not evidence that ordinary fresh coconut water causes brain infection. A 2021 published Danish case linked fatal encephalopathy to 3-nitropropionic acid, produced in a spoiled coconut by the fungus Arthrinium saccharicola. Investigators considered bongkrekic acid but did not detect it.
  • Encephalopathy = disturbed brain function; toxic/metabolic encephalopathy does not itself mean microbes infected the brain. Foodborne illness may involve infection, preformed toxins, or both.
  • Improper storage permits spoilage and microbial growth. Refrigeration slows growth; it neither sterilises food nor reliably removes toxins already formed. Follow product storage/use-by instructions and reject spoiled products rather than taste-testing them.

Unsafe storage → microbial growth/toxin formation → ingestion → gastrointestinal illness or systemic harm.

Governance and prevention

  • FSSAI: statutory food regulator under the Food Safety and Standards Act, 2006. Prevention requires hygienic handling, safe water, appropriate packaging, traceability, storage labels, and risk-based inspections across the supply chain.
  • Protect vulnerable consumers through vendor training and public awareness. Temperature sensors and cold-chain monitoring can flag failures; they supplement testing and enforcement.
  • Shared responsibility: producers and sellers ensure safe processing and clear instructions; regulators monitor compliance; consumers follow storage advice. Accountability should rest on evidence, not an assumption that every incident proves seller negligence.

Risk-based regulation: June 2025 editorial

  • Prevention of Food Adulteration Act 1954 →Food Safety and Standards Act 2006: consolidated, science-based food regulation. FSSAI established 2008, under Health Ministry; the PDF conflates Act year with institutional establishment. Older regulation did contain standards/limits—do not infer it ignored all contaminant distinctions.
  • Hazard: potential to harm; risk: harm likelihood/severity at actual exposure. MRL, mg/kg food, is an enforcement limit for a pesticide–commodity pair; ADI, usually mg/kg body weight/day, is estimated long-term daily exposure without appreciable health risk. MRL is not itself a toxic-dose threshold, and exceedance does not automatically quantify illness.
  • Total Diet Study (TDS) assesses population dietary exposure from representative foods, often prepared as consumed. Indian consumption, residues and vulnerable groups matter; imported toxicology data can remain informative while local exposure data improve relevance.
  • Editorial identifies gaps in representative national TDS, local data, trained assessors and communication; do not declare every existing Indian exposure estimate invalid or prove all standards attained Codex equivalence by 2020.
  • Source 0.01→0.1 mg/kg example needs pesticide/food/regulatory context; some default-limit discussions concerned spices/herbs, not a tenfold relaxation of every pesticide standard. Changes require evidence and transparent exposure assessment; a higher MRL alone neither proves increased toxicity nor automatically proves unchanged safety.

MSG and communicating evidence

  • Monosodium glutamate is a flavour additive; glutamate occurs naturally in foods and breast milk. JECFA 1987 “ADI not specified” means evaluated uses did not require a numeric ADI, not approval of unlimited doses or every infant-food use. JECFA evaluation.
  • The editorial critiques infant-warning labels and proposes review; natural occurrence alone does not establish every formulation/dose safe, and a recommendation is not proof labelling rules were repealed.
  • Proposals: representative local dietary/contaminant studies, periodic evidence review, staff training, clear charts/units, transparent assumptions, accessible public consultation and accurate uncertainty communication.

Codex: institution and trade

  • Codex Alimentarius is the collection of food standards; Codex Alimentarius Commission (CAC) is the FAO/WHO intergovernmental body, founded 1963, Secretariat/Rome association.
  • 189 members =188 countries +EU member organisation, not 189 countries. Regular FAO/WHO budgets support its work; observer status and membership differ. Codex membership.
  • Standards/guidelines/codes are generally voluntary international texts, with national enforceability through domestic adoption. WTO SPS Agreement recognises Codex food-safety standards as benchmarks; this is not a Codex court deciding every food trade dispute or automatic domestic legal force.
  • Scientific expert assessments (JECFA/JMPR) underpin ADIs/residue work; distinguish expert risk assessment from CAC adoption/risk management.
  • Committees: CCSCH—Spices/Culinary Herbs, hosted by India; CCCF—Contaminants; CCPR—Pesticide Residues; CCFA—Food Additives. Hygiene, labelling and contaminant/residue guidance protect health and support fair trade; country-specific stronger measures need applicable scientific/legal justification.
