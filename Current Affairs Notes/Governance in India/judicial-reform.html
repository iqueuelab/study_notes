Governance in India

Judicial reform, access and public trust

Editable notes

Diagnose the bottlenecks

  • Former CJI N. V. Ramana highlighted delay, cost, language barriers, weak infrastructure, appointment opacity and under-representation. Comments are reform arguments, not adjudicated findings against every judge.
  • Pendency and gender counts are dated snapshots; sanctioned, working and occupied posts differ. Do not treat the source's “33 positions” as the permanent Supreme Court sanctioned strength.
  • Speedy trial and fair access follow Article 21 jurisprudence; Article 39A supports equal justice/legal aid. Hussainara Khatoon (1979) is an important undertrial/speedy-trial landmark.
  • Priorities: timely vacancies, staff/infrastructure, case management, fewer avoidable adjournments, legal aid, intelligible language and accessible e-courts; digital-only access can exclude people.
  • Diversity and transparent merit criteria improve legitimacy. Judicial performance cannot be reduced to disposal counts at the expense of fairness or judgment quality.
  • “Indianisation” can mean accessible language/procedures, not rejection of constitutional rights. The unexplained “30th Law Commission Report” attribution should not be used as verified evidence of nepotism.

Reform instruments and limits

  • e-Courts, case management, legal aid and infrastructure address different bottlenecks; filling posts alone will not remove every delay.
  • Ordinary Lok Adalats settle eligible disputes by consent, not adjudicate contested merits like a regular court; non-compoundable offences are outside their settlement jurisdiction. Permanent Lok Adalats have a distinct public-utility framework.
  • NJAC was struck down in 2015. Judicial Accountability and Standards Bill and All India Judicial Service proposals were not enacted operational institutions in March 2025.
  • Article 312 allows the AIJS route under its special procedure; a service cannot include posts inferior to district judge. States' language, recruitment and federal concerns require assessment.
  • Malimath Committee 2003 concerned criminal-justice reform; recommendations are not automatically law. Vineet Narain focuses on independence/accountability of investigation, not a generic judicial-performance ranking.
  • Combine quality, rights, timeliness and access in evaluation; mandatory disposal quotas can harm fair hearings.

All-India judicial service: proposal versus existing law

  • Article 312 already authorises an all-India judicial service, subject to its conditions; it cannot include posts below district judge. This is different from creating a UPSC selection system for SC/HC judges.
  • Changing higher-court appointment rules involves Articles 124/217 and constitutional requirements; a proposal does not prove all those Articles must be amended simply to create district-level AIJS.
  • Common recruitment/training may widen access and consistency; concerns include federal judicial administration, local language, diversity and independence. Competitive exams alone do not eliminate corruption or guarantee executive insulation.

May 2025: accountability and constitutional supremacy

  • Editorial responds to Vice-President Jagdeep Dhankhar's criticism of assent-related judicial directions and the “super Parliament” label. Constitutional supremacy, rather than unqualified supremacy of any organ, frames the debate; political remarks are not constitutional rulings.
  • L. Chandra Kumar (1997) affirms constitutional judicial review and its basic-structure status. Independence is compatible with reasoned decisions, appellate/review remedies, fair criticism and accountability; judicial office does not confer blanket exemption from law.
  • Article 124(4): Supreme Court judge removal for proved misbehaviour or incapacity, by Presidential order after an address of each House supported by a majority of its total membership and at least two-thirds of members present and voting in the same session. Article 217 links High Court judge removal to the corresponding constitutional process. “Impeachment” is common shorthand; ordinary disagreement with a judgment is not proved misbehaviour.
  • Parliament can cure a judgment's legal basis through constitutionally valid legislation; it cannot simply declare a binding judgment wrong or evade fundamental rights/basic structure. Review of a decision differs from disciplinary accountability of its author.
  • Article142 permits complete justice in pending causes, within constitutional limits; it is not unrestricted legislative authority. Article50 specifically calls for separation of judiciary from executive in State public services, rather than comprehensively codifying all branches' separation.
  • Bill-assent delay raises democratic accountability and institutional-boundary questions; the April Tamil Nadu judgment and later clarification belong in the common Governor topic. This editorial's defence of intervention should not erase those legal developments.
  • Mains balance: independent review prevents arbitrary executive/legislative conduct; transparent reasons, restraint, predictable procedure and respectful institutional dialogue sustain legitimacy. Sweeping assertions that every intervention is constitutional, or that courts have no limits, obscure case-specific legal scrutiny.

August 2025: judicial discipline and the roster

  • Source reports 4 August SC directions pairing Allahabad HC Justice Prashant Kumar with a senior judge and withholding criminal cases; 8 August modification withdrew those roster directions after institutional objections and the CJI's letter. Treat the withdrawn directions as procedural history.
  • HC Chief Justice is master of the roster: allocates judicial business and constitutes benches. Appellate hierarchy does not make the SC the HC's administrative superior; Article 227 gives HCs superintendence over courts/tribunals within their territory, not SC superintendence over HCs.
  • Source cites Tirupati Balaji Developers (2004) and Mayavaram Financial Corporation (1991) on autonomy/allocation. Correcting legal error through appeal/review differs from controlling a judge's future administrative roster; institutional discipline needs reasons, proportionality and respect for constitutional allocation of authority.
  • Practice C (1 and 3); option A's printed “1 only 2” is malformed, and Article 227 statement is false.

October 2025: women’s representation and AIJS debate

  • Editorial contrasts roughly 14% women in High Courts and 38% in subordinate judiciary, discussing the India Justice Report 2025. Its Supreme Court claim “one of 34 =3.1%” is arithmetically inconsistent (one of 34≈2.9%) and mixes a dated working-strength snapshot with sanctioned strength; do not memorise it as a verified report table.
  • Barriers include opaque networks, unequal career opportunities, care responsibilities, lack of mentorship, hostile workplaces and inadequate court amenities. A more representative bench can improve trust and bring wider experience; no recruitment route automatically guarantees merit, independence or equality.
  • The editorial proposes UPSC-style national recruitment, reservations, common training and judicial supervision. Article 312 AIJS is at district-judge level and above within the judicial service, excludes inferior posts, and is not a new exam route replacing SC/HC appointment provisions. It requires the special Rajya Sabha resolution (at least two-thirds present and voting) and Parliamentary law; an editorial proposal does not establish an operational service. Department of Justice clarification.
  • Evaluate federal/local-language needs, judicial control, transparent eligibility and progression, social inclusion and executive influence. Combine fair recruitment/elevation criteria with childcare, separate facilities, safety, mentoring and gender-sensitivity training. Source's UPSC candidate/IPS figures illustrate the argument but do not prove a causal effect of exams on judicial diversity.
  • Practice answer: assess AIJS's possible transparency/inclusion gains against constitutional scope, federalism, language and independence; the printed 150-word/10-mark question has no PYQ year.

November 2025: subordinate-court time and procedure

  • Editorial cites 4.69 crore district-court pending cases as a dated NJDG snapshot. It links stagnation to procedural workload, training gaps and execution delays; broad claims that all newly recruited judges are incompetent are unsupported.
  • Proposed district administrative court/registry arrangement would consolidate routine calling of cases, summons, filings and suitable procedural work, prepare next-day lists in advance, and release trial judges for evidence, argument and judgments. This is a reform proposal, not an already mandated national institution; judicial tasks and parties' procedural rights require lawful allocation.
  • Practical High Court-linked induction, observed hearings, judgment-writing and continuing education complement experience-based recruitment. The claim that past recruitment universally required ten years' practice is not a reliable nationwide legal rule; distinguish service/cadre requirements from the author's preference.
  • Commercial Courts Act section 12A: Patil Automation v Rakheja Engineers (2022) treats pre-institution mediation as mandatory for covered suits not contemplating urgent interim relief; non-compliant plaints face rejection under Order VII rule 11, subject to the judgment's temporal rules. Ordinary exchanged legal notices do not by themselves satisfy mediation or prove it always delays settlement. Supreme Court clarification, 15 May 2025.
  • Hindu Marriage Act section 13B(2): six-month second-motion interval is directory and may be waived in justified cases under Amardeep Singh v Harveen Kaur (2017); distinguish it from the separate section 13B(1) one-year separation requirement. False declarations are not a legitimate reform instrument. Supreme Court judgment.
  • Focus on timely execution (Order XXI CPC), trained support staff, intelligible procedure and effective case management. Evaluate disposal quality, access and fairness alongside speed; mediation and adjudication serve complementary roles.

November 2025: dignified administrative language

  • A report of the Supreme Court’s Centre for Research and Planning, circulated to High Courts during CJI B. R. Gavai’s final days, recommends replacing archaic, colonial and caste-loaded court staff titles. Recommendations should not be described as already implemented uniformly by all courts.
  • It calls hierarchical/dehumanising terminology a “grammar of inequality”: language can preserve feudal/caste assumptions and turn persons into functional objects, undermining the dignity courts are expected to protect. Link to Articles 14, 15, 17 and 21 (equality, non-discrimination, abolition of untouchability and dignity).
  • Suggested replacements: Halalkhor/Scavenger → Sanitation Assistant; Dhobi → Laundry Operator; Coolie → Freight Assistant; Cycle Sawar → Logistics Assistant; Basta Bardar → Document Handler; Bundle Lifter → Material Coordinator; Masalchi → Kitchen Assistant; Malan → Horticulture Attendant; Chowkidar → Security Assistant. Old terms appear here only to explain the recommended reform.
  • Reform should align institutional practice with constitutional commitments: dignified titles, respectful workplace language and substantive anti-discrimination measures. Renaming is a useful symbolic/institutional step, but alone does not end caste exclusion or improve working conditions.
