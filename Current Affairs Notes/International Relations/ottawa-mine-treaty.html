International Relations

Ottawa Convention and humanitarian disarmament

Editable notes

Treaty essentials

  • Ottawa Convention / Anti-Personnel Mine Ban Convention: adopted at Oslo in September 1997, opened for signature at Ottawa; effective 1 March 1999. India, Russia, China and the US are not parties.
  • Bans anti-personnel mines: use, development, production, acquisition, stockpiling and transfer, including assistance with prohibited activities. It does not ban every anti-vehicle mine or cover cluster munitions under the same treaty.
  • Four years: destroy stocks, apart from the minimum retained for permitted detection/clearance/destruction training. Ten years: clear mined areas under jurisdiction/control; authorised extensions are possible. Both deadlines run from entry into force for that State, not signature.
  • Article 6 covers international cooperation and victim assistance. Article 20: withdrawal requires notice/reasons; normally effective six months after depositary receipt. If the State is then engaged in armed conflict, effectiveness waits until the conflict ends. Other international-law duties continue. UN treaty text.

March–April 2025: security versus civilian protection

  • Poland and the Baltic states—Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania—announced plans in March; Finland followed in early April. These were announcements, not completed withdrawals at the 6 April news date. Governments cited Russia-related threats and defensive flexibility. Lithuanian Defence Ministry.
  • Mines persist after fighting: casualties, inaccessible farmland, delayed return/reconstruction and costly clearance. The ICRC's 4 April 2025 statement reported over 80% of victims were civilians. Norway remained committed in the source-period discussion. ICRC statement.
  • Separate treaty: Convention on Cluster Munitions, 2008. Lithuania's withdrawal already became effective 6 March 2025; it was not merely under consideration. Unexploded submunitions create a long-term civilian hazard. ICRC.

Mains revision

  • Weigh asserted deterrence benefits against indiscriminate harm, treaty credibility, post-conflict livelihoods and victim-care costs. Withdrawal does not remove humanitarian-law obligations.
  • Priorities: sustained clearance funding, rehabilitation/social inclusion, risk education, security dialogue and compliant defensive alternatives. Self-deactivation does not make an otherwise prohibited anti-personnel mine lawful under this Convention. Foreign-aid interruptions can slow clearance; the source's claim that a 2025 review restarted programmes in 2024 is chronologically inconsistent.

February 2026: Poland withdrawal takes effect

  • Poland deposited withdrawal notice 20 August 2025, effective 20 February 2026; describing it as merely considering withdrawal is outdated by this issue. UN depositary action.
  • Security concerns about Russia motivate defensive flexibility, while civilian casualties, contaminated land, clearance costs and treaty credibility underpin humanitarian objections. Withdrawal does not erase other international humanitarian law obligations.
  • Do not memorise the issue's 164 parties/33 signatories as a February 2026 membership total; withdrawals make counts time-sensitive. The four-year stockpile/ten-year clearance rules and India non-party status are already covered; complete 2020 international-agreement PYQ is already in the common bank.
