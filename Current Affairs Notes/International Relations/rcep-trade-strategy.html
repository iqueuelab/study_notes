RCEP and India’s selective trade-agreement strategy

Editable notes

January 2026: “RCEP minus China” interpretation

  • RCEP was signed 2020, with 15 original parties: ten then-ASEAN members plus China, Japan, South Korea, Australia, New Zealand; about 30% global GDP/population is the standard rounded signing-era scale. India did not join in 2019, citing unresolved safeguards/import, dairy/agriculture and manufacturing concerns.
  • “RCEP minus China” describes India's separate agreements with ASEAN, Japan, Korea and Australia plus New Zealand negotiations concluded 22 December 2025. It is an analytical label, not a new treaty or proof India secured the same integrated rules-of-origin/services/supply-chain benefits as RCEP. New Zealand government, December announcement.
  • At this issue date the NZ FTA was concluded, not yet signed/in force. Therefore “operational FTAs with all 14 other RCEP partners” overstates the position. Its promised zero-duty access for Indian exports and US$20bn investment over 15 years repeat the existing India–NZ topic; they are prospective commitments, not immediate realised benefits.
  • AITIGA (goods, 2010), Korea CEPA (2010), Japan CEPA (2011), Australia ECTA (2022) are key milestones. With China, APTA offers limited preferences, not a comprehensive bilateral FTA; it does not guarantee no import surges. APTA is not the only rule affecting bilateral trade—WTO obligations also matter.
  • Separate bilateral agreements preserve tailored negotiations but add compliance complexity; tariff preferences need credible origin checks, competitive production, logistics, skills and safeguards. Trade deficits alone do not prove an FTA failed, while indirect Chinese inputs are not automatically illegal transshipment.
  • 2016 PYQ answer (b) ASEAN; already present in common IR bank, so preserve the existing question without duplication.
