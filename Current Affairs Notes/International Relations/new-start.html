New START and nuclear arms control

Editable notes

5 February 2026: expiry

  • New START expired on 5 February 2026. The source's earlier description “legally exists but not operational” is stale for the 11 February issue. Signed 2010, effective 2011, extended 2021 for five years; Russia announced suspension in 2023, impairing verification. US Congressional Research Service, 6 February 2026.
  • Limits per side: 700 deployed ICBMs/SLBMs/nuclear-capable heavy bombers; 1,550 treaty-accounted deployed strategic warheads; 800 deployed/non-deployed launchers and heavy bombers. Treaty counting is not an inventory of every warhead; each deployed heavy bomber counts as one warhead. Verification used inspections, notifications and data exchange.
  • START I (1991; effective 1994), SORT/Moscow Treaty (2002) and New START are successive strategic-arms frameworks. START I was not the first nuclear agreement ever to require destruction: INF (1987) had already eliminated an intermediate-range missile class.
  • Future negotiations face Ukraine-war distrust, missile defence, conventional strategic strike, new delivery systems and China's expanding arsenal. Avangard on covered ICBMs was within New START; not every hypersonic or tactical system was covered. Expiry increases uncertainty; the editorial's proposed inclusive framework is an argument, not an achieved successor treaty.

Other regimes and scope

  • NPT: opened 1968, effective 1970—non-proliferation, peaceful use and disarmament; five recognised nuclear-weapon States are US, Russia, UK, France, China, under its pre-1967 test criterion. India is not a party.
  • CTBT (1996) prohibits nuclear test explosions but has not entered into force. TPNW (2017; effective 2021) bans specified nuclear-weapon activities for parties; nuclear-armed States have not joined. Neither automatically replaces New START's bilateral verification/caps.
  • 2011 PYQ: New START is the US–Russia bilateral strategic nuclear arms reduction treaty—(a).
