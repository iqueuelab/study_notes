Institutional distinctions

  • Multilateral institutions coordinate health, climate, peace, human rights and trade; unilateral withdrawals, funding cuts and sanctions can weaken collective capacity.
  • In March 2025, US WHO/Paris exit processes and institutional disengagement had different legal timelines. A withdrawal instruction is not instant completion of every treaty procedure.
  • The US was not a Rome Statute party, so ICC sanctions are not withdrawal from ICC membership. UN Human Rights Council disengagement also differs from leaving the UN.
  • A proposed US DEFUND Bill is not enacted withdrawal. UN headquarters, contributions and peacekeeping effects are scenarios conditional on actual legal action.
  • WTO Appellate Body vacancies impede appeals; this does not mean all WTO rules or every dispute procedure vanished.
  • Smoot–Hawley tariffs (1930) illustrate escalation risks, but cannot alone explain the Great Depression or World War II.
  • India can advocate institutional reform, peaceful dispute settlement, climate/health cooperation and representative global governance; US retreat does not automatically secure India a permanent UNSC seat.

UN at 80: purpose, performance and reform

  • 24 October 1945 marks entry into force of the UN Charter; 2025 is its 80th anniversary. Peace and security, human dignity, development and cooperation remain its purposes. Cold War rivalry later constrained cooperation; calling the world already fully bipolar at the moment of founding oversimplifies the transition after WWII.
  • Peacekeeping, humanitarian assistance (UNHCR, UNICEF, WFP), standard-setting and the 2015–2030 SDGs demonstrate continuing value. Rwanda genocide (1994) and Srebrenica massacre (1995) illustrate failures of protection; Namibia and Timor-Leste illustrate contributions to independence and peacebuilding. Performance depends on mandates, resources and member-state action.
  • Correct two source errors: the G4 is India, Brazil, Germany and Japan, excluding South Africa; the Security Council did undergo membership reform. Charter amendments effective 31 August 1965 expanded it 11 → 15, with ten elected rather than six elected seats, and raised required affirmative votes 7 → 9. UN Charter amendments.
  • P5: China, France, Russia, UK, US; veto applies to substantive Council decisions, not every UN action or procedural vote. The present Council has five permanent and ten elected members; India has served repeated elected terms. Practice statements 1 and 3 are correct, 2 false.
  • Veto deadlock, underrepresentation, funding uncertainty, bureaucracy and politicisation constrain effectiveness. India links representative reform and African representation with its demographic weight, development role and peacekeeping contribution; advocacy does not itself confer permanent membership.
  • Mains framework: preserve convening/humanitarian/normative capacity → reform representation and accountability → realistic mandates, timely funding and field capacity → collective response to climate, pandemics and cyber threats. January 2026: mercantilism and India's preparedness

  • Editorial interprets greater tariff coercion, strategic supply chains and weakened cooperation as a shift towards mercantilism: treating trade surpluses and domestic production as instruments of national power. Its “end of globalisation” is a political argument, not proof cross-border trade, investment or multilateral institutions have ended.
  • Proposed causes: uneven distribution of gains, capital returns outpacing wages, regional manufacturing dislocation, populism and China's state-led integration into world markets. These are analytical explanations; avoid attributing every social or geopolitical change to a single cause.
  • India needs productive capacity and human development to convert demographic potential into broadly shared growth. Health, education, manufacturing capabilities, administrative capacity and social cohesion strengthen bargaining power; rhetoric alone cannot substitute for delivery.
  • DPI (Aadhaar/UPI), renewable energy, services and decentralised governance are potential strengths. Combine diversified partnerships and strategic autonomy with domestic capability, inclusive institutions and practical climate/financial cooperation; a world of greater rivalry still leaves space for collective rules.
