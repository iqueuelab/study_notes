# Colonial wealth extraction and inequality

## Oxfam: Takers, Not Makers (2025)
- Report links inherited fortunes, colonial exploitation and contemporary global inequality; arguments/statistics belong to the **2025 report**, not a neutral audited historical cash ledger.
- Estimate of India→UK extraction **1765–1900: US$64.82 trillion**; **US$33.8 trillion**, roughly **52%**, attributed to Britain's richest **10%**.
- **Method matters**: estimates of historical drain are **cumulated to 2020** in dollar terms; not simply the amount actually transferred in nineteenth-century dollars. Methodology contrasts **US$1.925 trillion cumulated to 1947**. Results depend on assumptions and compounding. [Oxfam methodology](https://www.oxfamfrance.org/app/uploads/2025/01/Oxfam-Davos-2025-Methodology-Note.pdf).
- Illustration that elite share could cover London in £50 notes nearly four times is an analogy, not an additional independent measurement. [Oxfam report summary](https://laos.oxfam.org/latest/publications/takers-not-makers).
## Mechanisms and historical connection
- Colonial revenue extraction, land/resource control, trade rules and labour exploitation transferred surplus abroad; raw-material exports and unequal market access constrained domestic development.
- **East India Company** combined commercial power and political coercion; important precursor to multinational networks, without treating all modern corporations as legally identical colonial rulers.
- Connect to **drain-of-wealth critique**: domestic surplus used abroad rather than reinvested locally. Different historical scholars' estimates must retain their period/method labels.
## Contemporary debate
- Oxfam links Global South labour/resource extraction to profits accruing in Global North; distinguish historical influence from assuming every present transaction has the same colonial mechanism.
- Proposed responses: effective progressive/wealth taxation, fairer labour/trade rules, debt relief, institutional reform, acknowledgement/reparations.
- Mains: explain mechanisms and distributional effects, assess methodology, and compare remedies for feasibility and equity; do not present reparations proposals as enacted policy.
