Indian Economy

Fiscal deficit, defence spending and fiscal space

Editable notes

Correct deficit arithmetic

  • Fiscal deficit = total expenditure − (revenue receipts + non-debt capital receipts); indicates net financing requirement. PDF's “expenditure minus revenue receipts” formula omits loan recoveries/disinvestment and other non-debt capital receipts.
TermMeaning
Revenue receiptsTaxes net to Centre plus non-tax revenue, including dividends/fees
Capital receiptsInclude borrowings and non-debt receipts; borrowings finance rather than reduce fiscal deficit
Revenue deficitRevenue expenditure minus revenue receipts
Primary deficitFiscal deficit minus interest payments
  • Expressing deficits as % GDP supports scale comparisons; a ratio can change with either rupee deficit or nominal GDP. Deficit is an annual flow; public debt a stock.
  • Drivers: expenditure/income choices, tax buoyancy, subsidies, interest costs, slowdowns and shocks. Deficit-financed productive investment differs from recurrent spending; assess growth/interest and expenditure quality, not one ratio alone.

Defence fiscal-space debate: May 2025

  • FY2025–26 Budget Estimate fiscal-deficit target: 4.4% GDP, versus 4.8% FY2024–25 Revised Estimate. Target ≠ final outturn. FRBM Act 2003 and fiscal statements provide the framework; the historic 3% ambition is not the operative 2025–26 Budget target. Budget fiscal statement.
  • Source reports Defence Ministry expected to seek ~₹50,000 crore additional allocation through Supplementary Demands for Grants. Expectation is not an approved/appropriated addition or actual spending; parliamentary authorisation and financing follow.
  • Article argues larger RBI surplus, lower oil prices, tax receipts and reprioritisation could absorb extra defence spending while maintaining target. This is a conditional fiscal assessment, not a guarantee; lower oil prices affect subsidy/import/inflation channels but do not automatically yield equal budget savings.
  • RBI FY2023–24 surplus ₹2,10,874 crore, about 141% above ₹87,416 crore, was transferred/received in FY2024–25. The source conflates surplus year with government-receipt year. A higher next transfer was still an expectation at the issue date, not the later Board payout already realised. CAG government-receipt table.
  • More non-tax revenue can reduce borrowing or finance spending; RBI risk provisions must remain prudent (see common economic-capital topic). Spending cuts across revenue heads can affect welfare/services; “revenue” does not automatically mean wasteful.

Historical comparison: avoid causal/date errors

  • 1971 war, Kargil 1999, Parliament attack 2001, Mumbai 2008, Uri 2016, Pulwama 2019 belong to different fiscal years/economic settings. PDF wrongly associates 2000–01/2001–02 deficit figures with Kargil.
  • The 2008 global crisis and later stimulus, revenue slowdown and accounting changes must be separated from security costs. COVID affected the final days of FY2019–20 and especially FY2020–21; do not explain the entire earlier year's deficit solely by pandemic response.
  • Source historic 3→3.45% and 5.3→6.1% figures omit a reconciled official series; avoid treating them as causal estimates of the cost of war. Deficits can rise/fall for many simultaneous reasons.
  • Mains: spending need → revenue/financing options → trade-offs for welfare/capex → transparent supplementary appropriations and credible debt/deficit monitoring. Macroeconomic tailwinds help but do not eliminate fiscal risk.

30 May 2025 provisional FY 2024–25 outturn

  • Embedded clipping reports fiscal deficit ₹15.77 lakh crore/4.8%GDP, matching revised target; total non-borrowing receipts ₹30.78 lakh crore, 97.8%RE; total spending ₹46.55 lakh crore, 97.8%RE. These are provisional fiscal accounts with the then GDP denominator, not audited final accounts.
  • Corporation-tax receipts ₹9.87 lakh crore, around 0.7% above RE; income-tax ₹11.83 lakh crore, around 6% below RE. Disinvestment ₹10,131.32 crore, non-debt capital receipts 52.1%RE: different receipt headings must not be conflated.
  • FY 2025–26 target 4.4% is separate from achieved/provisional FY 25 ratio. Repeated defence ~₹50,000 crore supplementary request remains an expectation, not parliamentary approval.
  • Repeated formula is corrected in common core: expenditure − revenue receipts − non-debt capital receipts; source omitted non-debt receipts. Revenue deficit/primary deficit differ.
  • Repeated article calls higher RBI transfer prospective, reflecting an earlier report, not necessarily status as at 31 May. Do not redate its historical₹2.1 lakh-crore surplus or claim every oil-price reduction translates into budget saving. Fiscal flexibility remains conditional on receipts, spending and buffer prudence.
  • Historical source again misdates Kargil 1999 to 2000–02; macroeconomic crises/accounting changes confound security-cost comparisons. Capital investment and necessary revenue services both have value; assess opportunity costs and transparent appropriations.

March 2026: supplementary grants and stabilisation buffer

  • The issue reports Lok Sabha approval of the second batch of Supplementary Demands for Grants, FY2025–26: ₹2,81,289.26 crore gross additional provision, ₹80,145.71 crore offset by savings/enhanced receipts or recoveries, and ₹2,01,142.96 crore net cash outgo, across 61 grants, plus ₹59 lakh token provisions for reappropriation. Gross authorisation is not identical to fresh cash spending or realised expenditure.
  • Economic Stabilisation Fund: ₹57,381 crore proposed/provided through the supplementary package, to create fiscal headroom against global headwinds, oil/energy shocks, supply disruption and sectoral pressures. This is a Union fiscal provision, not an RBI-managed monetary facility. The issue's ₹100/barrel example illustrates a shock, not an automatic fund-release trigger. DEA supplementary-grant records.
  • Article 115 provides supplementary/additional/excess grants where an authorised amount is insufficient, a new service arises, or spending exceeds authorisation; parliamentary grant voting and appropriation stages differ. Lok Sabha passage alone is not proof of completed presidential assent or money already spent.
  • FM's assurance that FY2025–26 fiscal-deficit target 4.4% GDP would be met is a contemporaneous commitment, not final fiscal outturn. The existing formula remains expenditure − revenue receipts − non-debt capital receipts; supplementary provision need not increase deficit one-for-one when savings/receipts offset it.
