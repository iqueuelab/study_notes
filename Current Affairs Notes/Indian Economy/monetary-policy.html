Indian Economy

Monetary Policy Committee and policy instruments

Editable notes

MPC: statutory role

  • Section 45ZB, RBI Act 1934, as amended in 2016: Central Government constitutes a six-member MPC; its policy-rate decision binds RBI. Price stability is the primary objective while keeping growth in mind.
  • Three RBI members: Governor (ex officio chair), Deputy Governor in charge of monetary policy, and an RBI officer nominated by the Central Board. Three external members: Central Government appointees with ability, integrity and expertise in economics/banking/finance/monetary policy.
  • Majority voting; Governor has a casting vote on a tie. Quorum four, including Governor or, in the Governor's absence, the monetary-policy Deputy Governor. External members have four-year terms without reappointment. Indian Economic Service: MPC.

Correct reserve definitions

  • CRR: required average cash balance with RBI as share of NDTL, generally non-interest-bearing. NDTL accounting is broader than simply adding savings/current/fixed deposits; applicable netting/exclusions matter.
  • RRBs are not generally excluded from CRR; bank-category rules apply. NBFC regulation differs, but that does not make every financial intermediary subject to the same reserve system.
  • CRR is a ratio, not the “reference/base lending rate”; repo, external benchmarks, MCLR/base-rate frameworks are different. Higher CRR ordinarily reduces lendable liquidity, holding other factors constant; it cannot mechanically guarantee lower inflation or deposit safety.
  • SLR: bank-held eligible liquid assets—cash, gold and approved securities—against NDTL; differs from CRR cash at RBI. Government securities earn returns and finance the issuer; SLR does not require every eligible asset to be a government bond.
  • 2010 PYQ(a): CRR increase →commercial banks have less money to lend. 2010 PYQ(d): SLR is the intended mechanism for banks' credit to government through government-securities holdings; LAF provides bank–RBI liquidity, not this government-credit mechanism.

Expansionary policy and transmission

  • Fiscal expansion: tax cuts/public spending support disposable income and demand. Monetary easing: lower policy rates/liquidity can lower borrowing costs. Reinforcement is possible, but the effects have lags and depend on bank transmission, credit demand, confidence and productive capacity.
  • Income-tax relief in Budget2025 and RBI easing form the issue's context. Its statement that RBI cut rates twice in2025 is stale by June23: February,April andJune cuts had occurred; the corrected June6 decision is above.
  • Weak credit/private investment may persist if firms/households save, deleverage or expect poor demand. Do not infer the entire economy failed to grow from this editorial description.
  • If demand later outstrips supply, inflation may rise; lower-than-expected tax receipts can constrain fiscal space. These are conditional risks, not inevitable deficit increases or automatic welfare cuts.

5 December 2025 decision, reported in the 6 December issue

  • MPC cut repo 25 basis points, 5.50% → 5.25%; PDF headline “26 basis points” is a typo. SDF 5.00%; MSF/Bank Rate 5.50%; neutral stance. These are dated decisions, not permanent rate definitions. RBI resolution.
  • Growth/inflation context: real GDP Q2 FY2025–26 growth 8.2%; source separately cites 1.7% inflation without a clear matching period and October's roughly 0.3%; do not treat both as the same current monthly reading. The Governor's “Goldilocks” characterisation means strong growth with low inflation, not absence of future price or external risks.
  • The 4% CPI target with 2–6% tolerance band is the issue-era framework. A single monthly reading below 2% does not itself trigger the statutory failure procedure or automatically require a rate cut. Monetary easing depends on the assessed outlook and transmission.
  • Lower funding costs can reduce lending/deposit rates and support demand, with lags and bank/borrower constraints. The PDF's claim that low inflation allows cuts “without risking a rise in prices” is too absolute.
  • Source reports cumulative 125 bps easing during 2025.
