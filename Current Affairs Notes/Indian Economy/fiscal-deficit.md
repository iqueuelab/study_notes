# Fiscal deficit, defence spending and fiscal space

## Correct deficit arithmetic
- **Fiscal deficit = total expenditure − (revenue receipts + non-debt capital receipts)**; indicates net financing requirement. PDF's “expenditure minus revenue receipts” formula omits **loan recoveries/disinvestment and other non-debt capital receipts**.
| Term | Meaning |
|---|---|
| Revenue receipts | Taxes net to Centre plus non-tax revenue, including dividends/fees |
| Capital receipts | Include borrowings and non-debt receipts; borrowings finance rather than reduce fiscal deficit |
| Revenue deficit | Revenue expenditure minus revenue receipts |
| Primary deficit | Fiscal deficit minus interest payments |
- Expressing deficits as **% GDP** supports scale comparisons; a ratio can change with either rupee deficit or nominal GDP. Deficit is an annual flow; public debt a stock.
- Drivers: expenditure/income choices, tax buoyancy, subsidies, interest costs, slowdowns and shocks. Deficit-financed productive investment differs from recurrent spending; assess growth/interest and expenditure quality, not one ratio alone.

## Defence fiscal-space debate: May 2025
- **FY2025–26 Budget Estimate fiscal-deficit target: 4.4% GDP**, versus **4.8% FY2024–25 Revised Estimate**. Target ≠ final outturn. **FRBM Act 2003** and fiscal statements provide the framework; the historic 3% ambition is not the operative 2025–26 Budget target. [Budget fiscal statement](https://www.indiabudget.gov.in/budget2025-26/doc/frbm1.pdf).
- Source reports Defence Ministry **expected to seek ~₹50,000 crore additional allocation through Supplementary Demands for Grants**. Expectation is not an approved/appropriated addition or actual spending; parliamentary authorisation and financing follow.
- Article argues larger RBI surplus, lower oil prices, tax receipts and reprioritisation could absorb extra defence spending while maintaining target. This is a **conditional fiscal assessment**, not a guarantee; lower oil prices affect subsidy/import/inflation channels but do not automatically yield equal budget savings.
- RBI **FY2023–24 surplus ₹2,10,874 crore**, about **141% above ₹87,416 crore**, was transferred/received in **FY2024–25**. The source conflates surplus year with government-receipt year. A higher next transfer was still an expectation at the issue date, not the later Board payout already realised. [CAG government-receipt table](https://saiindia.gov.in/uploads/download_audit_report/2026/6of2026UGEng-069ce496f1158e0.22682438.pdf).
- More non-tax revenue can reduce borrowing or finance spending; RBI risk provisions must remain prudent (see common economic-capital topic). Spending cuts across revenue heads can affect welfare/services; “revenue” does not automatically mean wasteful.

## Historical comparison: avoid causal/date errors
- **1971 war**, **Kargil 1999**, **Parliament attack 2001**, **Mumbai 2008**, **Uri 2016**, **Pulwama 2019** belong to different fiscal years/economic settings. PDF wrongly associates **2000–01/2001–02** deficit figures with Kargil.
- The **2008 global crisis** and later stimulus, revenue slowdown and accounting changes must be separated from security costs. COVID affected the final days of **FY2019–20** and especially **FY2020–21**; do not explain the entire earlier year's deficit solely by pandemic response.
- Source historic **3→3.45%** and **5.3→6.1%** figures omit a reconciled official series; avoid treating them as causal estimates of the cost of war. Deficits can rise/fall for many simultaneous reasons.
- Mains: spending need → revenue/financing options → trade-offs for welfare/capex → transparent supplementary appropriations and credible debt/deficit monitoring. Macroeconomic tailwinds help but do not eliminate fiscal risk.
