## Mechanism and trade-offs
- **Depreciation = more rupees for one dollar**. Importers face higher rupee costs; exporters may gain rupee receipts but imported inputs, hedging and weak foreign demand can offset gains.
- Oil/import dependence → possible imported inflation and external-balance pressure; borrowers with unhedged foreign-currency debt face greater repayment burden.
- Indian exchange rate is market determined with RBI volatility management; source **“free-floating”** is too absolute. Intervention can smooth disorderly moves, not guarantee a specific rate indefinitely.
- Source Brent **US$76.74/bbl** and negative rupee outlook are dated observations/forecasts. US tariff announcements and later pauses must be read chronologically.

## January 2026: diplomacy and capital-flow pressure
- Editorial argues that US tariff/geopolitical uncertainty and foreign capital outflows help explain rupee weakness despite relatively strong domestic indicators. It cites **April–December net flows +US$10.6bn (2024) versus −US$3.9bn (2025)**; retain these as its dated comparison, not a complete balance-of-payments account or proof of a single cause.
- **FY2025–26 growth 7.4%** is an estimate in this source period; its low CPI/CAD figures require their own reference periods, so should not be read as simultaneous real-time annual outcomes. The source's categorical “only diplomacy” remedy is an opinion: rates also respond to oil, relative interest rates, global dollar demand and risk expectations.
- Confidence loss → capital outflow → depreciation expectations → further risk aversion is a possible feedback loop. Diplomatic/trade clarity may ease uncertainty, while RBI intervention can moderate disorderly volatility without assuring appreciation or a fixed rate.
- Imported inputs dilute export-price advantages and oil/essential imports become costlier. **REER incorporates relative prices and trade-weighted exchange rates**; it does not cease to matter when inflation is low. Devaluation does not require domestic inflation to exceed global inflation.
- **2021 PYQ: (a), statement 1 only**. In its standard price-competitiveness setting, devaluation lowers foreign-currency export prices; it reduces the foreign value of domestic currency and does not necessarily improve the trade balance. Import dependence, demand elasticities and adjustment lags (**J-curve**) matter; the exam simplification is not a universal promise of export-volume gains.
