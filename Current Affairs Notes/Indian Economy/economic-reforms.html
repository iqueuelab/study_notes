Indian Economy

Manmohan Singh and economic liberalisation

Editable notes

Career and reform context

  • Finance Minister 1991-96, Prime Minister 2004-14; Cambridge economics Tripos 1957, Oxford DPhil 1962; earlier RBI Governor and Planning Commission Deputy Chairman.
  • 1991 balance-of-payments crisis triggered stabilisation and structural reform: rupee devaluation, fiscal discipline, lower trade barriers, industrial delicensing and greater scope for private/foreign investment. These were government-wide reforms, not the actions of one office alone.

Economic approach

  • Markets with an enabling state; inclusive growth rather than indiscriminate punitive redistribution; exports and global integration; autonomy/accountability for PSUs.
  • Human capital: education, health, women's literacy, nutrition and sanitation support productivity and opportunity.

Effects and debate

  • Greater competition, FDI and integration helped services/IT expansion and later growth. Remaining concerns: unequal gains, implementation/institutional capacity, infrastructure and governance.
  • Coalition-era criticism included corruption allegations and policy paralysis; allegations are not findings of personal guilt.
  • Source claims second-term growth averaged over 8% without a series/period; do not memorise this unsupported average.

Mains plan

  • Crisis → stabilisation measures → industrial/trade/financial reforms → growth and integration → inequality, governance and institution-building challenges.

Embedded editorial: growth, work and welfare

  • Santosh Mehrotra's retrospective highlights five channels: higher savings/investment, non-farm jobs, rising real wages, rural employment support and poverty reduction.
  • Editorial figures: savings/investment around 23%/24% of GDP in 2003-04, rising towards 35%/38%; average growth 7.8% during 2004-14. These do not establish the PDF's separate 'over 8% in the second term' claim.
  • Jobs narrative: about 7.5 million non-farm jobs annually; construction jobs 26 million (2004) → 54 million (2012); manufacturing 52 → 60 million; agricultural employment fell by about 6.7 crore between 2004-05 and 2017-18.
  • MGNREGA Act, 2005 provided an employer-of-last-resort channel; source links wages and private consumption to poverty decline 138 million fewer poor during 2004-05 to 2011-12.
  • Article contrasts later conditions: annual non-farm job additions 7.5 million (2004-13) → 2.9 million (2013-19); youth unemployment 6% (2011-12) → 12% (2022-23); graduate unemployment about 29%, postgraduate 33%. These are the author's figures with varying datasets/periods, not one harmonised series.
  • Manufacturing's GVA share reportedly reached 17% earlier and fell to 13% in 2022; manufacturing jobs 60 million (2012), 56 million (2019), 62.9 million (2022). Merchandise exports: $77 billion (2004), $323 billion (2014), $454 billion (2024).
  • Unpaid-family-work concern: share 23.8% (2012), 20.9% (2019), 20.2% (2024); counts 111 million (2004), 85.8 million (2012), 107.8 million (2018), 104 million (2023). Include unpaid work when judging labour quality; rising employment alone does not establish decent work.
  • The author's criticism of demonetisation, GST implementation and COVID lockdown concerns informal-sector disruption; evaluate evidence and causal claims rather than memorise opinion as settled fact.

Ethics illustration

  • A companion memoir portrays courteous leadership, humour, humility, tolerance of uncomfortable press questions and delegation to capable colleagues. Use as a qualitative example, not as a measurable policy outcome.
