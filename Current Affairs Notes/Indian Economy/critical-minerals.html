Indian Economy

Critical minerals, processing and supply-chain security

Editable notes

Criticality and uses

  • Critical = economic/strategic importance plus supply vulnerability; lists vary by country/time. Mineral occurrence, inferred resource, economically recoverable reserve, mined output and refining capacity are different.

  • China's extraction and especially processing concentration creates exposure; it does not dominate mining of every mineral. DRC cobalt and Australia/lithium-brine countries illustrate different mining locations.
  • India has rare-earth-bearing monazite and other mineral resources; source's blanket absence of domestic rare earths/cobalt/lithium is misleading. Reasi lithium inferred resource is not an operating mine or proven commercial reserve; unverified Rajasthan reserve headlines require caution.

Response across the value chain

  • Explore and assess geology; efficient permitting with social/environmental safeguards; domestic refining/separation and skilled workforce; diversify contracts/overseas assets; strategic stockpiles; recycling/e-waste recovery and material substitution.
  • 29 January 2025 NCMM approval, 2024–25 to 2030–31: government outlay ₹16, 300 crore, expected PSU/other investment ₹18, 000 crore. Cover exploration → mining → processing → recovery, including overseas partnerships and reserves. Expected investment ≠ public budget already spent. Cabinet release.
  • 30 minerals identified in India's 2023 report; legal schedule categories and that policy list are distinct. FTAs/Quad cooperation can support access, but source's January discussion of EU FTA should not be read as an agreement then in force.

Beach minerals and PYQs

  • Ilmenite / rutile → titanium; monazite → rare earths + thorium, not present in all Indian coastal sand. 2023 titanium (d); 2022 monazite statements 1, 2, 4 (b).
  • Monazite's radioactivity invokes special controls; question's legal position is tied to exam year. Source's lithium question labelled 2023 has no confirmed paper match and is held for review.

Ukraine mineral negotiations

  • February 2025 US–Ukraine minerals arrangement was under negotiation; agreement was signed later, 30 April 2025. A resource deal does not automatically provide a defence guarantee.
  • Distinguish geological resources from economically recoverable reserves, and mining shares from refining shares. Occupied-resource valuations in the source are dated estimates.

Exploration licences: March 2025

  • Auction launch covered 13 exploration blocks; the exploration-licence framework arose from the 2023 amendment to MMDR Act 1957.
  • It covers specified deep-seated minerals, including copper, zinc, platinum-group minerals and diamonds; it is not limited to rare earths.
  • Licence areas may extend up to 1,000 sq km under the framework. Exploration permission is not automatic permission to mine; subsequent leases, environmental approvals and applicable land/forest requirements remain separate.
  • Auctions and revenue-sharing incentives can mobilise risk capital; transparent information, competition, local interests and ecological safeguards determine outcomes.

April 2025: Kazakhstan rare-earth exploration

  • Kuirektykol, Karaganda region, Kazakhstan: reported exploration estimates of roughly one million tonnes of rare-earth metals; the wider Zhana Kazakhstan area's potential was projected at about 20 million tonnes. These are reported resource/potential estimates, not certified economically recoverable reserves or proof of the world's largest operating deposit.
  • Identified elements include cerium, lanthanum, neodymium and yttrium. The reported average concentration was about 700 g/tonne; richer zones reached approximately 0.25%. Grade, recovery, costs and environmental constraints determine commercial viability. Karaganda regional government.
  • Rare earths = 15 lanthanides + scandium + yttrium (17). They need difficult separation/refining; geological occurrence alone does not create a reliable supply chain.
  • Uses include electronics, catalysts, defence systems and permanent magnets in some EV motors/wind turbines. Lithium is not a rare earth; not every battery or renewable technology uses rare earths.
  • Kazakhstan's significance is prospective supply diversification. Foreign investment, processing technology, infrastructure and responsible extraction remain essential; discovery does not immediately displace China's established processing role.
  • India–Kazakhstan uranium/civil-nuclear links provide a base for mineral cooperation. India's coastal monazite resources also contain rare earths; distinguish domestic resources from processing scale and usable supply.

4 April 2025: Chinese rare-earth export controls

  • MOFCOM/Customs Announcement No.18 of 2025, effective 4 April, introduced licensing for specified medium/heavy rare-earth-related items: samarium (Sm), gadolinium (Gd), terbium (Tb), dysprosium (Dy), lutetium (Lu), scandium (Sc), yttrium (Y) and listed forms/products.
  • It was an export-control/permit regime, not a blanket prohibition on all 17 rare earths, nor a measure confined to shipments to India. The source's “2024” date is incorrect. Official announcement.
  • Delayed authorisations and concentrated separation/magnet production expose vehicle, renewable-energy and defence supply chains even where alternative ores exist. India's strategic task includes usable separated materials and components, beyond geological resources alone.
  • Source's coastal example mixes coasts: Kerala is on India's western coast; Tamil Nadu and Odisha include eastern-coast monazite deposits. Avoid treating all domestic reserves as readily available heavy-rare-earth supply.

April 23 revision additions: implementation and applications

  • GSI's 1,200 critical-mineral exploration projects for 2024–25 to 2030–31 are a mission target, not completed discoveries or producing mines. PIB, 9 April 2025.
  • Rare-earth uses also include catalysts, specialist alloys and medical technologies: gadolinium in some MRI contrast agents and particular isotopes of yttrium/lutetium in treatment. Application is element-, isotope- and technology-specific; “all REEs used in all semiconductors/solar panels” is too broad.
  • Source's 2,270-tonne 2023–24 imports and 6% reserve share need defined commodity/year denominators; do not equate mineral/compound import weight with separated elemental supply or economic reserves with total resources.
  • Japan's response to the 2010 supply shock offers a diversification lesson: alternative sourcing, recycling/substitution and stockpiles; source's exact “months-long” buffer is not a universal adequacy rule.

May 2025: germanium supply and export-control chronology

  • Indian Embassy in Beijing told the source it was engaging China through communications/meetings on germanium restrictions; the RTI response identified that particular industry concern. It does not establish successful supply restoration or absence of every rare-earth risk.
  • Germanium (Ge), atomic number 32: metalloid; gallium (Ga), 31: soft post-transition metal, melting near room temperature. Neither is one of the 17 rare earths. Discovery recall: Ge—Clemens Winkler, 1886; Ga—Paul-Émile Lecoq de Boisbaudran, 1875.
  • Ge/GeO2: optical-fibre glass preforms, infrared optics/night vision, specialised semiconductors and space-grade solar cells. GaAs/GaN: RF/power electronics, LEDs, radar/communications; gallium also enters CIGS photovoltaics. Do not infer every ordinary solar module or every green-hydrogen plant requires these materials.
  • Generally recovered as by-products: Ge from zinc-processing and some coal-related sources; Ga from bauxite/alumina processing and some zinc routes. Supply hinges on recovery/refining, not only standalone mines; China's concentration creates vulnerability. Source's crustal abundance/production shares mix definitions/years and should not be memorised as current refining totals.
  • 3 July 2023 announcement, effective 1 August 2023: China introduced licensing for listed Ga/Ge-related exports—not 2022. MOFCOM.
  • 3 December 2024, Announcement 46: tightened listed dual-use exports to US, including in-principle nonpermission for Ga/Ge/antimony/superhard-material exports and stricter graphite review. This is not a late-2023 worldwide ban applying identically to India. Official notice.
  • Source reports Indian import reliance/UAE sourcing and higher costs; transshipment country is not necessarily country of primary production. Its blanket “no domestic production” needs commodity-specific confirmation. Foxconn personnel/equipment delays do not establish officially confirmed retaliation.
  • Diversify suppliers and contracts, assess domestic by-product recovery/recycling, strategic buffers and substitution, and deepen processing skills/technology partnerships. Listed potential supplier countries do not prove immediately available export capacity or signed deals.
  • Existing NCMM approved January 2025 supersedes the source's “critical-minerals strategy merely proposed” wording. Do not retrospectively label a future Quad Critical Minerals Initiative as already launched in May; general Quad/iCET cooperation is separate.

June 2025: rare-earth applications and constraints

  • Europium/terbium/yttrium compounds form display phosphors; lanthanum enters optical glass and petroleum-refining catalysts; cerium enters catalytic converters/polishing and selected water-treatment materials. Lanthanum alloys occur in nickel-metal-hydride batteries, not every EV battery.
  • Mischmetal commonly contains cerium, lanthanum, neodymium and praseodymium; used in alloys/steelmaking. Bastnaesite, xenotime and loparite complement monazite as REE-bearing minerals.
  • Indian deposits are predominantly light rare earths; coastal monazite occurrences are geographically uneven, with inland occurrences too. IREL (India) Ltd under DAE undertakes mining/separation; downstream magnet capacity remains a constraint.
  • The June source reports instructions to prioritise domestic rare-earth needs over sales to Japan. Its China 90–95% production wording confuses stages: mining and separation/processing are distinct; do not memorise it as mined-output share. Its universal 2,000 tonnes toxic waste per tonne REE lacks a defined process/system boundary and is held for review.
  • 2025 PYQ: rare-earth phosphorescent properties explain display applications (a). 2022 monazite: statements 1,2,4 (b); monazite is not distributed throughout all Indian coastal sands.

Rare-earth permanent magnets

  • NdFeB (neodymium–iron–boron): high magnetic strength, widely used in motors/electronics. SmCo (samarium–cobalt): useful where high-temperature stability/corrosion resistance matter. These are permanent-magnet families, not interchangeable names for all rare-earth minerals.
  • Dependence on Chinese separation/processing and magnet manufacture can disrupt auto-component and clean-technology supply chains. Industry proposals for stockpiles, domestic processing and incentives require environmental safeguards and viable production links; a proposal is not an operational stockpile.
  • Medical-imaging examples need qualification: some MRI designs use permanent magnets, while many use superconducting electromagnets. China's processing dominance must not be confused with exclusive worldwide mineral occurrence.
