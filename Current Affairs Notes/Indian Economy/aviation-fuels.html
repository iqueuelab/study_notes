Indian Economy

ATF and sustainable aviation fuel

Editable notes

Fuel and emissions

  • Conventional ATF is jet fuel, generally kerosene-based. SAF uses approved waste/biomass or synthetic pathways and certified blends; arbitrary bio-oils cannot be put directly into aircraft.
  • SAF can reduce life-cycle emissions; combustion still emits CO₂. Feedstock, land/water use, renewable energy, transport and processing determine net savings.
  • Challenges: cost, reliable sustainable feedstock, competing uses, certification, scale and refinery/airport compatibility.
  • India's announced indicative international-flight blending targets: 1% in 2027, 2% in 2028, 5% in 2030, not 1% in 2025. CORSIA concerns international aviation emissions; a target is not evidence all flights already comply. Civil Aviation Ministry.
  • Mains: combine efficient aircraft/operations, credible life-cycle accounting, domestic SAF scale-up and safeguards against food/land competition.

1 June 2025 price report and taxation

  • Clipping reports Delhi ATF reduction ₹2,414.25/kilolitre (2.82%, rounded 3%) to ₹83,072.55/kl, third monthly cut after April/May; city/tax/date-specific price, not a permanent nationwide rate. Commercial 19-kg LPG cut ₹24, a separate product.
  • Jet A-1 is a kerosene hydrocarbon mixture with energy-density, freezing-point and thermal-stability requirements. Source 30–40% airline operating-cost share /VAT up to 30% are variable snapshots. ATF inclusion in GST is a proposal, not proof already operative.

SAF pathways and PYQ traps

  • SAF is not exclusively bio-derived: approved biomass/waste and synthetic power-to-liquid pathways qualify subject to lifecycle/certification criteria. Combustion still emits CO2; savings up to94% /fourfold cost are pathway/market-specific claims, not universal properties.
  • Corn grain → ethanol → alcohol-to-jet (ATJ); HEFA processes oils/fats, not grain directly. Agricultural/wood residues may undergo gasification/Fischer–Tropsch or other routes; sludge-derived fuels need appropriate processing/certification. ICAO pathways.
  • Feedstock eligibility does not establish every batch sustainable: food/land competition, electricity/carbon inputs and traceability matter. Approved blend limits apply; “drop-in” does not mean any unblended alternative liquid suits all existing aircraft.
  • 2024 PYQ: all four feedstocks—agricultural residues, corn grain, wastewater-treatment sludge, wood-mill waste—answer(c). Corn can be an eligible feedstock despite food-security concerns.
  • Indicative 1% in 2027, 2% in 2028, 5% in 2030 targets relate to international flights; the PDF mislabels 2030 as broader aviation. ICAO net zero by 2050 is an aspirational goal; CORSIA has separate implementation rules.
  • ATF also emits NOx/CO/particulates; aviation's climate effects include CO2 and non-CO2 processes. “Ozone depletion” alone is an oversimplified description—NOx chemistry depends on altitude and atmospheric conditions.

Panipat SAF project: planned commercial output

  • August report: IndianOil's Panipat refinery, Haryana, planned commercial SAF output by December 2025, 35,000 tonnes/year capacity, using used cooking oil and ISCC CORSIA certification. This records the announced plan at the issue date, not proof of completion or production already underway.
  • Feedstock collection is easier from organised hotels/restaurants than dispersed eateries/households; traceability, consistent quality, logistics, certification and high cost constrain scale. Source's threefold/fourfold price and up-to-94% emissions-saving claims vary by pathway and market.
  • IOC also explores ethanol → alcohol-to-jet. Corn-derived ethanol belongs to ATJ, not direct corn-to-HEFA. CORSIA certification and an approved fuel specification/blend limit address different requirements; 50% is not a universal limit for every pathway.
  • Retain indicative international-flight targets 1% in 2027, 2% in 2028, 5% in 2030, correcting source table's broader-sector label. Repeat 2024 PYQ: C, all four feedstocks; unyearred practice B, used cooking oil + forestry residues + sludge, excluding coal as renewable SAF feedstock.

November 2025: proposed national SAF policy

  • Civil Aviation Minister Ram Mohan Naidu said a national Sustainable Aviation Fuel policy would be unveiled to support production/blending/adoption. This reports a policy announcement/plan, not an already notified comprehensive policy. Retain the existing indicative international-flight targets 1% in 2027, 2% in 2028, 5% in 2030; PDF's “2030 TBD” is incomplete.
  • Issue estimates: 750 million tonnes annual biomass, 213 million tonnes surplus agricultural residue, 70 million litres annual production capacity, prospective 30 million litres for 1%/62 million for 2% and global demand ~2 million tonnes→183 million by 2040. These are dated minister/source estimates, not independently audited usable SAF supply or guaranteed demand; production capacity, sustainable feedstock and certified delivered fuel differ.
  • Potential: rural residue markets, less open burning/import dependence, refinery/processing jobs and export opportunities. Constraints: feedstock seasonality/collection and competing uses, processing/blending infrastructure, financing, ASTM approval and CORSIA sustainability certification; certification and lifecycle savings must match the pathway.
  • Source 70–80% emissions savings /2–4 times fossil-fuel cost are pathway/market-dependent claims; existing lifecycle and synthetic-SAF qualifications remain. EU ReFuelEU Aviation mandates differ from US incentives—do not state both jurisdictions impose the same national blending mandate. Neither SAF use nor ICAO's aspirational 2050 goal guarantees zero combustion emissions.
