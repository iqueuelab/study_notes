# Lapis lazuli: mineral colour and historic trade

## Composition and uses
- Deep-blue **metamorphic rock**, not a single mineral. **Lazurite** supplies blue colour through sulphur-related species in its structure; calcite creates pale areas and **pyrite** golden sparkles, not actual gold.
- Afghanistan's **Badakhshan** is a famous historic source; deposits also occur in Chile, Russia and elsewhere.
- Lapis ornaments at Indus civilisation sites indicate long-distance exchange much earlier than the source's narrow “as early as 1000 BCE” claim; finding an object in India does not prove local mining.
- Used in ancient jewellery/ritual artefacts and ground for valuable **natural ultramarine pigment** in European painting. Natural and later synthetic ultramarine should not be conflated.
- Colour, authenticity, mineral proportions and provenance affect value; fixed mineral percentages vary between specimens.

## February 2026: blue pigments and cultural value
- Article traces blue's sacred/royal prestige through scarce pigments and long-distance trade, then wider access through manufactured pigments. Lapis from Badakhshan, natural ultramarine in European painting and Indus ornaments illustrate exchanges already covered above.
- **Egyptian blue** is an ancient synthetic copper-calcium-silicate pigment; it is different from lapis-derived ultramarine and modern **cerulean blue**. The source's “ceruleum from copper, limestone and silica” should not be treated as the composition of every blue pigment.
- Newton's **Opticks (1704)** explains light/colour experiments; spectral colour is physical, while seven named divisions and symbolic meanings also reflect classification/culture. Earlier people possessed practical pigment knowledge: science did not begin colour perception in 1704.
- Blue depictions of Krishna/Vishnu and Goethe's emotional interpretation are cultural/artistic readings. Absence of a common colour word in selected Greek texts does not establish ancient Greeks could not physiologically see blue. Lapis mineral percentages and an exact 1000 BCE start of Indian imports remain unsuitable universal facts.
