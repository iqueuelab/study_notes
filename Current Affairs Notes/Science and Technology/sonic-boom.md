# Sonic booms and seismological tracking of space debris

## Sound and supersonic motion
- **Mach number = object speed/local sound speed**. Supersonic motion (**Mach >1**) generates shock waves heard as a sonic boom when they reach an observer. Sound speed in dry air is about **343 m/s at 20°C**, varying with temperature/conditions; sea level alone does not fix it.
- Shock waves are generated along the supersonic flight path, not only at the instant an aircraft crosses Mach 1. The observer hears a brief pressure disturbance; nose/tail waves can produce a double boom. Shape, size/weight, altitude, manoeuvres and atmospheric refraction affect the signature; it does not depend solely on speed. [NASA sonic-boom guide](https://www.nasa.gov/wp-content/uploads/2018/07/edu_srch_lower_the_boom_citizen_science_guide-508.pdf).
## January 2026: listening for re-entry
- **Imperial College London/Johns Hopkins research, published 22 January**, used ground vibrations from atmospheric sonic booms to reconstruct the **April 2024 Shenzhou-15 orbital module re-entry** over the western US. Seismometers can constrain trajectory, speed and fragmentation and help narrow debris-search areas. [Research institutions' account](https://www.imperial.ac.uk/news/articles/engineering/earth-science/2026/scientists-devise-way-to-track-space-junk-as-it-falls-to-earth/).
- A recorded ground vibration is not necessarily an earthquake or physical debris impact at that station. The method complements tracking and depends on sensor coverage, atmospheric modelling and identifiable signals; it does not guarantee recovery of every fragment.
