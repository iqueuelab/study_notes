# Satellite broadband, licensing and digital inclusion

## Network and economics
- Starlink uses many low-Earth-orbit satellites, user terminals, gateways and inter-satellite links. Lower orbital height can reduce latency compared with geostationary systems.
- Performance depends on coverage, congestion, obstructions, weather and power; advertised speeds/latency are not guaranteed at every location.
- March 2025 Jio/Airtel distribution announcements were **conditional on regulatory approvals**, not proof that Indian commercial service had launched.
- Terminal/subscription estimates in reports were not final officially announced Indian tariffs. Affordability and reliable power can limit inclusion even where geographic coverage exists.
## Regulation and public interest
- DoT licensing, security requirements, spectrum allocation and relevant IN-SPACe authorisations are separate functions; Home Ministry clearance is not the sole licence.
- Telecommunications Act 2023 provides administrative allocation for listed satellite services. The 2012 natural-resources advisory opinion did not make auction the sole constitutional method for every resource.
- Safeguard lawful interception, privacy, secure gateways, fair competition and emergency resilience; commercial coverage is not automatically a secure military network.
- Orbital debris, collision avoidance and sustainable spectrum/orbit use accompany digital-inclusion benefits.
## Starlink licence: 6 June 2025
- **DoT granted Unified Licence** authorisations for **GMPCS, Commercial VSAT CUG and ISP Category A** to **Starlink Satellite Communications Private Limited on 6 June**. This advances approval beyond the March distribution announcements; separate spectrum, relevant space authorisation, security/testing and commercial rollout stages still matter. [Official parliamentary confirmation](https://sansad.in/getFile/annex/268/AU3216_S4YKT2.pdf?source=pqars).
- Source describes Starlink as **third after OneWeb/Jio Satellite**; **Amazon Kuiper awaiting approval** in this dated snapshot. June 7 practice answer **(b),1,3,4** concerns licensing status at that time, not universal current service availability.
- Proposed **trial spectrum within 15–20 days** was an expectation, not a confirmed allocation/delivery deadline; security obligations include lawful interception.
- Diagram compares **LEO/MEO/GEO**: lower altitude generally reduces propagation delay but needs constellation/handover support; **Starlink uses LEO**. Existing notes retain affordability, gateway/power/coverage and debris qualifications.

## Orbit comparison and dual use
| Orbit | Approximate altitude | Communications trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| **LEO** | Below 2,000 km | Lower propagation delay; many satellites, tracking and handovers |
| **MEO** | Between LEO and GEO | Wider footprints than LEO; constellation still required (e.g. O3b) |
| **GEO** | ~35,786 km above equator | Appears fixed; broad footprint, high propagation delay, poorer polar coverage |
- A GEO satellite can see roughly one-third of Earth geometrically; usable coverage depends on elevation/beam/service constraints. Illustrative source latency **LEO20–40 ms, MEO150 ms, GEO600 ms** is end-to-end/system dependent, not a universal orbital constant.
- Remote mountains/islands/deserts can have high terrestrial cost per user; satellite links support ships/aircraft, telemedicine, farming and disaster recovery. Ground gateways, terminals, power and backhaul remain necessary network elements.
- **Dual use:** civilian and military communications, drone/support networks and remote-border backup; also potential illicit-device use. Secure access, control, interception/privacy rules and resilience matter.
- Source **US$500 terminal +US$50/month** is an illustrative overseas/report estimate, not an announced Indian tariff. Direct-to-device technology, scale and competition may lower costs; predicted price convergence is not assured. Practice: **all three (D)**; the explanation's “statement4” means its third statement.
