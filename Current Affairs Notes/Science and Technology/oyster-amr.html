Antimicrobial resistance: containment and research

Editable notes

January research finding

  • Southern Cross University, oyster haemolymph proteins: laboratory antibacterial activity and improved action of conventional antibiotics against selected bacteria.
  • Antimicrobial proteins/peptides are innate defences against microbes in marine habitats; potential drug leads require isolation, mechanism, toxicity, dosage and clinical validation. Not an approved human treatment or evidence eating oysters cures resistant infection. University's 21 January account.

Biology traps

  • Mollusca → Bivalvia; two irregular calcified shell valves, marine/brackish filter feeders drawing algae/particles across gills.
  • Filtration depends on size, species and conditions; source 1.3 gallons/hour is not a universal fixed rate. Many reproduce by broadcast spawning, with sex change in some species.
  • Oysters have nervous ganglia, despite lacking a vertebrate-like brain/spinal cord. That alone does not establish a definitive capacity/absence of pain.
  • Source eyes all over body confuses oysters with eye-bearing bivalves such as scallops; shell closure/sensory response does not require that anatomy.
  • University describes Sydney rock oyster proteins disrupting/preventing bacterial biofilms, one reason for improved antibiotic action in laboratory work.

AMR: public-health framework, April 2025

  • Antimicrobial resistance: microbes—bacteria, viruses, fungi and parasites—become less responsive to relevant medicines; infection becomes harder to treat and can spread, cause severe illness or death. The microbe becomes resistant, not the patient's body. Antibiotic resistance is the bacterial subset. WHO.
  • Bacterial resistance emerges through mutation and horizontal gene transfer; antimicrobial exposure selects resistant organisms. Drivers: inappropriate prescribing, self-medication and unnecessary broad-spectrum use; livestock/aquaculture misuse; inadequate WASH, infection prevention and diagnostic access; poor-quality medicines and environmental contamination.
  • Resistant infections jeopardise routine treatment and care dependent on effective infection control—surgery, transplants, cancer therapy and neonatal care. High infectious-disease burden, uneven surveillance/diagnostics and hospital infection control magnify India's vulnerability. TB/malaria resistance requires disease-appropriate drugs; antibacterial antibiotics do not treat every pathogen.
  • Burden correction: the study published in 2022, covering 2019 across 204 countries/territories, estimated 4.95 million deaths associated with bacterial AMR, including 1.27 million attributable to resistance. Associated deaths and attributable deaths are different counterfactual measures; this was not a 2021 mortality count. Western sub-Saharan Africa had particularly high mortality rates and South Asia a substantial burden. Original study02724-0).
  • Source's 60,000 neonatal deaths/year in India is an older, inadequately dated estimate, not a verified April 2025 annual count. India has surveillance systems; describe coverage/data gaps rather than their total absence.

Stewardship of newer antibiotics: 25 April clipping

  • Infectious-disease specialists urged DCGI to establish clear regulatory pathways for rational use of newer antibiotics. The clipping reports inappropriate community/broad-spectrum use of ceftazidime–avibactam, intended for targeted resistant gram-negative infections, and resistance even when combined with aztreonam. Treat this as expert concern, not a quantified national failure rate.
  • Cefiderocol and cefepime–zidebactam are described as forthcoming options in this dated report; do not infer universal Indian approval, market availability or efficacy against every resistant isolate.
  • Use culture/susceptibility testing where indicated, appropriate prescribing and review/de-escalation, hospital stewardship and surveillance. Education should accompany enforceable prescribing/dispensing controls; reserve agents should not become indiscriminate first-line substitutes.

Indian response and One Health priorities

  • National Action Plan on AMR launched April 2017: awareness, surveillance, infection prevention, optimised antimicrobial use, R&D/investment and multisectoral coordination. Delhi Declaration supports coordinated action across ministries and stakeholders. NCDC/ICMR networks provide resistance surveillance; India joined WHO GLASS in 2017. NCDC programme.
  • One Health: link human health, animal husbandry, food systems and the environment. Extend monitoring to community/rural settings, animals and effluents; improve sanitation, vaccination, diagnostics and healthcare infection control.
  • Free Diagnostic Services and Kayakalp can strengthen diagnosis and facility hygiene; neither automatically ensures appropriate prescribing. Affordable access to effective medicines must accompany restrictions on misuse.
  • R&D priorities: antibiotics, rapid diagnostics, vaccines and validated alternatives; finance development and sustainable supply while preserving access. Source suggestions for an international funding mechanism or patent-pooling arrangements are proposals, not proof of an established AMR-specific global fund or a patent pool covering all antibiotics.

International comparison and mains structure

  • US 2020–25 plan: prevent emergence/spread; strengthen surveillance; advance rapid diagnostics; accelerate R&D; improve international coordination.
  • UK 2019–24 framework emphasised reducing need/exposure, optimising use and innovation/access. It was succeeded by 2024–29 national action plan, published 8 May 2024; do not present the earlier plan as the current April 2025 framework. UK plan.
  • Use international examples to compare measured stewardship, surveillance and access, not assert success merely because a country has a plan.
  • 250-word practice answer: definition/burden → India-specific drivers → government response → remaining gaps → funded One Health implementation, diagnostic-led stewardship, infection prevention, R&D and accountable monitoring. The supplied daily question is practice, not a dated UPSC PYQ.

May 2025: antibiotic innovation and access

  • The issue revisits Nafithromycin, Wockhardt/BIRAC-supported indigenous oral antibacterial for community-acquired bacterial pneumonia (CABP), marketed as Miqnaf. Public scientific launch 20 November 2024 differs from regulatory clearance and later commercial rollout; company reports commercial launch 27 May 2025, after this issue. PIB research announcement, company rollout statement.
  • Proposed/labelled course once daily for three days and reported 97% clinical cure concern studied CABP patients, not every resistant infection or guaranteed individual cure. PIB describes 14 years of development (source rounds to 15). “First of its class in three decades” must not be expanded into “no new antibiotic class anywhere for 30 years”.
  • 2050 projections: 1.91 million annual deaths attributable to bacterial AMR, 8.22 million associated; model forecasts with uncertainty, not present mortality. 2019 global 1.27 million is attributable, not the full associated burden. Source India 297,000 is an attributable estimate rather than every infection-associated death. Original forecast study01867-1).
  • Source's 70% antibiotic use in animals/agriculture has no clear geography/measure; it is not a verified universal Indian consumption share. India's 2019 colistin prohibition covers its use in food-producing animals, poultry, aquaculture and animal feed supplements, not simply poultry growth promotion.
  • Delayed/poor-quality susceptibility testing, understaffing, high caseloads, self-medication and indiscriminate new-agent use undermine stewardship. Existing ceftazidime–avibactam resistance reinforces culture-guided use, infection prevention and review/de-escalation, not reflexively broader treatment.
  • New-drug research needs rigorous trials/regulatory evidence, affordable access and sustainable supply alongside restrictions on misuse. One Health implementation links human/veterinary/agricultural/environmental surveillance, public education and accountable dispensing; AMR is microbes resisting treatment, not humans becoming resistant.

Prescribing behaviour: knowledge versus practice

  • Source reports research using 2,282 private providers in Karnataka/Bihar, knowledge vignettes and anonymous standardised-patient visits for childhood diarrhoea. Roughly half lacked correct knowledge; among those knowing antibiotics were unnecessary, 62% still prescribed. Perceived patient demand, rather than drug supply or immediate dispensing profit, explained much of the gap.
  • Study counterfactuals: eliminating the know-do gap could reduce prescribing by about 30 percentage points, versus 6 points from perfect knowledge; these are not measured national reductions or relative-percent changes. Explicit requests for ORS reduced antibiotic use. Original study and author abstract.
  • Correct beliefs about patient preferences and reinforce communication/trust alongside clinical training, stewardship, appropriate dispensing, diagnostics and One Health surveillance. ORS/zinc treat dehydration/diarrhoea support needs; antibiotics are inappropriate for most uncomplicated childhood diarrhoea, but selected infections need clinical assessment.
  • The ~5 million AMR-associated deaths phrase repeats an older global estimate already qualified in these notes; it is not a September 2025 annual death measurement. Prescription totals likewise need a year and coverage definition. Modern One Health collaboration includes UNEP alongside WHO, FAO and WOAH.
  • 2019 MDR PYQ: (b), 2 and 3 only—incorrect antibiotic doses and antibiotic use in livestock contribute. Microbial resistance is not a patient's genetic predisposition; “only misuse matters” is too absolute because sanitation, infection control and transmission also matter.
  • 2014 Mains revision: overuse/non-prescription access → selection and transmission → Schedule H/H1 enforcement, NCDC/ICMR surveillance, stewardship/diagnostics, livestock/environment controls and affordable access. The source's 250 words/15 marks label is flagged for original-paper verification rather than assumed to be the 2014 paper's specification.

November 2025: NAP-AMR 2.0

  • 18 November 2025: Health Ministry launched National Action Plan on AMR 2.0, 2025–29. Awareness/education, laboratory capacity, healthcare infection prevention and coordinated antimicrobial-use action are implementation priorities; the source's description of limited first-plan progress is an editorial assessment, not proof nothing was achieved. Launch.
  • WHO October 2025 report uses 2023 data: about one in six laboratory-confirmed common bacterial infections worldwide resistant to treatment; regional and surveillance-coverage variation matters. The PDF's “one in three infections in India” must not be assumed a population-wide incidence estimate from representative testing. WHO.
  • Extend funded Centre–State One Health implementation across hospitals, communities, veterinary/agricultural/aquaculture systems, pharmaceutical waste and sewage; combine diagnostics and surveillance with stewardship, vaccination/WASH and infection control. Resistance in E. coli/Klebsiella is drug- and isolate-specific; not every isolate resists every carbapenem/colistin treatment.
  • Earlier colistin prohibition and surveillance expansion repeat existing notes. Kerala provides a State action-plan example, but “only Kerala implemented effectively” and a claimed community resistance decline need comparable evaluation before a national ranking. Measure outcomes, responsible access and environmental/animal transmission rather than launch documents alone.
