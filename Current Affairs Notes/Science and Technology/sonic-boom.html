Sonic booms and seismological tracking of space debris

Editable notes

Sound and supersonic motion

  • Mach number = object speed/local sound speed. Supersonic motion (Mach >1) generates shock waves heard as a sonic boom when they reach an observer. Sound speed in dry air is about 343 m/s at 20°C, varying with temperature/conditions; sea level alone does not fix it.
  • Shock waves are generated along the supersonic flight path, not only at the instant an aircraft crosses Mach 1. The observer hears a brief pressure disturbance; nose/tail waves can produce a double boom. Shape, size/weight, altitude, manoeuvres and atmospheric refraction affect the signature; it does not depend solely on speed. NASA sonic-boom guide.

January 2026: listening for re-entry

  • Imperial College London/Johns Hopkins research, published 22 January, used ground vibrations from atmospheric sonic booms to reconstruct the April 2024 Shenzhou-15 orbital module re-entry over the western US. Seismometers can constrain trajectory, speed and fragmentation and help narrow debris-search areas. Research institutions' account.
  • A recorded ground vibration is not necessarily an earthquake or physical debris impact at that station. The method complements tracking and depends on sensor coverage, atmospheric modelling and identifiable signals; it does not guarantee recovery of every fragment.
  • Source CDS 2023 question is excluded from CSE PYQs. Practice key (b), statement 2 only: supersonic condition correct, solely-speed intensity claim false. The explanation accidentally labels both clauses “statement 2”.
