Science and Technology

Sodium reduction and potassium-enriched salt substitutes

Editable notes

WHO guidance and mechanism

  • 27 January 2025 WHO guideline: adults should consume less than 2 g sodium/day ≈ 5 g salt, from all dietary sources; sodium mass ≠ salt mass.
  • If using table salt, conditional recommendation to replace regular salt with lower-sodium substitutes containing potassium, within an overall reduction strategy. Not encouragement to increase salt intake. Guideline.
  • Intended for general adult population; excludes pregnant women, children and people with kidney impairment/other impaired potassium excretion. Not simply “safe for everyone except known kidney disease”.
  • Excess sodium raises blood pressure and cardiovascular risk; substituting potassium may lower pressure. Source's direct sodium → fatty-plaque claim oversimplifies atherosclerosis; reduced salt is not a proven cure for diabetes.

India and implementation

  • Source estimates 10.4 g salt/day in India and cites 1.9 million global deaths associated with excess sodium; dated estimates, not identical to each person's intake/risk.
  • Food added at table/cooking and processed snacks all matter. Hyperkalaemia risk requires appropriate clinical assessment, especially kidney disease and relevant medicines; screening/clear labels and public-health counselling.
  • Affordable products, industry sodium reduction, accurate nutrient claims, education through health workers/SHGs and monitoring of intake/outcomes; price subsidy/display mandates are source proposals, not enacted requirements.
  • Source edible-salt 97% NaCl specification must not be mechanically applied to every salt substitute. 2.2% refers to water-insoluble-matter provision in relevant standards; not a blanket limit on all anti-caking agents. FSSAI additives compendium.
  • Sodium reduction and iodine sufficiency must be considered together; replacing a food ingredient does not automatically preserve iodine delivery.

September 2025: make salt reduction part of HFSS policy

  • The editorial argues that public messaging focused on sugar/oil should also address salt. It reports Indian intake 8–11 g/day, 75% from homemade food, 28.1% adult hypertension and 60% NCD death share; these have different/unspecified reference populations and dates, so are source estimates rather than one new 2025 survey.
  • WHO identifies sodium reduction as a cost-effective intervention, with an estimated at least $12 return per $1 invested. Sodium raises blood pressure and cardiovascular risk; less than 2 g sodium ≈ 5 g salt/day for adults remains the distinction. Rock/pink/black salts also contain sodium and are not automatically healthier; preserve iodine sufficiency. WHO public-health guidance.
  • Suggested package: gradual reformulation and less cooking/table salt; public awareness from early life; labelled sodium/front-of-pack warnings; standards and trained cooks for school meals, Anganwadis and hospitals; responsible food marketing to children; restaurant/family practices; coordinated health, education, food-processing and consumer policy with intake monitoring.
  • Chile/Mexico warning labels, UK reformulation and Japanese education are illustrative approaches, not proof equivalent measures already operate throughout India. Strengthening a successor to the 2017–22 multisectoral NCD plan is a recommendation, not a newly notified programme.
  • Mains practice, 250 words/15 marks: dietary NCD burden → sodium/BP pathway → food environment and household drivers → coordinated prevention/regulation/monitoring. This is an undated daily practice prompt, not a CSE PYQ.
