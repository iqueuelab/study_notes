# Reusable launch vehicles: physics, recovery and economics

## Reuse, physics and economics
- Rocket propulsion conserves momentum by expelling exhaust; it needs no surrounding air to push against. The ideal **Tsiolkovsky equation: Δv = vₑ ln(m₀/mf)** links velocity change, effective exhaust speed and initial/final mass. Gravity/drag losses add mission requirements; staging discards empty structure to improve performance. Source mass fractions and crewed/uncrewed cost multiples are configuration-specific, not physical constants.
- **Recovery → inspection/refurbishment → reflight** are distinct milestones. Falcon 9's first stage uses controlled engine burns, aerodynamic guidance and landing on land/drone ships; its upper stage remains expendable. **Starship aims at full two-stage reuse**; the goal is not proof every mission has achieved it.
- Reuse spreads manufacturing costs across flights and can raise cadence, but recovery hardware/reserved propellant reduce payload. Reliability, material fatigue, thermal cycles, engine/tank damage, inspection, refurbishment cost and flight demand determine economic value. Source **5–20× savings, 520+ recoveries, 30+ reuses and US$1-trillion 2030 space economy** are dated counts/estimates, not guaranteed system-wide benefits.
- Sustainability requires lifecycle assessment: fewer discarded stages can coexist with increased launch frequency, emissions, debris and manufacturing impacts. Private finance, modular design, additive manufacturing and integrated production can support reuse without eliminating public R&D/safety regulation.
## Indian technology paths
- **ISRO Pushpak/RLV** demonstrates winged autonomous runway landing; **LEX-02 (22 March 2024)** used helicopter release from **4.5 km** at Chitradurga. Landing tests are not proof of an operational reusable orbital launcher. Orbital re-entry and vertical take-off/landing stage-recovery technologies are further development paths. [ISRO LEX-02](https://www.isro.gov.in/ISRO_EN/RLV_Landing_Experiment.html).
- Develop guidance, reusable engines, thermal/structural qualification, rapid refurbishment and viable launch demand; fewer stages alone cannot guarantee lower cost. Compare winged recovery with retro-propulsive boosters and distinguish technology demonstrators from commercial service.
