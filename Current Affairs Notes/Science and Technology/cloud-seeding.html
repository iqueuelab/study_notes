Science and Technology

Cloud seeding: mechanisms and evidence limits

Editable notes

Mechanism and applications

  • Weather modification seeks to enhance precipitation from existing suitable clouds, not create rain clouds or moisture from clear sky. Glaciogenic seeding uses ice nuclei in supercooled clouds; hygroscopic seeding encourages liquid-droplet growth/coalescence.
  • Source agents: silver iodide, potassium iodide and dry ice (solid CO₂). Aircraft and ground-based generators are common delivery methods; rockets/shells are used in some programmes, not the universal definition of ground seeding.
  • Applications under suitable conditions: rain/snow enhancement, certain supercooled-fog dispersal and attempts at hail suppression. Potential benefits for reservoirs/agriculture depend on demonstrable additional precipitation and economics.
  • Indian source examples Karnataka, Maharashtra, Tamil Nadu, Andhra Pradesh; Karnataka's Varshadhari is associated with the 2017 drought-era programme, so the issue's sole 2018 date should not be treated as its first launch. International examples include China, US, UAE and Israel; operation does not itself prove successful outcomes everywhere.

Evidence, risks and governance

  • Natural rainfall variability makes attribution difficult; use control areas/randomised trials where feasible, cloud/radar observations, uncertainty estimates and transparent cost-benefit evaluation. Results cannot simply be transferred across different clouds/regions.
  • WMO reports no established ability to eliminate severe weather or control floods/tropical cyclones. The source's claims that over-seeding causes floods or long-term climatic imbalance are not established facts. Published operations using usual seeding-agent quantities have not shown significant health/environmental effects; increased quantities/new agents still require assessment. WMO scientific statement.
  • Constraints: suitable-cloud availability, aircraft/personnel cost, uncertain response and possible downwind impacts needing investigation. Local/inter-state and international cooperation, environmental monitoring, consent/transparency and clear legal responsibilities matter; cloud seeding complements water conservation rather than guarantees drought relief.

2025 PYQ mechanism recall

  • Artificial rainfall agents asked: silver iodide and potassium iodide (a). Seeding needs existing suitable clouds; it cannot reliably manufacture clouds/moisture from clear sky.
  • AgI is an ice-nucleating agent in supercooled clouds, rather than every seeding agent functioning identically as a condensation nucleus. Hygroscopic salts encourage droplet growth; dry ice promotes freezing. Intended pollution washout depends on actual rain and does not remove emission sources.
  • Distinguish weather modification from SAI solar intervention/cirrus radiative thinning; atmospheric layer, objective and evidence differ.

Delhi trials: October 2025, not October 2024

  • Source clipping concerns 28 October 2025 Delhi/IIT Kanpur trials; its expanded notes repeatedly misdate them 2024. Issue reports no substantial rain and limited pollution relief. Failure to produce rain under unsuitable cloud conditions does not prove every seeding programme fails; success elsewhere does not establish Delhi winter effectiveness.
  • CAIPEEX (IITM Pune, Ministry of Earth Sciences) began in 2009, with a later 2017–19 Solapur campaign. IITM reports 46 ± 13% at some locations, averaging 18 ± 2.6% over 100 km² downwind in the rain-shadow region. The issue also cites 867 million litres additional water. These are study-specific estimates with different denominators, not an automatic statewide/winter rainfall guarantee. IITM results.
  • Existing notes retain cloud suitability, hygroscopic versus ice-nucleating agents, attribution and WMO limits. Do not infer that CAIPEEX proved only monsoon clouds anywhere can ever be seeded. Historical claims such as a precise first Delhi failure date need primary documentation; the source's “William Schaefer” should be Vincent Schaefer. Pollution control requires emissions reduction even if rain temporarily washes particles out.
