Darwin’s bark spider: silk toughness and biological trade-offs

Editable notes

Adult female silk and biological trade-offs

  • Darwin's bark spider, Caerostris darwini, is a Madagascan orb-weaver; major ampullate (dragline) silk forms structural frames/anchors and safety lines. Exceptionally tough silk allows large river-spanning webs; anchor spans up to ~25 m do not mean an orb capture surface itself is 25 m wide.
  • A 2025 Integrative Zoology study compared size/sex classes of C. darwini and C. kuntneri under controlled conditions. Large adult females produced exceptionally tough silk with higher initial stiffness; juvenile females and males produced lower-performance silk, supporting body-size-related selection/ontogenetic plasticity. This is evidence from two species, not a universal law for all spiders. Original-study abstract.
  • Tensile strength measures stress at failure; stiffness resistance to deformation; extensibility stretch before failure; toughness energy absorbed per volume before breaking (area under stress–strain curve). They are related but not interchangeable, so “toughness = strength + elasticity” is only an informal shortcut. Source tensile strength ~1.6 GPa does not establish superiority over every steel grade in every property.
  • Source describes adult females' sparse webs/less silk per area versus denser juvenile webs, similar extensibility, high proline content and specialised spinning ducts. These suggest investment in silk performance as body/web demand grows; they do not prove a conscious on/off choice or that proline alone causes toughness. Protein composition, molecular organisation and spinning mechanics interact.
  • Biomimetics can learn from biological optimisation of material performance and energy cost; engineering transfer still requires controllable manufacture and testing.
