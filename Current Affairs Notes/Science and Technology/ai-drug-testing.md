# AI in drug development and regulatory evidence

## Uses and limits
- AI can identify targets, predict protein/drug interactions, screen molecules, assess toxicity and ADME, select trial participants and analyse evidence. **ADME = absorption, distribution, metabolism, excretion**.
- Human organ models, organoids and other new approach methodologies may reduce animal use; they do not automatically replace every animal study or human clinical trial.
- Development cost/time and success-rate figures vary by study, drug class and denominator; source “10 years / billions / 14% success” is contextual, not a universal law.
## FDA January 2025 draft framework
- Applies to AI-generated information supporting **drug/biological regulatory decisions**, not only preclinical testing. **Draft guidance**, not an automatic approval for every model. [FDA draft](https://www.fda.gov/regulatory-information/search-fda-guidance-documents/considerations-use-artificial-intelligence-support-regulatory-decision-making-drug-and-biological).
- Define question/context of use → assess model risk → set credibility requirements → validate/evaluate evidence → document limitations → monitor when use/data changes.
- Fit-for-purpose data, external validation, reproducibility and representative populations matter. Model confidence cannot substitute for demonstrated clinical benefit or safety.
## India and governance
- 2023 drug-rule changes recognised suitable alternative testing approaches; regulatory acceptance depends on the relevant endpoint and evidence, not wholesale abolition of trials.
- Risks: biased training data, rare adverse effects, poor generalisation, privacy breaches, opaque predictions and overreliance. Not every AI model continuously learns after deployment.
- Response: human/regulatory oversight, auditable datasets, secure consent-based use, staged validation and independent review. Opportunity: faster discovery and reduced animal use alongside equitable, reliable evidence.
