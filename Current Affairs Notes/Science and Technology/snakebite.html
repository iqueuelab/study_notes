Snakebite envenoming and neglected tropical diseases

Editable notes

Disease and inequality

  • Envenoming: toxin injury from venom injection; certain spitting snakes can also cause ocular exposure. Not every snakebite injects venom.
  • WHO neglected tropical disease (NTD) portfolio since 2017; rural/agricultural, poor and tribal communities face greater exposure and delayed care. Disability can include tissue loss/amputation, renal injury, visual impairment and psychological harm.
  • India's conventional “Big Four”: Indian cobra, common krait, Russell's viper, saw-scaled viper. Important causes, but not every serious bite or region is covered by this list; “90% of all bites” is not a universal verified share.

Prevention and response

  • WHO 2019 strategy: halve mortality and disability by 2030; community empowerment, safe/effective treatment, stronger health systems and partnerships. WHO strategy.
  • WHO plans include better antivenom availability, quality manufacturing and stockpiles; source 25% manufacturer increase is a strategy target, not achieved capacity.
  • India National Action Plan for Prevention and Control of Snakebite Envenoming (NAP-SE), 12 March 2024, One Health approach and target to halve deaths by 2030. Source's 2015 launch conflicts with the actual launch. Health Ministry.
  • Community education, early transport/referral, accessible quality antivenom, trained personnel and surveillance; antivenom choice/treatment requires clinical assessment.

NTD trap

  • NTDs disproportionately affect disadvantaged communities and receive insufficient research/service attention. Examples: scabies, yaws, trachoma, leishmaniasis, Chagas disease and snakebite envenoming.
  • Not all NTDs are infections: snakebite is toxin-mediated. Lack of clean water/sanitation matters for several NTDs but is not the cause of every NTD.

Russell's viper venom and local climate — 10 April 2025 study

  • IISc Centre for Ecological Sciences researchers published Significant Serpents in PLOS Neglected Tropical Diseases on 10 April 2025, correcting the supplied PDF's April 2024 date.
  • 115 venom samples from 34 locations across India were compared with historical temperature/precipitation using regression models and biochemical assays. Activities examined included proteases, phospholipases and L-amino acid oxidase.
  • Venom functions varied geographically. Protease activity was greater in drier conditions; climate variables did not explain every enzyme equally, including amino acid oxidase. Predictive maps estimate geographic functional variation.
  • These are associations and model predictions: they do not demonstrate that climate change alone caused every venom difference or that the map already improves clinical outcomes.
  • Applications: representative regional venom sampling, antivenom evaluation, surveillance and healthcare planning. Climate shifts could alter venom profiles; prospective validation is needed. Primary research.

The PDF lists Least Concern; this conservation category does not measure medical danger. Activity and movement vary with conditions; avoid interpreting “nocturnal/sedentary” as an absolute rule.

  • Russell's viper belongs to India's conventional Big Four, alongside Indian/spectacled cobra (Naja naja), common krait (Bungarus caeruleus) and saw-scaled viper (Echis carinatus).
  • It is a major cause of severe envenoming, including clotting disturbance and renal injury. The source's “over 40% of all Indian snakebite deaths” lacks a clearly specified national dataset and should not be memorised as a universal share.
  • Agricultural exposure, accidental encounters and delayed access to care contribute to burden. Regional venom research complements the existing One Health, NAP-SE and antivenom-access discussion; it does not replace clinical assessment. IISc on geographic venom variation.
