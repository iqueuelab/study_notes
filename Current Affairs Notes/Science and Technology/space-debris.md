# Space debris: responsibility and liability

## Treaty distinction
- Non-functional human-made orbital/re-entering objects create collision/re-entry risks. Identifying origin and responsible launching States can be difficult.
- **Outer Space Treaty 1967 Article VI:** State responsibility for national activities, including private operators; authorisation/continuing supervision.
- **Liability Convention 1972:** launching State absolute liability for damage on Earth's surface/to aircraft in flight; fault-based liability for damage to another space object elsewhere. Components are included in “space object”; debris is not automatically outside the Convention. [UNOOSA](https://www.unoosa.org/oosa/en/ourwork/spacelaw/treaties/liability-convention.html).
- Launching State includes launch/procurement and territory/facility connections; several States may be involved. Absolute liability still requires attributable damage and applicable claims procedures.
## Examples and reform
- Cosmos 954 fell in Canada 1978; negotiated settlement illustrates attribution/compensation. Kenya December 2024 metal object was initially suspected debris, not conclusively assigned to an operator by appearance alone.
- Source Long March July 2024 date, 100, 000 satellite forecast and 30%compliance are not a verified uniform inventory; distinguish planned constellations from satellites already launched.
- Better tracking, registration, disposal/re-entry plans, passivation and collision avoidance; voluntary guidelines differ from domestic licence rules.
- Tribunal/penalty proposals are reforms, not existing universal powers. Reusable launchers/on-orbit servicing can help but do not inherently create zero debris.
## 10 May 2025: Kosmos 482 re-entry
- Soviet **Venera-programme** Venus probe launched **1972**, failed to escape Earth orbit owing to a launcher failure; the surviving descent capsule remained about **53 years**. Source **~495 kg, ~1 m**, robust titanium design intended for Venus's high-temperature/high-pressure environment.
- **Uncontrolled re-entry10 May 2025**; source “11 May 2024” is erroneous. Russia reported Indian-Ocean descent, but **precise time/location and surviving wreckage were not independently established**. The source's Bay-of-Bengal splashdown is too definite. ESA's 10 May update inferred re-entry after a radar non-detection and reported no direct visual/ground-impact observations. [ESA tracking updates](https://blogs.esa.int/rocketscience/2025/05/07/reentry-prediction-soviet-era-venera-venus-lander-cosmos-482-descent-craft/comment-page-1/).
- Capsule toughness made survival plausible, not proven. **~242 km/h** intact-impact estimate attributed to Marco Langbroek is conditional, not measured impact velocity. After decades, parachute/battery usefulness cannot be assumed; this was not a controlled functioning landing system.
- Atmospheric drag depends on solar activity/density, orbit and object's orientation; prediction windows narrow with radar/other tracking but retain uncertainty. Low individual risk does not erase accountability or the need for responsible disposal and transparent alerts.
- **Outer Space Treaty Article VIII** retains ownership of objects/components despite return to Earth; **Rescue Agreement Article 5** governs notification/recovery/return on request with relevant conditions and expenses. **Liability Convention** addresses damage, not an automatic transfer of ownership or blanket immediate duty for any finder to ship debris. [UNOOSA Rescue Agreement](https://www.unoosa.org/oosa/en/ourwork/spacelaw/treaties/rescueagreement.html).
- Tracking cooperation, registration, passivation, planned disposal and end-of-life licensing complement liability rules; legacy debris shows that mission failure can leave enduring safety responsibilities.
