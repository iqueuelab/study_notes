# Light-controlled bioresorbable pacemaker

## April 2025 research
- Northwestern researchers reported a **1.8 × 3.5 × 1 mm** temporary pacemaker, smaller than a rice grain, in Nature. Its biocompatible components dissolve after use, avoiding surgical retrieval.
- An internal galvanic battery uses two metal electrodes and body fluids as electrolyte; finite chemical energy supplies pacing. A chest wearable detects an abnormally low heart rate and sends **infrared light pulses** through tissue to activate the implant.
- Heart-rate sensing → wearable light pulse → implant electrical stimulation → controlled heartbeat. The implant needs external control, although it has no connecting pacing wire and incorporates its own battery.
- The small implant can fit a syringe tip, supporting less invasive delivery; injection still penetrates tissue. This is not a phone-controlled, permanently self-powered replacement for all implanted pacemakers.
## Significance and limits
- Intended for **short-term pacing**, especially after cardiac surgery and potentially for newborns with congenital heart defects. Several implants responsive to different light wavelengths could enable coordinated pacing at multiple sites.
- Validation involved animal models and donated human hearts; this was **preclinical research**, not established human clinical efficacy or routine regulatory approval.
- Potential gains: fewer retrieval-related infections/tissue injuries and lower surgical burden. Human trials, safe stimulation/dissolution timing, controller reliability, manufacturing and clinician training remain necessary; cost savings are prospective.
