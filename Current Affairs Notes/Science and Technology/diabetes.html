Diabetes: types, complications and public health response

Editable notes

Core distinction

  • Diabetes mellitus: chronic hyperglycaemia due to inadequate insulin production, impaired insulin action, or both. Insulin is produced by pancreatic beta cells and helps regulate glucose uptake/storage.
TypeMain featureRevision trap
Type 1Usually autoimmune beta-cell destruction; insulin deficiencyCan begin in adults as well as children; education does not prevent autoimmune onset
Type 2Insulin resistance with inadequate compensatory insulin secretionGenetics and social/environmental conditions interact with obesity/inactivity; children can develop it
GestationalHyperglycaemia arising during pregnancyRequires pregnancy-specific screening and follow-up; mother and child have higher future type 2 risk
  • Type 1 requires insulin; type 2 may require lifestyle support, medicines and sometimes insulin. Type 2 is the large majority of cases; hospital-specific “2% type 1” is not a national population prevalence estimate. WHO.

Complications and early recognition

  • Persistent hyperglycaemia → vascular/nerve damage → retinopathy, kidney disease, neuropathy, foot ulcers/amputation, heart disease and stroke. Thirst, frequent urination, fatigue and unexplained weight loss may occur; type 2 can remain unrecognised for years.
  • Family history and HLA associations such as DR3-DQ2 / DR4-DQ8 indicate susceptibility, not inevitable disease. The source’s fixed “8% family risk” and undated child counts should not be treated as universal estimates.

Diagnosis versus monitoring

  • Laboratory tests include fasting plasma glucose, HbA1c and oral glucose tolerance testing. HbA1c reflects approximately three months’ average glycaemia; diagnostic selection differs for pregnancy/type 1 and some blood disorders.
  • Typical non-pregnant adult diabetes thresholds: HbA1c ≥6.5%; fasting plasma glucose ≥126 mg/dL; two-hour OGTT ≥200 mg/dL. Confirmation is usually required unless there is unequivocal symptomatic hyperglycaemia; pregnancy uses different criteria. NIDDK.
  • Glucose meters/CGMs support monitoring; closed-loop “artificial pancreas” systems link sensors and insulin delivery, rather than replacing the anatomical pancreas. Clinical interpretation and follow-up remain necessary.

Public health response

  • NPCDCS launched in 2010, now broadened as NP-NCD: health promotion, screening, primary-care management, district/CHC capacity, referral and follow-up. NHM.
  • WHO Global Diabetes Compact launched in 2021, insulin discovery’s centenary: affordable medicines/technologies, primary-care integration, prevention and equitable care. World Diabetes Day: 14 November, Banting’s birthday; “Access to Diabetes Education” was the 2022 theme, not a permanent theme. WHO Compact.
  • Strengthen longitudinal NCD care at Ayushman Arogya Mandirs, affordable insulin/medicines and testing, nutrition/activity support, tobacco control and maternal screening; telemedicine can complement local services. Health education can reduce type 2 risk and complications, not prevent all childhood/type 1 diabetes.
  • Epidemiological numbers depend on year, age coverage and method. The source’s “77 million, IDF 2021” attribution is unreliable; avoid presenting it as India’s current total or an official “diabetes capital” ranking.
