# Desalination: water security and infrastructure vulnerability

## March 2026: West Asian water-security risk
- Conflict raises risks to desalination plants, power supplies and water networks. The source reports a **Qeshm Island** incident affecting villages and damage claims concerning Bahrain; these are attributed reports, not a verified account of every attack's perpetrator.
- Aridity, limited renewable freshwater, urban growth and industrial demand make **Kuwait, Qatar, Bahrain, UAE, Oman and Saudi Arabia** dependent to varying degrees on desalination. **Israel is not on the Persian Gulf**; its principal seawater plants are on the Mediterranean. Municipal drinking-water shares differ from all freshwater use, which includes agriculture; the source's “more than half” cannot be applied uniformly to all listed countries.
## Process and constraints
- **Desalination removes dissolved salts** from seawater/brackish water. **Reverse osmosis:** apply pressure exceeding osmotic pressure → water crosses a selective membrane → lower-salinity product water and concentrated reject brine. Pretreatment, maintenance and post-treatment are necessary; not every dissolved contaminant is perfectly removed.
- Thermal distillation is another major method; RO's prevalence does not mean every Gulf plant uses it. Energy, intake ecology and brine disposal affect cost/environmental impact. [UN water–energy desalination paper](https://www.un.org/sites/un2.un.org/files/2020/08/global_water_and_energy_solutions_knowledge_platform_-_scoping_paper_with_cover8july2020.pdf).
- Damage to concentrated coastal facilities can interrupt water for large populations and cause humanitarian disruption. Backup stocks vary by location; “days/weeks” is not one region-wide guarantee. Redundant power/distribution, storage, repair capacity, demand management and protected civilian infrastructure complement production; tanker/bottled-water delivery cannot quickly replace full metropolitan demand.
