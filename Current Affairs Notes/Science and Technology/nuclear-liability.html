Nuclear liability reform: compensation, investment and safety

Editable notes

February 2025 proposal

  • Budget proposed amendments to Atomic Energy Act, 1962, and Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act, 2010, to support nuclear expansion and private/foreign participation; announcement ≠ enacted amendment.
  • 100 GW by 2047 target; ₹20,000 crore SMR R&D mission, at least five indigenous SMRs by 2033 sought. Targets are not current installed capacity. DAE Budget account.

Liability distinction

  • Operator bears statutory no-fault liability toward victims; section 17 gives a right of recourse after compensation in specified circumstances, including contractual rights and supplier-related defects. Supplier recourse is not identical to automatically assigning every supplier direct primary victim liability.
  • Section 46 preserves other applicable law; interpretation and insurability raised concerns. CSC supplements the compensation framework; India's ratification 2016 should not be confused with merely signing it. DAE explanation.
  • Source says Fukushima 2011 caused clauses in the 2010 Act: chronologically impossible. Bhopal 1984 informed accountability concerns; Fukushima later influenced safety debate.

Projects and policy trade-off

  • Jaitapur, Maharashtra—EDF; Kovvada, Andhra Pradesh—Westinghouse. Liability is one obstacle alongside cost, financing, technology, approvals and local concerns; amendment alone does not guarantee completion.
  • SMRs may offer modular construction/flexible deployment; safety, waste, fuel security and commercial costs still require assessment.
  • Balance reliable low-carbon power and investment with enforceable compensation, supplier quality, independent safety oversight, emergency readiness and parliamentary/public scrutiny.

June 2025 debate: legal reform and investment

  • Discussion of Atomic Energy Act 1962 /Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act 2010 amendments remained a reform proposal, not permission already granted to every private/foreign operator. Correct the source's plural “Damages” to the Act's title Damage; 100 GW by 2047 remains a target and the source's ~8 GW a rounded baseline.
  • Ashley Tellis's position: legal predictability/liability and commercial incentives affect foreign supply and technology transfer. D. Raghunandan's counterview: financing, returns, construction costs and investor appetite can constrain expansion even after legal changes. These are competing assessments, not a demonstrated single cause of all stalled projects.
  • Source refers to Russian contractual arrangements before the 2010 Act and later NPCIL specification/waiver disputes. Do not treat the interview's “violates parliamentary intent” as a final court ruling or assume every historical arrangement was governed identically by the later Act.
  • Existing notes distinguish operator's primary no-fault victim liability from section 17 recourse against suppliers after compensation. Contract terms cannot automatically be assumed to override applicable statute; the precise agreement, law and judicial interpretation matter. Official Act.
  • CSC: India ratified in 2016, not merely a current signatory; compensation framework combines national arrangements and supplementary funding. Describe statutory/financial arrangements accurately instead of a universal “pre-accident cash fund”; permitted recourse grounds differ from direct primary supplier liability.
  • SMRs promise modular/scalable deployment, but safety, waste, fuel, regulation and cost require design-specific evidence; no universal guarantee they are safer/cheaper. Technology transfer requires commercial incentive, legal permission, IP access and domestic absorptive capacity.
  • Westinghouse bankruptcy 2017 is historical, not proof it remained insolvent in June 2025. Source's sweeping claim US/France/UK were not expanding nuclear power obscures differing projects/policies; evaluate actual commitments rather than assume no foreign investment possible.
  • Capacity expansion requires financing/offtake certainty, domestic components and skilled workers, regulatory capability, realistic delivery, independent safety and effective victim compensation; reform can facilitate investment but cannot replace these requirements.

June 17 liability clarification

  • Under the 2010 Act as applicable in June 2025, ₹1,500 crore is the operator limit for reactors of thermal power 10 MW or above; smaller research reactors and fuel-cycle/transport categories have different limits. The overall statutory amount is 300 million SDR or a higher notified amount, not a fixed ₹2,300 crore government ceiling. Section 7 addresses Central Government liability beyond the operator limit.
  • Section 2(g) defines nuclear damage, including death/injury, property and specified economic/environmental losses. Compensation still requires assessment and claims procedures; no-fault liability does not make payment automatic without a claim.
  • Section 17 recourse follows payment: express written contract; supplier/employee act including defective equipment/material or substandard services; or an individual's intentional act/omission. This should not be paraphrased as an unconditional duty to sue every supplier.
  • DAE's interpretation is that section 46 does not create additional civil nuclear-damage compensation claims against suppliers; commercial/legal uncertainty in the debate is distinct from an established right to unlimited supplier tort recovery. DAE FAQ, 2010 Act.

July expansion debate: implementation

  • Editorial adds tariff/offtake disputes, including NPCIL–Gujarat purchaser disagreement, to legal and financing obstacles. Proposed long-term power-purchase agreements, viability-gap support, predictable tariffs and stronger independent safety regulation seek bankable projects.
  • Its possible 49% FDI, green-finance treatment and investor names are suggestions/speculation, not enacted investment permission or committed projects. Nuclear is low-carbon/non-fossil but not thereby renewable. Capacity/SMR targets and supplier-recourse distinctions remain as recorded above.

October 2025: proposed Nuclear Bill deliberations

  • The issue reports continuing deliberations on reform of Atomic Energy Act 1962 and Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act 2010 to expand private/foreign participation. Waste/reprocessing responsibility, access to sensitive R&D, investment terms and victim compensation remained contested; this is source-era proposal status, not enacted permission.
  • Existing notes already cover the 100-GW-by-2047 ambition, SMR mission, government-controlled operation, operator liability/supplier recourse and 220-MWe BSR distinction. Correct the source's unlimited operator liability: section 6 specifies category-dependent caps, including ₹1,500 crore for larger power reactors, and the overall statutory amount/central responsibility are separate. 2010 Act.
  • Its CSC table is misleading: CSC provides supplementary international compensation, India ratified in 2016, and supplier recourse is not equivalent to direct primary supplier liability. BSR/SMR size alone guarantees neither lower cost nor safer cooling; nuclear is non-fossil/low-carbon, not renewable. No inference of foreign ownership approval or fully settled regulator independence follows from PPP discussion.

December 2025: SHANTI Bill proposal

  • Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India (SHANTI) Bill, 2025 proposes a unified framework replacing the Atomic Energy Act 1962 and Civil Liability for Nuclear Damage Act 2010, licensing eligible private participation and giving AERB statutory status. On December 16 this is a Bill, not a completed repeal/operative licence for every private firm.
  • Source's “only NPCIL could operate” overlooks government-sector BHAVINI; distinguish a restricted government framework from a literal NPCIL-only rule. Its blanket permission for foreign firms to build/own/operate needs qualification: licensing eligibility and investment rules matter; overseas incorporation does not automatically qualify an entity.
  • Proposed liability reform distinguishes operator compensation liability, supplier recourse and regulatory penalties. The sheet's “₹1 crore maximum penalty” must not be used as a universal ceiling on nuclear-accident compensation. Proposed category-based operator limits are separate from penal provisions and broader compensation arrangements.
  • Policy aim: reliable low-carbon power, domestic manufacturing, investment and technology; assess independent oversight, safety/emergency readiness, waste, insurance, adequate victim compensation and public trust. Nuclear capacity is non-fossil, not renewable.
  • Existing targets remain 100 GW by 2047, ₹20,000 crore SMR mission, not delivered capacity; the sheet's 8.8 GW is a December-era rounded baseline. Its 220-MW PHWR/Bharat Small Reactor reference differs from generic SMR research. DAE explanatory overview.

December 2025: SHANTI Bill clears Lok Sabha

  • Image-only clipping reports Lok Sabha passage on 17 December of Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India (SHANTI) Bill, 2025. It proposes replacement of Atomic Energy Act 1962/CLND Act 2010, licensing private participation and statutory standing for the nuclear regulator; this is a parliamentary stage, not proof operative repeal had occurred by the issue date.
  • Debate concerns operator compensation caps, government backstop, supplier recourse for defective equipment, licensing and regulator independence. Victims' primary claims against operators and subsequent recourse against suppliers are distinct. Government argued safeguards remain; opposition challenged accountability and the speed of consideration. DAE account of debate.
  • The 100 GW by 2047 goal already recorded requires capital, safety/regulatory capacity, waste management and viable tariffs; legal permission alone does not achieve capacity or establish every foreign company may own reactors.

January 2026: SHANTI legislation and accountability debate

  • Government rationale: investment and dependable low-carbon electricity for energy security and the 100-GW-by-2047 ambition. Opposition questions liability adequacy, regulator independence, secrecy, worker safeguards, public participation and possible socialisation of accident costs. These are policy arguments, not judicial findings. Nuclear is non-fossil but not renewable; neither universal low cost nor complete technological self-sufficiency follows.
  • Correct the source against the enacted text: SHANTI means Sustainable Harnessing and Advancement of Nuclear Energy for Transforming India; assent 20 December 2025, Gazette 21 December. Commencement requires notification; assent alone does not establish every provision/repeal operational by 6 January.
  • Eligible Indian companies may seek licences; no blanket 49% private/51% government rule is prescribed. Foreign-incorporated companies are excluded from the defined company category. AERB receives statutory footing, not automatic institutional independence.
  • Section 16 retains written-contract and intentional-act recourse; removes the old specific defective-supply ground, not all supplier recourse. Second Schedule uses thermal MW, not electrical output: caps ₹3,000/1,500/750/300/100 crore across >3,600 / >1,500–3,600 / >750–1,500 / >150–750 / ≤150 MW categories. ₹1 crore regulatory penalty is separate from accident compensation.
  • Section 39 restricts specified declared information, not all nuclear information automatically. Section 42 assigns administration of worker-safety law to Central Government, with delegation, not wholesale abolition of worker protection. Gazetted Act.
  • The supplementary claims PFBR already operational, uniform 49/51 ownership, fully removed supplier accountability, blanket labour-law exemption and no parliamentary oversight are not reliable statements of the January position. PHWRs, fast-breeder development and thorium ambitions are different programme stages. Source's 25 reactors/seven sites and Fukushima “700×” comparison need dated definitions and comparable liability/damage measures; do not memorise them as a legal rule or current confirmed tally.
