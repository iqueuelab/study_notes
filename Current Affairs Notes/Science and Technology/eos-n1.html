EOS-N1 and PSLV-C62: launch plans and mission outcome

Editable notes

8 January 2026: PSLV-C62 launch scheduled

  • PSLV-C62 / EOS-N1 was scheduled for 12 January 2026, 10:17 IST, First Launch Pad, SDSC Sriharikota. At this issue date it remained a planned launch; “restores confidence” describes the intended purpose after PSLV-C61/EOS-09's May 2025 failure, not an achieved result.
  • ISRO's dated mission notice: 64th PSLV flight, PSLV-DL with two solid strap-ons; ninth dedicated NSIL commercial mission, building/launching an Earth-observation satellite for a user and carrying 15 co-passengers from domestic/international customers. The planned Kestrel Initial Technology Demonstrator (KID) from a Spanish startup would test a small re-entry prototype; not every co-passenger was an Earth-imaging satellite. ISRO's 8 January mission notice.
  • EO supports mapping, agriculture, disaster response and security. The issue calls EOS-N1 strategic/dual-use but does not establish every classified sensor specification; avoid inferring precise military performance from general EO applications. The reported 105th Sriharikota launch counts a different series from the PSLV-flight number.
  • Prior LVM3 BlueBird Block-2 commercial launch illustrates heavy-lift services; it is a different mission and vehicle. PSLV's four-stage architecture and polar/sun-synchronous capabilities do not make it the Gaganyaan human-rated launcher.

12 January 2026: PSLV-C62 anomaly

  • PSLV-C62/EOS-N1 failed to accomplish its intended deployment after an anomaly near the end of PS3, advancing the earlier scheduled-launch entry. ISRO initiated detailed analysis; observed roll disturbance/trajectory deviation is not a concluded root-cause finding about a particular motor component or quality-control failure. ISRO mission record.
  • Source describes loss of EOS-N1 and 15 co-passenger payloads from intended Sun-synchronous deployment and expected PS4/KID re-entry over the South Pacific. An anticipated re-entry is not a tracked recovery; the capsule's intended re-entry experiment differs from an Earth-observation satellite's orbital objective.
  • PSLV's four stages alternate solid → liquid → solid → liquid. This failure followed C61 in May 2025; D1 in 1993 and C39 in 2017 are other source-listed failures. Counting complete versus partial mission outcomes needs consistent definitions; the chronology does not prove C61 and C62 had the same cause.
  • Strategic Earth-observation continuity, launch confidence, insurance and commercial schedules are implications. Source reports private payload insurance and possible NSIL refund/re-flight negotiations; coverage, cross-waivers and compensation depend on actual contracts, not an automatic universal exemption or guaranteed repayment of satellite value. EOS-N1's DRDO attribution is source reporting; official mission nomenclature is retained without inventing classified specifications.
